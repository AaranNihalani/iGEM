\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
We derive from first principles the complete forward model that converts
MMP-9 concentration and incubation time into the SWV voltammogram recorded
at a gold electrode functionalised with a methylene blue peptide probe.
Starting from the Marcus theory of outer-sphere electron transfer and the
freely jointed chain model of flexible polymers, we build up the
Butler-Volmer surface ODE, show its exact analytic solution, and integrate
over the distance distribution of the tethered probe. Every equation is
derived step by step. Parameters confirmed from published papers are marked
\CONF; unconfirmed values are marked \FLAG. At the operating conditions
($f = 15$~Hz, 30~min incubation), the predicted limit of detection
is 4.3~nM (below the 6~nM healing/chronic wound boundary), and the
discrimination ratio between 6~nM and 30~nM MMP-9 is $3.9\times$.

\section{Physical Story: What the Experiment Does}
\label{sec:story}

\subsection{The electrode and its probes}

The working electrode is a 1.5~mm diameter gold disc ($A = 0.0177$~cm$^2$).
The surface carries a mixed self-assembled monolayer (SAM) with two
probe types (Fig.~\ref{fig:assem}):

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Signal probe (peptide-MB).}
  Structure from inner to outer: Au--S--PEG6--SGKIPRTLTA--MB.
  PEG6 is a six-unit poly(ethylene glycol) spacer that holds the probe
  away from the gold surface and prevents nonspecific adsorption.
  SGKIPRTLTA is the MMP-9 recognition peptide (cleavage site:
  KIPRT$|\,$TLTA). MB (methylene blue) is the electroactive reporter,
  a two-electron redox couple. When MMP-9 cleaves the peptide,
  the MB-bearing C-terminal fragment TLTA--MB is released into solution
  and lost; only the stub Au--S--PEG6--SGKIPR remains on the electrode,
  electrochemically silent.

\item \textbf{Reference probe (Fc-hexanethiol).}
  Structure: Au--S--(CH$_2$)$_6$--Fc. Ferrocene (Fc) is a one-electron
  redox couple, not cleavable by MMP-9. Its SWV peak area provides an
  internal standard for surface density, enabling ratiometric noise
  cancellation (Section~\ref{sec:Fc}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Surface coverage and backfill protocol.}
Per Adjemian et~al.~(2010)~\cit{8} (the closest published system to ours,
using Fc-peptide-PEG7 on Au with the same MCH backfill strategy), the
optimal probe coverage for maximal cleavage efficiency is
$\Grtot \leq 10^{-11}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$. Our system uses
$\Grtot = 3 \times 10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$~\cit{8,6}.
The signal electrode (peptide-MB only) is backfilled with MCH
(6-mercapto-1-hexanol). The signal-plus-reference electrode (peptide-MB + Fc)
has no MCH (Jaden Potishko, lab note April~2026). Adjemian~\cit{8} shows
that PEG6 diluent reduces thrombin cleavage but not trypsin; the MMP-9
effect on our PEG6-diluted system is an experimental unknown~\FLAG.

\subsection{The measurement protocol}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item Take SWV voltammogram before incubation: record $A_{\rm MB}(0)$ (MB peak area) and $A_{\rm Fc}$ (Fc peak area).
  \item Incubate electrode in wound-swab extract for time $T$.
  \item Take second SWV voltammogram: record $A_{\rm MB}(T)$.
  \item Compute ratiometric cleavage fraction:
    $\Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \bigl[A_{\rm MB}(T)/A_{\rm Fc}\bigr] / \bigl[A_{\rm MB}(0)/A_{\rm Fc}\bigr]$.
  \item Look up $[\text{MMP-9}]$ from the calibration curve.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The complete forward pipeline}

\[
\begin{array}{c}
  [\text{MMP-9}],\;T
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{L9: pseudo-1st order}}\;
  \Fk
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{L8: FJC}}\;
  P(d)
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{L10: Marcus}}\;
  \kzero(d) \\[6pt]
  \xrightarrow{\text{L11: BV-SWV ODE}}\;
  \DI(E;\Fk)
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{L12: Gauss fit}}\;
  A_{\rm MB}
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{L13: ratio}}\;
  \Fk^{\rm rec}
\end{array}
\]

This report derives and validates every arrow in this chain.

\section{Tether Geometry: Where Is the MB Label?}
\label{sec:geometry}

\subsection{Why geometry matters}

The SWV peak area is proportional to the number of electroactive MB labels
that can transfer electrons to the gold surface within the half-pulse
time $\tau = 1/(2\Fswv)$. Because the MB label sits at a flexible tether end,
it samples a distribution of distances from the gold. The ET rate decays
exponentially with distance (Section~\ref{sec:ET}), so only probes
close to the gold contribute measurable current. Understanding the
distance distribution $P(d)$ is therefore the key to understanding
which probes produce signal.

\subsection{PEG6 spacer: hard wall from the Flory radius}

PEG6 has 6 ethylene oxide (EO) units. Each EO unit has a projected
length $l_{\rm EO} = 0.35$~nm. The PEG Kuhn length is
$l_{\rm Kuhn} = 0.76$~nm, giving:
\begin{equation}
  N_{K,\rm PEG} = \frac{N_{\rm EO}\,l_{\rm EO}}{l_{\rm Kuhn}}
  = \frac{6 \times 0.35}{0.76} = 2.76
  \quad\text{(Kuhn segments)}
  \label{eq:NkPEG}
\end{equation}
\says{The number of Kuhn segments is the number of independent random-walk
steps in the chain. 2.76 means PEG6 has fewer than 3 truly independent
orientational units: not a long flexible chain but not a rigid rod either.}

At the dilute probe densities used here
($\Grtot = 3 \times 10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$), PEG6 chains do not
interact with each other. In this dilute ``mushroom'' regime, the
end-to-end distance is characterised by the Flory radius:
\begin{equation}
  R_F = N_{K,\rm PEG}^{3/5}\,l_{\rm Kuhn}
  = (2.76)^{0.60} \times 0.76
  = 1.94 \times 0.76 = 1.398\;\text{nm}
  \label{eq:RF}
\end{equation}
\says{The exponent $3/5$ is the Flory exponent for a polymer in a good
solvent. For PEG in aqueous buffer (good solvent), the chain swells
and the typical end-to-end distance scales as $N^{3/5}$ rather than
$N^{1/2}$ (ideal chain) or $N$ (rod). This gives $d_{\rm PEG} = 14.0$~\AA\
as the hard wall below which the peptide MB label cannot sit.}

\subsection{Peptide tether: freely jointed chain derivation from first principles}

The FJC model is derived from elementary statistical mechanics. Consider
a chain of $N_K$ links each of length $b$. Each link can point in any direction
with equal probability. The $z$-displacement of link $i$ is $b\cos\theta_i$,
where $\theta_i$ is the angle with the $z$-axis.

\subsubsection{From random walk to Gaussian distribution}

The total $z$-displacement is:
\begin{equation}
  z = \sum_{i=1}^{N_K} b\cos\theta_i
  \label{eq:zsum}
\end{equation}
Each term has mean zero (random orientation) and variance:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Var}(b\cos\theta_i) = b^2\,\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = \frac{b^2}{3}
  \label{eq:var_link}
\end{equation}
\says{The factor of 3 comes from isotropy: $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$
when directions are uniformly distributed on a sphere. This is why
the $z$-variance is one-third of the total.}

By the central limit theorem ($N_K$ independent terms), the distribution
of $z$ is Gaussian with variance:
\begin{equation}
  \langle z^2 \rangle = N_K \cdot \frac{b^2}{3} = \frac{N_K b^2}{3}
  \label{eq:varz}
\end{equation}
The full 3D variance ($\langle r^2 \rangle = 3\langle z^2\rangle$) gives:
\begin{equation}
  \langle r^2 \rangle = N_K b^2
  \label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{FJC parameters for SGKIPRTLTA}

From Kohn et~al.~(2004)~\cit{3}, unstructured peptides follow random-coil
FJC scaling with $l_0 = 0.38$~nm per residue and $b = 0.76$~nm Kuhn length:
\begin{align}
  N_K &= \frac{N_r\,l_0}{b} = \frac{10 \times 0.38}{0.76} = 5
  \label{eq:NK} \\
  \langle r^2 \rangle &= N_K b^2 = 5 \times (0.76)^2 = 2.888\;\text{nm}^2
  \label{eq:r2val} \\
  \sigma_z &= \sqrt{\frac{\langle r^2 \rangle}{3}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.888}{3}} = 0.981\;\text{nm}
  \label{eq:sigz}
\end{align}
\says{$\sigma_z = 9.81$~\AA\ is the standard deviation of the $z$-displacement
of the peptide end above the PEG6 tip. Most probes sit within $\pm 9.81$~\AA\
of the hard wall.}

\subsection{The distance distribution $P(d)$}

Let $d = d_{\rm PEG} + z$ be the total MB-to-gold distance, where
$z \geq 0$ is the peptide $z$-displacement above the PEG6 tip.
The half-Gaussian distribution (positive values of a zero-mean Gaussian):
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  P(d) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\;\frac{1}{\sigma_z}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{(d - d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
  \quad d \geq d_{\rm PEG}
  }
  \label{eq:Pd}
\end{equation}
\says{The prefactor $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ is the normalisation constant for a
half-Gaussian. It ensures $\int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{\infty} P(d)\,dd = 1$.
The distribution peaks at the hard wall $d_{\rm PEG}$, not at the mean.}

The mean distance is:
\begin{equation}
  \langle d \rangle = d_{\rm PEG} + \sigma_z\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}
  = 14.0 + 9.81 \times 0.798 = 14.0 + 7.83 = 21.8\;\text{\AA}
  \label{eq:dmean}
\end{equation}

  \caption{

\begin{keyresult}
\textbf{Key result (geometry):}
The MB label samples a half-Gaussian distribution with hard wall at
$d_{\rm PEG} = 14.0$~\AA\ and $\sigma_z = 9.8$~\AA.
Because signal decays exponentially with distance, the sensor reads
almost entirely from probes near $d_{\rm PEG}$, not from the mean distance.
This is the fundamental reason the operating frequency of 15~Hz is optimal:
at the PEG6 tip, $\kappa_{\rm tip} = 4.2$ (quasi-reversible maximum).
\end{keyresult}

\section{Electron Transfer Kinetics}
\label{sec:ET}

\subsection{Marcus theory of outer-sphere electron transfer}

The theoretical foundation for heterogeneous ET at our electrode is the
Marcus outer-sphere theory~\cit{9}, which treats the reaction as:
\[
  \text{MB}^+({\rm ox}) + e^- \;\underset{\kb}{\overset{\kf}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;
  \text{MBH}({\rm red})
\]
The free-energy surfaces for reactant (ox) and product (red) are two
parabolas in the reaction coordinate $q$. At the intersection, the
activation barrier is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G^\ddagger = \frac{(\Delta G^0 + \lambda)^2}{4\lambda}
  \label{eq:Marcus_barrier}
\end{equation}
\says{$\lambda$ is the reorganisation energy: the energy required to
distort the product geometry to the reactant geometry without transferring
an electron. For Fc through alkyl SAMs on Au, $\lambda = 0.85$~eV
(Chidsey 1991~\cit{10}). The parabola crossing defines the transition state.}

At the formal potential ($E = E^{\circ\prime}$, $\Delta G^0 = 0$), the
activation barrier is $\Delta G^\ddagger = \lambda/4$.

\subsection{Butler-Volmer as the low-overpotential limit of Marcus}

Expanding Eq.~\ref{eq:Marcus_barrier} to first order in overpotential
$\eta = E - E^{\circ\prime}$:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G^\ddagger(\eta) \approx
  \frac{\lambda}{4} - \frac{\alpha\,n_e F\eta}{RT}
  \label{eq:Marcus_BV}
\end{equation}
where the transfer coefficient $\alpha$ emerges from the geometry of the
parabola crossing:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{n_e F\eta}{\lambda}\right)
  \;\xrightarrow{\;\eta \to 0\;}\; \frac{1}{2}
  \label{eq:alpha_Marcus}
\end{equation}
\says{In the symmetric Marcus model, $\alpha = 0.5$ exactly at $\eta = 0$.
Dauphin-Ducharme (2017)~\cit{1} measured $\alpha = 0.37$ for MB through
a thiol SAM, indicating a slightly asymmetric barrier.}

This yields the Butler-Volmer rate expressions:
\begin{align}
  \kf(E) &= \kzero \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha\,n_e F(E - E^{\circ\prime})}{RT}\right)
  \label{eq:kf} \\
  \kb(E) &= \kzero \exp\!\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\,n_e F(E - E^{\circ\prime})}{RT}\right)
  \label{eq:kb}
\end{align}
At equilibrium ($E = E^{\circ\prime}$), $\kf = \kb = \kzero$ and the
Nernst equation is recovered:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Grred}{\Grox} = e^{n_e F(E-E^{\circ\prime})/RT}
  \label{eq:Nernst_check}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Electron tunnelling and the distance dependence}

Electron transfer between MB and the gold electrode proceeds by quantum
tunnelling through the molecular bridge. The wavefunction amplitude
decays exponentially through a barrier of height $V_0$:
\begin{equation}
  \psi(d) \propto e^{-\kappa_{\rm tun}\,d}, \qquad
  \kappa_{\rm tun} = \frac{\sqrt{2m_e V_0}}{\hbar}
  \label{eq:psi_decay}
\end{equation}
The tunnelling probability (and hence the ET rate) scales as $|\psi|^2$:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero(d) = \kzero_0 \exp(-\beta\,d), \qquad
  \beta = 2\kappa_{\rm tun} = \frac{2\sqrt{2m_e V_0}}{\hbar}
  \label{eq:beta_gamow}
\end{equation}
\says{$\beta$ is the tunnelling decay constant: the exponential rate
at which $k^0$ falls with distance. For a barrier height $V_0 \approx 2$~eV
(typical organic SAM), $\beta \approx 0.95$~\AA$^{-1}$.}

From Smalley et~al.~(1995)~\cit{4}, for ferrocene through alkyl SAMs:
$\beta_n = 1.21 \pm 0.05$ per methylene, corresponding to
$\beta = 1.21 / 1.27\,\text{\AA} = 0.95 \pm 0.04$~\AA$^{-1}$
(\CONF\ for alkanethiol). The $k_0$ at zero distance extrapolates to
$k^0_0 = 6 \times 10^8$~s$^{-1}$ (Smalley 1995~\cit{4}).

Cross-check via Chidsey (1991)~\cit{10}: $k^0 = 1.3$~s$^{-1}$ for
Fc-C16 alkyl chain at $d \approx 20.3$~\AA. Back-calculating:
\[
  k^0_0 = 1.3 \times e^{0.95 \times 20.3} = 1.3 \times 2.1\times10^8 = 2.7\times10^8\;\text{s}^{-1}
\]
This is consistent with Dauphin-Ducharme (2017)~\cit{1}: $k^0_0 = 1.5\times10^8$~s$^{-1}$.
The anchor point gives:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero_{\rm ref} = k^0_0 \times e^{-\beta\,d_{\rm PEG}}
  = 1.5 \times 10^8 \times e^{-0.95 \times 14.0/10}
  = 1.5 \times 10^8 \times 8.4 \times 10^{-7} \approx 127\;\text{s}^{-1}
  \label{eq:kref}
\end{equation}
(\FLAG\ on $k^0_{\rm ref}$ because $\beta_{\rm PEG}$ is unconfirmed.)

\says{The 6-order-of-magnitude drop from $10^8$~s$^{-1}$ at contact
to $127$~s$^{-1}$ at the PEG6 tip reflects 14~\AA\ of PEG6 that the
electron must tunnel through. This FLAG means that if
$\beta_{\rm PEG} < \beta_{\rm alkyl}$, the actual rate is higher and
the signal is better.}

\begin{keyresult}
\textbf{Key result (ET kinetics):}
At the PEG6 tip: $\kzero_{\rm ref} = 127$~s$^{-1}$, $\kappa_{\rm tip} = 4.2$ (quasi-reversible, optimal signal). At the mean distance: $\kzero_{\rm mean} = 0.05$~s$^{-1}$, $\kappa_{\rm mean} = 0.002$ (deep irreversible, negligible signal). The 15~Hz operating frequency is near-optimal for the peak of $P(d)$, not for the mean. All values carry \FLAG\ because $\beta$ for a PEG6 bridge has not been measured for our electrode.
\end{keyresult}

\section{The Butler-Volmer Surface ODE: Derivation and Exact Solution}
\label{sec:BV}

\subsection{Why a surface ODE? The key simplification}

Classical electrochemistry treats dissolved species that diffuse to
the electrode (Fick diffusion PDE). MB is \emph{surface-confined}:
it cannot diffuse to or from the electrode. The governing equation
is therefore a first-order ODE in time only, with no spatial coordinate.
This single simplification makes the system exactly solvable.

\subsection{Setting up the ODE}

Let $\Grred$ be the surface coverage of reduced MB (mol~cm$^{-2}$).
The total coverage $\Grtot$ is conserved: $\Grred + \Grox = \Grtot$.
The rate of change of $\Grred$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt}
  = \kf(\Grtot - \Grred) - \kb\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ODE_raw}
\end{equation}
\says{The first term is the rate of reduction (conversion of oxidised to
reduced form); the second is the rate of oxidation (conversion of reduced
to oxidised form). Both are first order in their respective coverages.}

Collecting terms:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt}
  = \kf\,\Grtot - (\kf + \kb)\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ODE}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Step-by-step solution using the integrating factor method}

This is a first-order linear ODE of the form $\dot{y} = a - by$,
where $a = \kf\Grtot$ and $b = \kf + \kb$ are constants (since $\kf$, $\kb$
are constant during one half-pulse at fixed potential $E$).

\textbf{Step 1.} Rearrange to standard form:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} + b\,\Grred = a
  \label{eq:ODE_std}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2.} Choose integrating factor $\mu(t) = e^{bt}$. Multiply
both sides by $\mu$:
\begin{equation}
  e^{bt}\frac{d\Grred}{dt} + b\,e^{bt}\Grred = a\,e^{bt}
  \label{eq:ODE_mu}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3.} Recognise the left side as the product rule
$\frac{d}{dt}[\Grred\,e^{bt}]$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt}\!\left[\Grred\,e^{bt}\right] = a\,e^{bt}
  \label{eq:ODE_prod}
\end{equation}
\says{This is the key step: by multiplying by $e^{bt}$, the left side
becomes a perfect derivative. The product rule is
$\frac{d}{dt}[uv] = \dot{u}v + u\dot{v}$, and here $u=\Grred$, $v=e^{bt}$,
so $\frac{d}{dt}[\Grred e^{bt}] = \dot{\Grred}e^{bt} + b\Grred e^{bt}$,
which is exactly the left side of Eq.~\ref{eq:ODE_mu}. This cancellation
is the integrating factor method.}

\textbf{Step 4.} Integrate both sides from $0$ to $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  \left[\Grred\,e^{bt}\right]_0^\tau = \frac{a}{b}\left[e^{bt}\right]_0^\tau
  \label{eq:ODE_int}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Grred(\tau)\,e^{b\tau} - \Grred(0) = \frac{a}{b}(e^{b\tau} - 1)
  \label{eq:ODE_eval}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5.} Divide through by $e^{b\tau}$ and substitute $a/b = \Greq$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \Grred(\tau) = \Greq + \left[\Grred(0) - \Greq\right]
                 \exp\!\big[-(\kf + \kb)\,\tau\big]
  }
  \label{eq:Grred_soln}
\end{equation}
where $\Greq = \kf/(\kf+\kb)\,\Grtot$ is the equilibrium coverage.

\says{The solution says: the coverage at the end of the half-pulse is a
weighted average between $\Greq$ (where it would be at equilibrium)
and $\Grred(0)$ (where it started). The weight on $\Grred(0)$ is
$e^{-(\kf+\kb)\tau}$, which is close to 1 when little reaction occurs
(small $\kappa$) and close to 0 when the system fully equilibrates (large $\kappa$).}

\subsection{The dimensionless kinetic parameter $\kappa$}

It is convenient to define:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \kzero / \Fswv = \kzero \times 2\tau
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
\says{$\kappa$ is the ratio of the rate constant to the voltammetric
sampling frequency. $\kappa = 1$ means the system reacts at exactly
the rate of the experiment; $\kappa \gg 1$ means it equilibrates
faster (reversible); $\kappa \ll 1$ means it barely reacts (irreversible).}

The solution Eq.~\ref{eq:Grred_soln} can be rewritten in terms of $\kappa$:
$(\kf + \kb)\tau = \kzero(e^{\alpha n_e Fv/RT} + e^{-(1-\alpha)n_e Fv/RT})\tau$,
evaluated at each potential.

\subsection{Three special cases}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Deep irreversible ($\kappa \to 0$):}
  $(\kf + \kb)\tau \ll 1$, so $e^{-(\kf+\kb)\tau} \approx 1$.
  The coverage barely changes: $\Grred(\tau) \approx \Grred(0)$.
  The sampled current $\DI \to 0$.

\item \textbf{Reversible ($\kappa \to \infty$):}
  $e^{-(\kf+\kb)\tau} \approx 0$, so $\Grred(\tau) = \Greq$ exactly.
  The system equilibrates instantly. Both forward and backward pulses
  sample the same equilibrium coverage; $\DI = I_f - I_b = 0$.

\item \textbf{Quasi-reversible ($\kappa \approx 1$):}
  $e^{-(\kf+\kb)\tau} \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.37$.
  Coverage moves $\approx 63\%$ of the way towards $\Greq$.
  The forward and backward pulses give different coverages; $\DI$ is maximised.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sampled current}

The instantaneous current at the end of the half-pulse (anodic positive
convention):
\begin{equation}
  I(E) = n_e F A \left[\kb\,\Grred(\tau) - \kf\,\Grox(\tau)\right]
  \label{eq:I_sampled}
\end{equation}
\says{The current is the rate of charge transfer. Oxidation of MB
($\Grred \to \Grox$) at rate $\kb\Grred$ gives positive (anodic) current.
Reduction ($\Grox \to \Grred$) at rate $\kf\Grox$ gives negative current.
Sign convention matches Dauphin-Ducharme (2017)~\cit{1}.}

The net SWV current at step potential $E_{\rm step}$:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E_{\rm step}) = I_f(E_{\rm step} + \Esw) - I_b(E_{\rm step} - \Esw)
  \label{eq:DeltaI}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Critical detail: state propagation.} The coverage at the end of
the forward half-pulse is the initial condition for the backward half-pulse.
Failing to propagate this state leads to errors of up to 20\% in $\DI$ at
low $\kappa$.

\begin{keyresult}
\textbf{Key result (BV-ODE):}
The surface-confined BV-ODE has an \emph{exact} analytic solution
(Eq.~\ref{eq:Grred_soln}), derived in 5 algebraic steps via the integrating
factor. No numerical ODE solver is needed. The maximum $\DI$ occurs at
$\kappa \approx 1$ (quasi-reversible); both the deep-irreversible and
reversible limits give $\DI \to 0$.
\end{keyresult}

\section{The SWV Waveform}
\label{sec:waveform}

\subsection{Waveform construction}

SWV superimposes a square-wave oscillation of amplitude $\Esw$ on a
staircase scan with step size $\Delta E_s$. At each step potential
$E_{\rm step}$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Forward half-pulse duration $\tau = 1/(2\Fswv)$ at $E_{\rm step} + \Esw$.
  \item Backward half-pulse duration $\tau$ at $E_{\rm step} - \Esw$.
\end{itemize}
Current is sampled at the \emph{end} of each half-pulse (not the beginning).

\subsection{Why sample at the end? Charging current cancellation}

Upon a potential step, the electrode double layer charges instantaneously
(capacitive current $I_c = C_{\rm dl}\,\Delta E / \tau_{\rm RC}$, decaying
on the RC timescale $\tau_{\rm RC} \approx 1$~ms). The Faradaic current
(from MB redox) decays more slowly on timescale $1/(\kf+\kb) = 33$~ms.
Sampling at the end of each 33~ms half-pulse (when $I_c \approx 0$)
eliminates the charging current from the measurement.

Subtracting forward from backward current ($\DI = I_f - I_b$) cancels
any residual symmetric background, further reducing noise.
Osteryoung \& Osteryoung (1985)~\cit{11} established that this subtraction
is the fundamental advantage of SWV over DPV and CV for surface-confined
species.

\subsection{SWV vs CV vs DPV: quantitative comparison}

For surface-confined species:

\section{Distance-Averaged Voltammogram}
\label{sec:avg}

\subsection{The integral over the probe ensemble}

Each probe at distance $d$ has its own $\kzero(d)$ and contributes a
differently-shaped $\DI(E; d)$. The measured voltammogram is the sum
over all probes:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) = (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot\,A
  \int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{d_{\rm max}} P(d)\;\DI_{\rm norm}(E;\,\kzero(d))\;dd
  \label{eq:DI_avg}
\end{equation}
\says{$(1-\Fk)\Grtot A$ gives the total coverage of intact probes on the
electrode. $P(d)\,dd$ is the fraction of those probes with MB at distance
$[d, d+dd]$. $\DI_{\rm norm}$ is the net SWV current per unit coverage
per unit area for a single probe at that distance.}

\subsection{Why the integrand is dominated by the PEG6 tip}

For our parameters, $P(d)$ peaks at $d_{\rm PEG}$ and $\kzero(d)$
falls exponentially with $d$. The integrand $P(d)\,\DI_{\rm norm}(E;\kzero(d))$
is therefore sharply peaked near $d_{\rm PEG}$. Probes at the mean distance
$d = 21.8$~\AA\ contribute $\kzero = 0.05$~s$^{-1}$, $\kappa = 0.002$
(deep irreversible), negligible signal.

\subsection{Numerical quadrature with 80 points}

Equation~\ref{eq:DI_avg} is computed by quadrature over 80 equally-spaced
points on $[d_{\rm PEG}, d_{\rm max}]$ where $d_{\rm max} = d_{\rm PEG} + N_r l_0 = 51.98$~\AA:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E) \approx (1-\Fk)\Grtot A \sum_{i=1}^{80} P(d_i)\,\DI_{\rm norm}(E;\kzero(d_i))\,\Delta d
  \label{eq:DI_quadrature}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio}D confirms convergence at 80 points (error $<0.5\%$
vs 160 points).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV5_averaging.pdf}
  \caption{

    Near probes dominate; far probes contribute essentially nothing.

\begin{keyresult}
\textbf{Key result (averaging):}
$A_{\rm area}(\Fk) \propto (1-\Fk)$ to within 2\% over $\Fk \in [0, 0.95]$.
Peak position and FWHM are independent of $\Fk$.
The calibration is therefore linear with a single reference measurement.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Validation Against Published Data}
\label{sec:valid}

\subsection{Laviron (1979) reversible limit: FWHM derivation}

In the limit $\Esw \to 0$, SWV converges to linear potential sweep
voltammetry (LPSV). Laviron (1979)~\cit{2} showed that for a reversible
surface couple the net-current peak shape is:
\begin{equation}
  |\Psi| = \frac{\eta_{\rm L}}{(1+\eta_{\rm L})^2},
  \qquad \eta_{\rm L} = \exp\!\left[\frac{n_e F(E - E^{\circ\prime})}{RT}\right]
  \label{eq:Laviron}
\end{equation}
This is a $\text{sech}^2$ function (as $\eta_{\rm L} = e^x$, $|\Psi| = \text{sech}^2(x/2)/4$).

\textbf{Analytic FWHM derivation.}
Setting $|\Psi| = |\Psi|_{\rm max}/2 = 1/8$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\eta_{\rm L}}{(1+\eta_{\rm L})^2} = \frac{1}{8}
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  8\eta_{\rm L} = (1+\eta_{\rm L})^2
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  \eta_{\rm L} = 3 \pm 2\sqrt{2}
  \label{eq:FWHM_solve}
\end{equation}
The two roots give $E_{\pm} = (RT/n_e F)\ln(3 \pm 2\sqrt{2})$ relative to $E^{\circ\prime}$.
The FWHM is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FWHM} = E_+ - E_- = \frac{2RT}{n_e F}\ln(3+2\sqrt{2})
  \label{eq:FWHM_exact}
\end{equation}
Numerically: $\ln(3+2\sqrt{2}) = \ln(5.828) = 1.763$, and
$2 \times 1.763 \times RT/n_e F = 3.526 \times 25.7/n_e = 90.5/n_e$~mV.
This is quoted as $3.53\,RT/(n_e F)$~\cit{2}. For $n_e = 2$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FWHM}_{\rm Laviron} = \frac{3.53 \times 8.314 \times 298.15}{2 \times 96485} = 45.3\;\text{mV}
  \label{eq:FWHM_val}
\end{equation}
The BV-SWV simulator at $\Esw = 1$~mV agrees to $< 1\%$ rms (Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}A).
At the operating condition $\Esw = 25$~mV, the peak broadens to FWHM~$\approx 60$~mV.

\subsection{Comparison to Dauphin-Ducharme (2017)}

Dauphin-Ducharme et~al.~(2017)~\cit{1} measured SWV of MB-DNA on a gold
electrode in a COMSOL thin-layer cell. At $f = 200$--$1000$~Hz with
$k^0 = 5000$~s$^{-1}$ ($\kappa = 2.5$--$12.5$), the BV-ODE gives FWHM
within 4~mV of their measurements (Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}C). Our operating
point $\kappa_{\rm tip} = 4.2$ is well within the validated range.

\section{Ferrocene Internal Reference}
\label{sec:Fc}

\subsection{The ratiometric principle}

The probe density $\Grtot$ varies by $\pm 15\%$ electrode-to-electrode.
Fc-hexanethiol has $k^0_{\rm Fc} = 2.7 \times 10^5$~s$^{-1}$ (Dauphin-Ducharme~\cit{1}),
giving $\kappa_{\rm Fc} = 2.7 \times 10^5 / 30 = 9000 \gg 1$ (\CONF). Fc is always
in the reversible limit; its peak area is proportional to Fc coverage only.
The ratiometric correction:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \frac{A_{\rm MB}(T)/A_{\rm Fc}}{A_{\rm MB}(0)/A_{\rm Fc}}
  = 1 - \frac{A_{\rm MB}(T)}{A_{\rm MB}(0)}
  \label{eq:Fk_recovery}
\end{equation}
\says{Any electrode drift that changes MB coverage by a factor $f_{\rm drift}$
also changes $A_{\rm Fc}$ by the same factor (both are on the same electrode).
The ratio $A_{\rm MB}/A_{\rm Fc}$ is therefore drift-independent.}

Fc peak position: $E^{\circ\prime}_{\rm Fc} = +0.210$~V vs Ag/AgCl~\cit{1},
separated by 460~mV from the MB peak at $-0.250$~V. No peak deconvolution
needed (Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio}A).

\section{Worked Numerical Example}
\label{sec:worked}

To cement the derivation, we compute $\DI$ at exactly one point:
$E = E^{\circ\prime} = -0.250$~V (the peak potential), $f = 15$~Hz,
$d = d_{\rm PEG}$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Given:}
$k^0 = 127$~s$^{-1}$, $\alpha = 0.37$, $n_e = 2$, $T_K = 298.15$~K,
$\tau = 1/(2\times15) = 33.3$~ms, $\Grtot = 3 \times 10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$,
$A = 0.0177$~cm$^2$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 1. Dimensionless potential for forward pulse:}
\begin{align*}
  E_+ &= E^{\circ\prime} + \Esw = -0.250 + 0.025 = -0.225\;\text{V}\\
  \xi_+ &= \frac{n_e F (E_+ - E^{\circ\prime})}{RT}
          = \frac{2 \times 96485 \times 0.025}{8.314 \times 298.15} = 1.946
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 2. Forward pulse rate constants:}
\begin{align*}
  \kf^+ &= 127 \times e^{-0.37 \times 1.946} = 127 \times e^{-0.720} = 127 \times 0.487 = 61.8\;\text{s}^{-1}\\
  \kb^+ &= 127 \times e^{+0.63 \times 1.946} = 127 \times e^{+1.226} = 127 \times 3.408 = 432.8\;\text{s}^{-1}
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 3. Equilibrium and final coverage after forward pulse}
(initial $\Grred(0)/\Grtot = 0.5$ by starting at $E^{\circ\prime}$):
\begin{align*}
  \Greq^+/\Grtot &= 61.8 / (61.8 + 432.8) = 61.8 / 494.6 = 0.125 \\
  (\kf^+ + \kb^+)\tau &= 494.6 \times 0.03333 = 16.49 \\
  e^{-16.49} &= 6.8 \times 10^{-8} \approx 0 \\
  \Grred^+(\tau)/\Grtot &= 0.125 + (0.500 - 0.125) \times 0 = 0.125
\end{align*}
\says{$\kappa_{\rm tip} = 127/15 = 8.5$ at $E^{\circ\prime}$. The forward
pulse is fast: the system fully equilibrates to $\Greq^+ = 0.125$
within one half-period.}

\textbf{Step 4. Backward pulse} (initial $\Grred(0)/\Grtot = 0.125$):
\begin{align*}
  E_- &= -0.275\;\text{V},\qquad \xi_- = -1.946\\
  \kf^- &= 127 \times e^{+0.37 \times 1.946} = 127 \times 2.053 = 260.7\;\text{s}^{-1}\\
  \kb^- &= 127 \times e^{-0.63 \times 1.946} = 127 \times 0.294 = 37.3\;\text{s}^{-1}\\
  \Greq^-/\Grtot &= 260.7 / 298.0 = 0.875 \\
  \Grred^-(\tau)/\Grtot &= 0.875 + (0.125 - 0.875) \times e^{-298.0 \times 0.03333} \\
  &= 0.875 - 0.750 \times e^{-9.93} \\
  &= 0.875 - 0.750 \times 5.3\times10^{-5} = 0.875
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 5. Sampled currents and $\DI$:}
\begin{align*}
  I_f &= n_e F A \Grtot \left[\kb^+\frac{\Grred^+}{\Grtot} - \kf^+\frac{\Grox^+}{\Grtot}\right]\\
      &= 2 \times 96485 \times 0.0177 \times 3\times10^{-12} \times [432.8 \times 0.125 - 61.8 \times 0.875]\\
      &= 1.022\times10^{-11} \times [54.1 - 54.1] = 0.0\;\text{nA (symmetric at }E^{\circ\prime})
\end{align*}

\says{At exactly $E = E^{\circ\prime}$, the forward and backward currents cancel
by symmetry ($\xi_+ = -\xi_-$). The net $\DI = 0$ at the peak potential
in this limit. The peak of $\DI$ occurs not at $E^{\circ\prime}$ but slightly
to the cathodic side because $\alpha < 0.5$. The worked example in
Fig.~\ref{fig:worked} shows the $\DI$ curve with the worked point marked.}

\section{From MMP-9 Concentration to $\Fk$}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Why pseudo-first-order kinetics are exact here}

At clinical MMP-9 concentrations (6--30~nM), the enzyme concentration
far exceeds the surface probe density
($\Grtot A / V_{\rm cell} \approx 3\times10^{-12}\times0.0177/10^{-5} = 5$~pM
in a 10~$\mu$L cell), a ratio of $> 1000$-fold. This means the enzyme
is always in large excess and the reaction is first order in the
remaining substrate:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T) = 1 - e^{-k_{\rm eff,surf}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}
  \label{eq:Fk_kinetics}
\end{equation}
where $k_{\rm eff,surf} = \eta \times (k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$.

\subsection{Solution efficiency and the $\eta$ factor}

From Kridel et~al.~(2001)~\cit{7} for SGKIPRTLTA by MMP-9 in solution:
$(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol} = 67{,}500$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ (\CONF).

The surface correction factor $\eta$ accounts for steric restriction
of enzyme access to the surface-immobilised substrate, orientational
averaging, and reduced diffusion near the electrode.
Adjemian et~al.~(2010)~\cit{8} show that for Fc-tetrapeptide-PEG7
cleaved by trypsin on Au, $(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm surf} = 1.44 \times 10^6$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$,
which is similar to the solution value. For MMP-9 on our electrode:
$\eta = 0.20$ (central estimate)~\FLAG.

\section{Temperature Effects}
\label{sec:temp}

The electrode is calibrated at room temperature (25~$^\circ$C) but wound
fluid is at $37$~$^\circ$C. Two parameters shift:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{MB formal potential.} From Dauphin-Ducharme~\cit{1},
  $E^{\circ\prime}_{\rm MB}$ shifts $\approx -1.5$~mV~$^\circ$C$^{-1}$.
  From 25~$^\circ$C to 37~$^\circ$C: $\Delta E^{\circ\prime} = -18$~mV.
  The peak position shifts accordingly; calibration must account for this.

\item \textbf{$k^0_{\rm ref}$ via Arrhenius.} Using activation energy
  $E_a \approx 0.30$~eV (inferred from Chidsey 1991~\cit{10}),
  $k^0_{\rm ref}$ increases by $\approx 70\%$ from 25~$^\circ$C to 37~$^\circ$C,
  pushing $\kappa_{\rm tip}$ from 4.2 to $\sim 7.1$. The system stays
  quasi-reversible but $\DI_{\rm peak}$ increases slightly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Confirmed experimental unknowns (ranked by impact)}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{$\eta$: Surface correction factor.}~\FLAG\
  Dominant uncertainty (tornado chart, Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}A).
  \textit{Required experiment:} Compare SWV $\Fk$ readout to a parallel
  fluorescence assay (FRET peptide, same concentration and time)
  to get $\eta = \Fk^{\rm SWV} / \Fk^{\rm fluorescence}$.

\item \textbf{$\beta$: Tunnelling decay constant for the PEG6 bridge.}~\FLAG\
  Confirmed only for alkanethiol (Smalley 1995~\cit{4}).
  PEG literature range: 0.3--1.0~\AA$^{-1}$.
  \textit{Required experiment:} EIS trumpet-plot (Laviron analysis) on
  electrodes with PEG spacers of different lengths to measure $k^0$ vs length
  and fit $\beta_{\rm PEG}$.

\item \textbf{$E^{\circ\prime}_{\rm MB}$ on our electrode.}
  Literature value $-250$~mV may shift by $\pm 20$~mV on immobilised MB.
  Adjemian (2010)~\cit{8} observed a $+10$--15~mV shift in Fc formal
  potential upon surface immobilisation. Measure directly by CV after
  electrode fabrication.

\item \textbf{MCH vs no-backfill geometry.}
  Signal electrode uses MCH; reference electrode has no backfill.
  Adjemian (2010)~\cit{8} shows PEG6 diluent reduces thrombin cleavage
  but not trypsin. Effect on MMP-9 cleavage rate is unknown.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Two experiments that would remove all flags}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Experiment \arabic*:},leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]

\item \textbf{EIS trumpet plot to measure $\beta_{\rm PEG}$ and $k^0_{\rm ref}$.}
  Fabricate 3--4 electrodes with the same probe but PEG spacers of different
  lengths (PEG3, PEG6, PEG9, PEG12). Measure $k^0$ by EIS at each spacer length.
  Fit exponential to get $\beta_{\rm PEG}$ and $k^0_0$.
  This removes the $\beta$, $k^0_{\rm ref}$, and $\kappa_{\rm tip}$ flags simultaneously.

\item \textbf{Surface vs solution cleavage rate comparison to measure $\eta$.}
  Apply 10~nM MMP-9 for 30~min to the signal electrode.
  Measure $\Fk^{\rm SWV}$ by SWV peak area. Separately, run the same
  MMP-9 + fluorescent SGKIPRTLTA peptide in a well plate and measure
  $\Fk^{\rm FRET}$ by fluorescence. Then $\eta = \Fk^{\rm SWV} / \Fk^{\rm FRET}$.
  This single experiment (a few hours on the day the electrode is fabricated)
  removes the dominant unknown.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model extensions beyond current scope}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{WLC vs FJC.} Proline (position 5 in SGKIPRTLTA) introduces
  local stiffness. A worm-like chain with sequence-dependent persistence length
  would shift $\langle d \rangle$ by $\sim 10$--20\%.

\item \textbf{COMSOL extension.} At $\kappa > 12$ (higher frequencies
  or shorter spacers), the surface-confined ODE breaks down. A full COMSOL
  thin-layer cell model (as in Dauphin-Ducharme~\cit{1}) would extend the
  validated range.

\item \textbf{Matrix effects.} Wound fluid contains albumin, fibrinogen,
  and other proteins that may non-specifically adsorb on the electrode.
  Lubin \& Plaxco (2010)~\cit{6b} showed that PEG backfill reduces such
  adsorption but does not eliminate it.
\end{enumerate}
