\noindent\textbf{Scope.}
This document presents the forward model describing how MMP-9 activity at
the electrode surface is transduced into a measured voltammogram. It is
organised into six sections, each corresponding to one physical step in
the signal chain from probe to peak. Each section follows a consistent
structure: a plain-language description of the process; the assumptions
required to obtain the stated result, given explicitly; the governing
equation, with its physical interpretation stated before its derivation;
the relevant parameters; and the resulting prediction. Values obtained
from a published source are annotated \CONF. Values that represent a
working assumption not yet confirmed on the fabricated device are
annotated \FLAG.

\section{Overview: Electrode Structure and Operating Principle}
\label{sec:story}

The sensor is a gold electrode with a geometric area of 1.7~mm$^2$
(0.017~cm$^2$) \cit{12} \CONF, which supersedes an earlier 1.5~mm-diameter
design specification (0.0177~cm$^2$); the $\sim$4\% discrepancy is too
small to alter any qualitative conclusion in this report. A single probe
type is attached to the electrode surface via a thiol (sulfur) anchor
(Figure~\ref{fig:assem}): a ten-residue peptide (SGKIPRTLTA) that
constitutes the MMP-9 cleavage site, connected to the gold via a PEG6
spacer, and terminating in a covalently conjugated methylene blue (MB)
label. MB undergoes reversible electron transfer with the gold surface,
which constitutes the measured signal. Upon cleavage of the peptide by
MMP-9, the MB-bearing fragment is released into solution and the
resulting truncated probe is no longer electroactive. An earlier
configuration of this device additionally incorporated a ferrocene (Fc)
reference probe alongside MB for a ratiometric correction; Fc has since
been removed from the protocol \cit{12}, for reasons detailed in
Part~E.

As MMP-9 activity increases, the quantity of intact MB-bearing probe
decreases, and the corresponding voltammetric peak diminishes in
proportion. The extent of this decrease, measured on a single electrode
before and after a defined incubation period, constitutes the readout of
enzymatic activity from which [MMP-9] is subsequently estimated.

addressing one stage of this signal chain:

\section{Part A: The Surface Chemistry}
\label{sec:sam}

\subsection{Overview}

An accurate description of the electrode surface composition is a
prerequisite for the electrochemical treatment that follows. This section
supersedes an earlier, incorrect description. Previous versions of this
report, and related layer reports, described a 6-mercapto-1-hexanol (MCH)
backfill step intended to fill unoccupied sites between probes, together
with a two-component deposition incorporating a ferrocene (Fc) reference
probe. Neither description reflects the current protocol. The protocol in
current use \cit{12} deposits a single probe species, MB-peptide only,
with no MCH and no Fc.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Single-component deposition (no MCH, no Fc).} This is a
  confirmed property of the fabrication protocol \cit{12}, not a modelling
  choice.
\item \textbf{Total probe coverage $\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$.}
  This value has not been measured for the electrodes in use; it is an
  engineering estimate within the range reported for a comparable
  peptide-PEG system in the literature \cit{8} \FLAG. Direct measurement
  is not possible with the instrumentation currently available to the
  team \cit{12}, so this value is expected to remain a placeholder for
  the foreseeable future. Any prediction sensitive to the exact value of
  $\Grtot$ should be interpreted accordingly.
\item \textbf{Exposure of bare gold between probes.} In the absence of an
  MCH backfill, regions of bare gold are likely to remain exposed between
  probes, presenting a plausible route for non-specific protein adsorption
  from wound fluid (fouling). This has not been experimentally assessed
  \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

The distance distribution $P(d)$ used in Part~\ref{sec:geom} was derived
under the assumption of an isolated, PEG6-spaced probe with no neighbouring
species. This assumption has not been re-derived or validated for the
present MCH-free surface, and $P(d)$ should accordingly be regarded as a
plausible working form rather than a measured quantity.

\section{Part B: Position of the MB Label Above the Electrode}
\label{sec:geom}

\subsection{Overview}

The MB label is not rigidly fixed above the gold surface: it is positioned
at the terminus of a flexible tether (the PEG6 spacer together with the
peptide itself) and consequently samples a distribution of distances. The
rate of electron transfer decreases approximately exponentially with
distance, consistent with standard Marcus/tunnelling theory \cit{9,10},
such that only probes positioned close to the gold contribute
appreciably to the measured signal.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{The tether behaves as a freely-jointed chain (FJC).} This is
  a standard polymer-physics model for a flexible, non-rigid linker, used
  here for the peptide segment of the tether. It is a modelling choice, not
  a measurement on our specific peptide.
\item \textbf{This geometry has not been validated on the real (MCH-free)
  surface.} See Part~\ref{sec:sam}: the calculation below assumes a clean,
  isolated PEG6-spaced probe, which has not been directly confirmed on the
  fabricated electrode \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Derivation}

\textbf{Chain statistics.} The peptide segment of the tether (SGKIPRTLTA,
$N_{\rm res}=10$ residues) is treated as a freely-jointed chain (FJC): a
sequence of $N_K$ independent, freely-orienting rigid (Kuhn) segments,
the standard coarse-grained model for a flexible, unfolded polymer
\cit{3}. For a polypeptide backbone, the contour length per residue is
$l_0 \approx 0.38$~nm and the Kuhn length is $b \approx 0.76$~nm (two
residues per Kuhn segment) \cit{3}, giving
\begin{equation}
  N_K = \frac{N_{\rm res}\,l_0}{b} = \frac{10 \times 0.38}{0.76} = 5
  \ \text{Kuhn segments.}
  \label{eq:NK}
\end{equation}
Because the $N_K$ segments are oriented independently and isotropically,
the end-to-end vector $\mathbf{R}$ of the chain is a sum of $N_K$
independent, identically distributed random vectors. By the central
limit theorem, for $N_K \gg 1$ each Cartesian component of $\mathbf{R}$
is approximately normally distributed with mean zero and variance
\begin{equation}
  \langle R^2\rangle = N_K b^2, \qquad
  \sigma_i^2 = \frac{\langle R^2 \rangle}{3} \quad (i=x,y,z),
  \label{eq:fjc_var}
\end{equation}
the factor of 3 arising because the total mean-square displacement is
shared equally among the three Cartesian directions by isotropy.
Numerically,
\[
  \langle R^2 \rangle = N_K b^2 = 5\times(0.76~{\rm nm})^2 = 2.888~{\rm nm^2},
  \qquad
  \sigma_z = \sqrt{\langle R^2\rangle/3} = 0.981~{\rm nm},
\]
where $z$ is the coordinate normal to the gold surface, along which
distance from the electrode is measured.

\textbf{Reference point.} The peptide is not anchored directly to the
gold; it is attached via a PEG6 spacer, treated as a second, independent
FJC segment of 6 ethylene-oxide units ($l_{\rm EO}=0.35$~nm per unit,
same Kuhn length $b=0.76$~nm), giving $n_{K,\rm PEG} =
6\times0.35/0.76 = 2.76$ Kuhn segments. Because this spacer is short, its
equilibrium extension is estimated using Flory's excluded-volume scaling
law for a self-avoiding chain in a good solvent,
\begin{equation}
  R_F = n_K^{3/5}\,b,
  \label{eq:flory}
\end{equation}
rather than the ideal-chain ($R\propto N^{1/2}$) result used for the
longer, more nearly ideal peptide above. This gives $R_F =
2.76^{3/5}\times0.76~{\rm nm} = 1.40$~nm, taken as the mean distance
$d_{\rm PEG}$ of the PEG6 tip from the gold surface.

\textbf{Resulting distribution.} The MB label sits at the tip of the
peptide segment, itself rooted at the PEG6 tip at mean distance $d_{\rm
PEG}$ from the gold. Since the gold surface is impenetrable, only
configurations with $d \geq d_{\rm PEG}$ are physically accessible;
folding the Gaussian distribution of the $z$-displacement (variance
$\sigma_z^2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:fjc_var}) onto this half-line and
renormalising gives the half-Gaussian distance distribution
\begin{equation}
  P(d) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{1}{\sigma_z}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
  \qquad d \geq d_{\rm PEG},
  \label{eq:Pd}
\end{equation}
with $d_{\rm PEG}=1.40$~nm and $\sigma_z=0.981$~nm as derived above. The
prefactor $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ restores $\int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{\infty}
P(d)\,{\rm d}d = 1$, since an unrestricted Gaussian of standard deviation
$\sigma_z$ has exactly half its area on each side of its mean.

\textbf{Electron-transfer rate versus distance.} MB undergoes
non-adiabatic electron transfer with the gold electrode by
quantum-mechanical tunnelling through the intervening tether. Marcus
theory predicts that the electronic coupling element, and hence the
standard rate constant $\kzero$, decays exponentially with tunnelling
distance \cit{9,10}:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero(d) = \kzero(0)\,e^{-\beta d},
  \label{eq:k0d}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the tunnelling decay constant of the intervening medium
and $\kzero(0)$ is the (extrapolated, not physically attainable) rate at
zero distance. For alkanethiol-tethered ferrocene and methylene blue,
$\beta = 1.0$~\AA$^{-1} = 10$~nm$^{-1}$ is a confirmed literature value
\cit{4}; no PEG-specific value for $\beta$ exists in the papers consulted
for this report, so the alkanethiol value is used as a placeholder
\FLAG. Dauphin-Ducharme (2017) \cit{1} report $\kzero(0) =
1.5\times10^8$~s$^{-1}$ for MB directly at the electrode (their
Figure~4 extrapolation). Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d} at the PEG6 tip,
$d=d_{\rm PEG}=1.40$~nm, gives the reference rate used throughout this
report:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero_{\rm ref} = \kzero(d_{\rm PEG}) = 1.5\times10^8 \times
  e^{-10\times1.40} = 127~{\rm s^{-1}} \quad \FLAG.
  \label{eq:k0ref}
\end{equation}
Because $\kzero(d)$ falls by roughly an order of magnitude for every
$\sim$2.3~\AA\ of additional distance, MB labels only slightly further
from the PEG6 tip than average contribute negligibly to the measured
signal, even though such distances are not rare under $P(d)$; this is
the origin of the "near probes dominate" behaviour shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:geom}B.

\subsection{Operating point}

At the confirmed operating frequency of 10--20~Hz \cit{12}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0ref}
corresponds to $\kappa_{\rm tip} = \kzero_{\rm ref}/(2f) = 6.4$ at 10~Hz
or $3.2$ at 20~Hz, both within the quasi-reversible regime
(Part~\ref{sec:core}) in which the signal is largest, although the
precise value depends on the frequency employed for a given scan. An
earlier version of this report assumed 15~Hz, giving $\kappa_{\rm
tip}=4.2$; this value has been superseded by the confirmed operating
frequency.

\section{Part C: The Core Signal Model}
\label{sec:core}

\subsection{Overview}

Each MB label transitions between oxidised and reduced states at a rate
determined by the applied potential, following Butler-Volmer kinetics,
the standard model for electrode reactions \cit{9}. Because MB is
confined to the electrode surface rather than diffusing freely in
solution, the governing equation for the fraction of MB in the reduced
state admits a single, exact closed-form solution, obtained without
recourse to numerical simulation.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Butler-Volmer kinetics apply to surface-confined MB.} This is
  the standard, textbook model for a redox species attached to an
  electrode, not specific to our device, and is not in question here.
\item \textbf{The potential is held fixed within each SWV half-pulse.} This
  is true by construction of the SWV waveform (Part~\ref{sec:wave}) and is
  what makes the equation below exactly solvable.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations}

The rate of change of reduced-MB coverage $\Grred$ follows:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} = k_f\,(\Grtot - \Grred) - k_b\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
Because $k_f$, $k_b$ are constant during a half-pulse, this has a
closed-form solution:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Grred(\tau) = \Greq + \bigl[\Grred(0) - \Greq\bigr]\,e^{-(k_f+k_b)\tau}\;}
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:soln} indicates that, over each pulse, the MB coverage
relaxes exponentially towards its equilibrium value $\Greq$. When the
pulse duration is short relative to the characteristic reaction time, the
coverage changes negligibly and the resulting signal is weak. When the
pulse duration is long, the system reaches equilibrium before the pulse
ends, and the forward and backward currents cancel, again yielding a weak
signal. The largest net signal therefore occurs at intermediate pulse
durations, where the coverage advances partway towards equilibrium within
each pulse; this accounts for the existence of an optimal scan frequency
(Figure~\ref{fig:geom}D). Integrating this result over the full potential
range, and summing the contribution of every MB label over the distance
distribution $P(d)$ (Part~\ref{sec:geom}), yields the predicted
voltammogram $\DI(E)$: a peak centred near the MB formal potential, whose
amplitude scales with the quantity of intact probe remaining on the
electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) \;\propto\; (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
where $\Fk$ denotes the fraction of probe cleaved by MMP-9. This
proportionality was verified numerically and holds to within 2\% across
the full range of $\Fk$: the peak amplitude decreases in direct proportion
to the cleavage fraction, while its position and width remain
approximately constant. The full derivation of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ode}--\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}
follows below.

\subsection{Derivation}

\textbf{Butler-Volmer rate constants.} For the one-electron-transfer step
between the oxidised and reduced forms of a surface-confined redox
couple, the Butler-Volmer model gives forward (reduction) and backward
(oxidation) first-order rate constants
\begin{equation}
  k_f(E) = \kzero\exp\!\left[-\alpha \frac{n_e F(E-E^{0\prime})}{RT}\right],
  \qquad
  k_b(E) = \kzero\exp\!\left[(1-\alpha) \frac{n_e F(E-E^{0\prime})}{RT}\right],
  \label{eq:bv_rates}
\end{equation}
where $\kzero$ is the standard (potential-independent) rate constant at
the formal potential $E^{0\prime}$, $\alpha$ is the transfer coefficient
($\alpha=0.37$ for MB \cit{1}), $n_e$ is the number of electrons
transferred ($n_e=2$ for MB \cit{6}), and $E-E^{0\prime}$ is the
overpotential. As $E$ moves positive of $E^{0\prime}$, $k_f$ decreases
and $k_b$ increases, driving the couple towards the oxidised state, and
vice versa.

\textbf{Governing equation.} Let $\Grred(t)$ denote the surface coverage
(mol~cm$^{-2}$) of reduced MB at time $t$, with total coverage
$\Grtot=\Grred+\Grox$ conserved. The rate of interconversion is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} = k_f\,\Grox - k_b\,\Grred
  = k_f\,(\Grtot-\Grred) - k_b\,\Grred = k_f\Grtot - (k_f+k_b)\Grred,
  \label{eq:ode_full}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode} written out. Because $E$, and therefore
$k_f$ and $k_b$, is held fixed within a single SWV half-pulse
(Part~\ref{sec:wave}), Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode_full} is a linear, first-order
ODE with constant coefficients over that interval, and is exactly
solvable.

\textbf{Solution by integrating factor.} Writing $K \equiv k_f+k_b$,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode_full} reads $\dot{\Grred} + K\Grred = k_f\Grtot$.
Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{Kt}$,
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\left(\Grred\,e^{Kt}\right) = k_f\Grtot\,e^{Kt}.
\]
Integrating from $0$ to $\tau$ and dividing through by $e^{K\tau}$,
\[
  \Grred(\tau) = \Grred(0)\,e^{-K\tau}
  + \frac{k_f\Grtot}{K}\left(1-e^{-K\tau}\right)
  = \frac{k_f}{K}\Grtot + \left[\Grred(0) - \frac{k_f}{K}\Grtot\right]e^{-K\tau}.
\]
Identifying the equilibrium coverage $\Greq \equiv (k_f/K)\Grtot$, the
coverage the system would reach at fixed $E$ as $\tau\to\infty$,
recovers the closed-form result already stated as Eq.~\eqref{eq:soln}:
\[
  \Grred(\tau) = \Greq + [\Grred(0)-\Greq]\,e^{-(k_f+k_b)\tau}.
\]

\textbf{Current and the SWV waveform.} The measured current is
proportional to the net rate of charge transfer,
$I = n_eFA\,[k_b\Grred(\tau) - k_f\Grox(\tau)]$ (anodic-positive
convention). Each SWV cycle at staircase potential $E_{\rm step}$
consists of a forward half-pulse at $E_{\rm step}+\Esw$ of duration
$\tau=1/(2f)$, followed immediately by a reverse half-pulse at
$E_{\rm step}-\Esw$ of the same duration, with $\Grred$ carried
continuously from the end of one half-pulse into the start of the next
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:soln} applied twice in succession, not independently).
The net SWV current at each step is the difference of the two sampled
currents,
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E_{\rm step}) = I_{\rm fwd} - I_{\rm rev},
  \label{eq:DI_step}
\end{equation}
evaluated after the simulator has been run through several full cycles
at the initial potential to reach the periodic steady state, so that the
reported $\DI$ is independent of the (arbitrary) initial condition
$\Grred(0)$.

\textbf{Averaging over the probe population.} Eqs.~\eqref{eq:soln}--\eqref{eq:DI_step}
give the voltammogram for a single MB label at a fixed distance $d$ from
the electrode, entering only through $\kzero=\kzero(d)$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d}, Part~\ref{sec:geom}). Since different labels sit at
different distances according to $P(d)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Pd}), the total
measured current is the coverage-weighted average over the distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) = (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot\,A \int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{d_{\rm max}}
  P(d)\,\DI_{\rm norm}\bigl(E; \kzero(d)\bigr)\,{\rm d}d,
  \label{eq:dist_avg}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm norm}$ is Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_step} evaluated per unit
coverage and unit area, the factor $(1-\Fk)$ scales the total quantity
of intact (uncleaved) probe remaining on the electrode, and the integral
is evaluated numerically by quadrature (80 points, Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}D)
since Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_step} has no closed form in $d$. Equation~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}
is the empirical statement that, to within 2\%, Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg}
factorises as a peak shape independent of $\Fk$ multiplied by the scalar
prefactor $(1-\Fk)\Grtot$.

\subsection{Validation against published data}

This subsection reports the outcome of direct comparisons against two
published sources. Two claims made in an earlier version of this
document, agreement with the Laviron (1979) peak shape to within 1\% and
agreement with Dauphin-Ducharme (2017) peak widths to within 4~mV, were
not supported by any underlying calculation and are superseded by the
results reported below, which were obtained by direct computation and by
reading both source publications in full.

\textbf{Laviron (1979) \cit{2}.} The full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM)
expression used throughout this report is Laviron's closed-form analytic
result (Equation~6 of that work), the reversible-limit peak shape
\begin{equation}
  |\Psi(\eta)| = \frac{\eta}{(1+\eta)^2}, \qquad
  \eta \equiv \exp\!\left[\frac{n_e F(E-E^{0\prime})}{RT}\right] \equiv e^{\xi}.
  \label{eq:laviron_psi}
\end{equation}
\textit{Derivation of the FWHM.} The peak of Eq.~\eqref{eq:laviron_psi}
is found by setting $d|\Psi|/d\eta=0$:
$(1+\eta)^2 - \eta\cdot2(1+\eta) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (1+\eta)-2\eta=0
\;\Rightarrow\; \eta=1$, giving $|\Psi|_{\rm max}=1/4$. The half-maximum
points satisfy $\eta/(1+\eta)^2 = 1/8$, i.e.\ $8\eta = (1+\eta)^2$, a
quadratic in $\eta$:
\[
  \eta^2 - 6\eta + 1 = 0
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \eta_\pm = 3\pm2\sqrt{2}.
\]
Since $(3+2\sqrt2)(3-2\sqrt2)=9-8=1$, the two roots are reciprocal, so
$\xi_\pm=\ln\eta_\pm=\pm\ln(3+2\sqrt2)$ and the width in $\xi$ is
$\Delta\xi = 2\ln(3+2\sqrt2) = 3.525$. Converting back to potential via
$\xi=n_eF(E-E^{0\prime})/RT$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FWHM} = \Delta\xi\,\frac{RT}{n_eF}
  = \frac{2\ln(3+2\sqrt2)\,RT}{n_eF}
  \approx \frac{3.53\,RT}{n_eF} = \frac{90.6}{n_e}~\text{mV at }25^\circ\text{C},
  \label{eq:fwhm}
\end{equation}
giving $45.3$~mV at $n_e=2$, the value used throughout this report. This
expression is used here exactly as published and is not re-derived from
the present simulator. Agreement between the
simulator and the full \emph{shape} of the Laviron curve has not been
established. Such agreement is not, in general, expected: the Laviron
expression describes an idealised, continuously-swept system at
equilibrium, whereas the present simulator describes a discretely-pulsed
square-wave protocol sampled once per half-cycle. Direct computation
confirms that the simulator output differs from the Laviron shape by a
substantial margin (24\% RMS at $\kappa=10$), and this discrepancy does
not diminish as $\kappa$ increases. Above $\kappa\approx15$, the
simulator instead exhibits numerical instability, evident as erratic peak
position and shape; this behaviour arises from floating-point
cancellation in the $I_f - I_b$ subtraction at large $\kappa$ and does
not represent a physical prediction. This instability does not affect the
predictions reported elsewhere in this document: the operating value of
$\kappa_{\rm tip}$ for MB is 3.2--6.4 (Part~\ref{sec:geom}), well within
the range over which the simulator behaves in a numerically stable
manner. A quantitative shape match to the Laviron limit is nonetheless
not currently established.

\textbf{Dauphin-Ducharme et al.\ (2017) \cit{1}.} This publication
characterises methylene blue tethered to gold via a hexanethiol linker
(their Figure~4), a system directly comparable in general chemistry, and
reports $k^0 = 1.5\pm0.5$~cm~s$^{-1}$, converted therein to
$k^0_{x=8\text{\AA}} = 5.0(\pm1.7)\times10^4$~s$^{-1}$, with
$\alpha=0.37\pm0.02$. Both values were confirmed by direct reading of the
source text. An earlier version of the model code had recorded this rate
constant as $5\times10^3$~s$^{-1}$, misattributed to a different figure
within the same publication; this represents an order-of-magnitude
transcription error, which has since been corrected. At the reported
$k^0$ and the frequencies employed in that study (20--1000~Hz), $\kappa$
takes values of order $10$ to $10^3$, within the numerically unstable
regime identified above. A quantitative comparison to these data is
therefore not currently possible. This limitation arises from the
present simulator, which has not been validated over the relevant range
of $\kappa$, and not from any deficiency in the cited source, which
remains directly applicable. Resolution would require a numerically
stable reformulation of the current calculation at high $\kappa$; this
has not been undertaken and is recorded here as an explicit limitation of
the present implementation.

\section{Part D: Rationale for Square-Wave Voltammetry}
\label{sec:wave}

\subsection{Overview}

Square-wave voltammetry samples the current only at the end of each
pulse, by which point the background charging current at the electrode
has decayed to a negligible level, and subsequently subtracts the
forward and backward readings. Both operations remove background
contributions that a simpler technique, such as cyclic voltammetry, would
retain, giving SWV substantially greater sensitivity for a surface-bound
probe of this type \cit{11}.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{The sensitivity advantage of SWV over CV/DPV for
  surface-bound probes} is an established result in electroanalytical
  theory \cit{11}, not specific to this device.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Operating parameters}

The specific SWV settings employed in this model are as follows: pulse
amplitude $\Esw = 25$~mV \cit{6,12} \CONF, matching the Baker (2006)
literature value exactly and confirmed against the fabricated device and
potentiostat settings; operating frequency 10--20~Hz \cit{12} \CONF;
electrode area 1.7~mm$^2$ (Overview) \CONF; transfer coefficient
$\alpha = 0.37$ and $n_e = 2$ for MB \cit{1} \CONF.

\section{Part E: Removal of the Reference Reporter and Its Implications}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{Protocol Change: Removal of Fc}

Earlier versions of this model included a ferrocene (Fc) reference probe,
co-deposited alongside the MB-peptide, used to construct a ratiometric
correction intended to cancel electrode-to-electrode variation. The team
has since removed Fc from the protocol entirely \cit{12}, and the
electrode now carries a single probe type, MB-peptide only. This section
replaces the earlier ratiometric-correction description with an account
of what that removal changes, what it does not change, and what is now
the principal open risk.

The removal is not simply a loss of capability. Several of the problems
identified in earlier versions of this report concerned Fc specifically:
its formal potential did not match the literature anchor used, its
borrowed rate constant predicted a vanishing signal that contradicted
direct observation, and no distinct dual peak was observed on a real
scan. Removing Fc resolves each of these problems, since the component to
which they applied is no longer part of the device.

\subsection{Reassessment: The Actual Contribution of Fc}

The measurement protocol (Overview) already takes an SWV scan on each
electrode \emph{before} incubation and again \emph{after}: a baseline and
a follow-up reading on the same physical electrode, not a comparison
between different electrodes. Without Fc, the cleavage fraction is read
directly from that pair:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)}
  \label{eq:Fk_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm MB}$ is the net SWV peak height (Parts~\ref{sec:core}--\ref{sec:wave}).

What the earlier Fc-based ratio actually contributed can be established
algebraically:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Fk^{\rm rec}\right)_{\rm old\ (with\ Fc)} = 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}
  \;=\; 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)} \quad\text{\emph{whenever} } \DI_{\rm Fc}(T) = \DI_{\rm Fc}(0),
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio_old}
\end{equation}
so the Fc terms cancel exactly, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old} reduces to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} whenever Fc coverage is stable during incubation.
Fc therefore never corrected for baseline electrode-to-electrode coverage
differences: Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} already self-normalises against
that difference, since each electrode is compared only to its own earlier
reading, not to any other electrode or to a reference curve. An earlier
version of this document stated that removing Fc would make the
$\sim$100$\times$ electrode-to-electrode signal variation (Part~\ref{sec:sam})
the central, unavoidable problem for calibration; that claim overstated
the loss and is superseded by the analysis above.

\subsection{Consequences of Removal}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{No means of detecting non-cleavage signal drift during
  incubation.} The diagnostic value of Fc lay in flagging cases where
  $\DI_{\rm Fc}(T) \neq \DI_{\rm Fc}(0)$, that is, where something other
  than MMP-9 cleavage (non-specific fouling, partial probe desorption,
  electrode degradation) altered the signal during the incubation window.
  Without Fc, any such drift is indistinguishable from real cleavage and
  is read as additional $\Fk$. This risk is directional: it biases
  $\Fk^{\rm rec}$ upward, towards over-reading cleavage, so a chronic
  wound could read as more severe than it is, whereas a healing wound is
  unlikely to read as falsely reassuring \FLAG. This effect has not been
  quantified and is the single most important open question created by
  this change.
\item \textbf{No real-time electrode-health check.} A dead, poorly
  contacted, or badly fabricated electrode would previously appear as a
  missing or anomalous Fc signal, independent of the MB read. That
  diagnostic is no longer available; a failed electrode and a
  fully-cleaved electrode may now be harder to distinguish from the MB
  scan alone \FLAG.
\item \textbf{The 3.4\% noise floor used in the LOD calculation
  (Part~\ref{sec:conc}) no longer applies as derived.} That figure came
  from error propagation through the ratio-of-ratios in
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old}. The noise floor for the simpler
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} has not been separately derived, and there is
  no principled reason to assume it is unchanged: removing a measured
  quantity from the calculation could plausibly reduce propagated noise
  (fewer error sources) or increase it, if Fc had been absorbing a
  systematic error term, such as amplifier gain drift, that
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} no longer cancels \FLAG. Until re-derived,
  every LOD and clinical prediction figure in Part~\ref{sec:conc} that
  depends on the 3.4\% value should be treated as provisional.
\item \textbf{Low-coverage electrodes retain worse precision, even though
  $\Fk^{\rm rec}$ itself is unbiased by coverage.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple}
  correctly cancels the electrode-to-electrode baseline coverage
  difference algebraically, but an electrode with low absolute coverage
  still has a smaller absolute $\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$, and therefore a worse
  signal-to-noise ratio against any fixed absolute noise floor (electronic
  noise, baseline drift) when resolving a small $\Fk$. This is a distinct
  effect from the baseline-cancellation question addressed above, and the
  $\sim$100$\times$ coverage variation (Part~\ref{sec:sam}) remains
  relevant to it \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\section{Part F: From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Overview}

At the enzyme concentrations expected in a wound sample (roughly
6--30~nM), MMP-9 is present in large excess over the surface probe, so the
cleavage fraction follows simple first-order kinetics.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{MMP-9 is in large excess over surface probe, so cleavage is
  pseudo-first-order.} Consistent with clinical concentration ranges
  (6--30~nM) against a surface probe density orders of magnitude lower.
\item \textbf{Surface efficiency $\eta = 0.20$.} A central estimate, not a
  value measured for this device \FLAG. It accounts for cleavage on a
  surface typically proceeding more slowly than in free solution (steric
  hindrance, orientation effects), and still requires direct measurement,
  for example by comparing electrode readout to a parallel solution-phase
  fluorescence assay.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Derivation and parameters}

With MMP-9 in large excess (Assumptions above), its concentration
$[\text{MMP-9}]$ is effectively constant over the incubation, so the
depletion of intact surface probe $\Grtot^{\rm intact}(t)$ follows
first-order kinetics in the probe itself:
\[
  \frac{d\Grtot^{\rm intact}}{dt} = -k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,\Grtot^{\rm intact},
  \qquad k_{\rm eff} = \eta \times (k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol},
\]
where $(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$ is the measured solution-phase
second-order cleavage rate constant and $\eta$ is a surface correction
factor (Assumptions above). This is separable and integrates directly:
\[
  \Grtot^{\rm intact}(T) = \Grtot^{\rm intact}(0)\,
  e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}.
\]
Since $\Fk \equiv 1 - \Grtot^{\rm intact}(T)/\Grtot^{\rm intact}(0)$ by
definition, this gives the cleavage fraction directly:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T) = 1 - e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
where $(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol} = 67{,}500$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ is the
measured solution-phase cleavage rate for this exact peptide \cit{7}
\CONF.

\textbf{Bottom line.} The core electrochemistry (Parts~\ref{sec:core} and
\ref{sec:wave}) rests on standard Butler-Volmer theory and an exact,
independently verifiable closed-form solution; the governing equations
are not in question. Whether this specific numerical implementation
reproduces a real published curve is a separate question, and remains
open (Part~\ref{sec:core}, "Validation against published data") rather
than confirmed. What requires direct experimental attention before the
device can be trusted end to end concerns the real surface: what is
actually present on it (Part~\ref{sec:sam}), and, now that Fc has been
removed, whether non-cleavage signal drift during incubation can be
ruled out or bounded (Part~\ref{sec:ratio}). This is the principal
failure mode the device can no longer detect on its own.
