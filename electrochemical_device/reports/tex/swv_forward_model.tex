\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
This report presents the forward model of the square-wave voltammetric
readout of the MMP-9 sensor. The model predicts the voltammogram recorded at a
gold electrode carrying a methylene blue (MB) labelled peptide probe as a
function of the MMP-9 concentration and the incubation time. The signal chain is
formulated in seven steps: surface composition, tether geometry, single-probe
electrode kinetics under a square-wave potential program, summation over the
probe population, validation, readout of the cleavage fraction from the MB peak
height, and cleavage kinetics with detection limit. The peak height is
proportional to the fraction of intact probe, so the cleavage fraction
$\Fkrec=1-\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$ is independent of probe coverage.
At the working point (10~Hz, $\Grtot=\gammaZero$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$,
$A=0.017$~cm$^2$) the predicted peak is \peakNA~nA at \peakEmV~mV with a width of
\fwhmMV~mV, arising from labels a few tenths of a nanometre above the tip of the
spacer. Healing (6~nM) and chronic (30~nM) MMP-9 concentrations give cleavage
fractions of \fkHealThirty\% and \fkChronThirty\% after 30~minutes. The absolute current depends on
unmeasured inputs and is uncertain by a large factor; the detection limit depends on the
readout noise and the surface efficiency of cleavage, neither of which has been measured.

\noindent\textbf{Scope and conventions.}
The model describes the MB channel of the electrode. The electrode is prepared
by one-pot co-immobilisation of the MB-peptide-PEG6 probe and ferrocene-hexanethiol
(Fc-hexanethiol) without mercaptohexanol (MCH); Fc-hexanethiol acts as the diluent
and is not represented in the model (Section~I, Part~F). The report is organised
into a description of the system and measurement (Section~I) followed by eight
parts, A to H, each treating one step of the signal chain. Each part states its
objective and result, the assumptions, the governing equations with derivations
and worked values, the predictions, and the supporting evidence. Values obtained
from a published source or confirmed on the fabricated device are annotated
\CONF. Working assumptions not yet confirmed on the device are annotated
\FLAG. Symbols and abbreviations are listed in the Nomenclature.

\section{System Description and Measurement Principle}
\label{sec:story}

\subsection{Application}

The matrix metalloproteinase MMP-9 is present at a higher concentration in
chronic (non-healing) wound fluid than in healing wound fluid; representative
values are about 6~nM in healing and about 30~nM in chronic wounds \cit{7b}. The
device estimates the MMP-9 concentration in a small sample of wound fluid from an
electrical measurement on a disposable gold electrode. The model relates the
MMP-9 concentration and incubation time to the electrode readout.

\subsection{Electrode and probe}

The sensor is a gold electrode of geometric area 1.7~mm$^2$ (0.017~cm$^2$)
\cit{12} \CONF. Two species are attached to the gold through thiol anchors in a single
preparation step (\figref{fig:assembly}A):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item the \textbf{probe}: the peptide SGKIPRTLTA, which contains the MMP-9 cleavage
  site (the bond between residues 7 and 8), joined to the gold through a flexible
  spacer of six ethylene-glycol units (PEG6) and carrying the redox label
  \textbf{methylene blue} (MB) at its free end;
\item \textbf{ferrocene-hexanethiol} (Fc-hexanethiol): a six-carbon chain terminated
  by a ferrocene group, co-deposited with the probe in one pot without
  mercaptohexanol (MCH). It occupies the gold between probes and acts as the diluent
  that keeps probes separated \cit{12} \CONF.
\end{itemize}
The MB label exchanges electrons with the gold, and this exchange is the measured
signal. The model describes the MB channel; Fc-hexanethiol is not represented, and
Part~F treats the consequences of this scope.

\subsection{Measured quantity}

\textbf{Voltammogram.} The current is a flow of electrons between the gold and the MB
label. MB is either reduced (two electrons gained) or oxidised, depending on the
voltage applied to the electrode relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The
\textbf{formal potential} $\Eform$ is the voltage at which the two forms are equally
favoured; for MB it is close to $-0.25$~V \cit{1}. As the applied voltage is
varied through $\Eform$ a current flows, and the graph of current against applied
voltage, the \textbf{voltammogram}, has a peak near $\Eform$ (\figref{fig:anatomy}A).
The peak is characterised by its \emph{height}, proportional to the number of MB
labels participating, its \emph{position}, near $\Eform$, and its \emph{width}, the full
width at half the maximum height (FWHM).

\textbf{Effect of cleavage.} Only MB labels that remain tethered to the gold exchange
electrons at a rate sufficient to contribute. Cleavage by MMP-9 releases the
MB-bearing fragment, so the peak height decreases in proportion to the fraction of
intact probes (\figref{fig:anatomy}C). The \textbf{cleavage fraction} $\Fk$ is the
fraction of probes cleaved during an incubation.

\textbf{Square-wave voltammetry (SWV).} The applied voltage is stepped along a
staircase and a square wave of amplitude $\Esw=25$~mV is superimposed, so that at
each step the voltage alternates between a forward (higher) and a reverse (lower)
pulse. The current is sampled at the end of each pulse and the net current
$\Delta I=I_{\rm fwd}-I_{\rm rev}$ is plotted against the staircase voltage
(\figref{fig:anatomy}B). The square-wave frequency is 10 to 20~Hz \cit{12} \CONF. The
rationale for the technique is given in Part~C.

\subsection{Measurement protocol and readout}

Each electrode is scanned twice: once \emph{before} incubation and once
\emph{after} the sample has been on it for a time $T$ (\figref{fig:protocol}). The
cleavage fraction is read from the pair of peak heights on the same electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \Fkrec = 1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)},
  \label{eq:Fk_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm MB}$ is the net peak height. Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} follows because the peak height is proportional to the amount of
intact probe (Part~D): $\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)=1-\Fk$. Because each electrode is compared only
with its own initial scan, the number of probes it carries cancels
out of $\Fkrec$ (Part~F). The MMP-9 concentration is then obtained from
$\Fkrec$ using the cleavage kinetics of Part~G.

\section{Part A: The Electrode Surface}
\label{sec:sam}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To determine the probe density and whether probes may be treated
as independent. \textbf{Result.} At the working coverage of
\gammaZero~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ the electrode carries about \nProbesFmol~fmol of probe
(\nProbes{} molecules), one per \areaPerProbeNM~nm$^2$ of gold. The mean
spacing is \spacingNM~nm, above the distance (\overlapSpacing~nm) below which the labels of
neighbouring probes could overlap, so probes are treated as independent. The coverage is an
assumed value; it sets the absolute size of the current but does not enter the readout
(Part~F).

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{One-pot deposition of MB-peptide-PEG6 and Fc-hexanethiol, without MCH.}
  This is the protocol in use \cit{12} \CONF.
\item \textbf{Total probe coverage $\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol\,cm$^{-2}$.}
  An engineering estimate within the range reported for a comparable
  peptide-PEG system \cit{8}, not a measurement on these electrodes \FLAG. Direct
  measurement is not possible with the available instrumentation \cit{12}, and
  the coverage is not yet reproducible from electrode to electrode.
\item \textbf{The surface composition differs from the solution composition.} The
  peptide-PEG probe is expected to adsorb more strongly than the short
  Fc-hexanethiol, so the surface fraction of probe is not the fraction in the
  deposition solution \FLAG.
\item \textbf{Fc-hexanethiol fills the gold between probes.} Whether it forms a
  fully packed layer has not been determined \FLAG. If it does, little bare gold remains
  and non-specific adsorption of protein onto gold is reduced, although adsorption on the
  layer itself is not excluded.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations}

\textbf{Probes per area and spacing.} A coverage $\Grtot$ in mol\,cm$^{-2}$
corresponds to $\Grtot N_A$ molecules per cm$^2$, where $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}$~mol$^{-1}$ is
Avogadro's number. The area per probe is the reciprocal, and for probes on a square grid
the centre-to-centre spacing is its square root:
\begin{equation}
  a=\frac{1}{\Grtot N_A},\qquad s=\sqrt{a}.
  \label{eq:spacing}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} $\Grtot N_A = 3\times10^{-12}\times6.022\times10^{23}
=1.81\times10^{12}$~cm$^{-2}=1.81\times10^{-2}$~nm$^{-2}$ (using
1~cm$^2=10^{14}$~nm$^2$), so $a=\areaPerProbeNM$~nm$^2$ and $s=\spacingNM$~nm. For randomly
placed probes the mean distance to the nearest neighbour is smaller. With number density $\rho=1/a$, the
probability that a disc of radius $r$ contains no other probe is $e^{-\rho\pi r^2}$, so the nearest neighbour lies
between $r$ and $r+{\rm d}r$ with probability density $p(r)=2\pi\rho r\,e^{-\rho\pi r^2}$ (the probability that the
disc is empty, times the probability that a probe lies in the ring of area $2\pi r\,{\rm d}r$). The mean is
\begin{equation}
  \langle r\rangle=\int_0^\infty r\,p(r)\,{\rm d}r=2\pi\rho\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-\pi\rho r^2}{\rm d}r .
  \label{eq:nn_int}
\end{equation}
This integral is evaluated in two steps, and the same Gaussian integral is used again in Part~B.

\emph{Step 1: the Gaussian integral.} For $a>0$ let $I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}\,{\rm d}x$. Its square is a double
integral over the plane, $I^2=\int\!\!\int e^{-a(x^2+y^2)}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y$. In polar coordinates $x^2+y^2=\rho'^2$ and the
area element is $\rho'\,{\rm d}\rho'\,{\rm d}\phi$, so
\[
  I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\!{\rm d}\phi\int_0^\infty e^{-a\rho'^2}\rho'\,{\rm d}\rho'
  =2\pi\left[-\frac{e^{-a\rho'^2}}{2a}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{\pi}{a}.
\]
Hence $I=\sqrt{\pi/a}$, and because the integrand is even the integral over $[0,\infty)$ is half of this:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}\,{\rm d}x=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}},\qquad
  \int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}\,{\rm d}x=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}} .
  \label{eq:int_gauss}
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 2: the second moment.} Differentiate the second form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:int_gauss} with respect to $a$. On the left,
$\partial_ae^{-ax^2}=-x^2e^{-ax^2}$; on the right, $\frac{d}{da}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}a^{-1/2}\bigr)=-\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{4}a^{-3/2}$. Cancelling
the minus signs,
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\infty x^2e^{-ax^2}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4a^{3/2}} .
  \label{eq:int_r2}
\end{equation}
Put $a=\pi\rho$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nn_int}:
\[
  \langle r\rangle=2\pi\rho\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4(\pi\rho)^{3/2}}=\frac{2\pi\rho\sqrt\pi}{4\pi^{3/2}\rho^{3/2}}
  =\frac{1}{2\sqrt\rho}=\nnRandom~\text{nm}.
\]
The total number of probes
is $\Grtot N_A A=\nProbes$, that is $\nProbesFmol$~fmol on the
$A=0.017$~cm$^2$ electrode.

\textbf{Fraction of a full monolayer.} A full alkanethiol monolayer on gold has one molecule
per 0.2165~nm$^2$, that is $\fullMono\times10^{-10}$~mol\,cm$^{-2}$. The probe therefore
occupies only \fractionMono\% of the sites, roughly 1 in \oneInN; the remaining sites carry
Fc-hexanethiol if the layer is fully packed.

\textbf{Independence of probes.} The label at the end of a tether is displaced
sideways from its anchor by a random amount with root-mean-square value
$\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_z=\lateralRms$~nm (derived in Part~B). Two labels can interfere
only if their anchors are closer than about twice this, $\overlapSpacing$~nm. The
spacing of \spacingNM~nm exceeds this (\figref{fig:spacing}), which justifies computing the signal of one probe and
multiplying by the number of probes.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_04_spacing.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} mean probe spacing (Eq.~\ref{eq:spacing}) against coverage.
    Below the shaded band the sideways reach of neighbouring labels overlaps. The
    working value (dot) lies above it, and remains above it up to about
    \gammaOverlap~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$. \textbf{B:} a top view of a random arrangement of probes
    at the working coverage, with the sideways reach of each label shaded.}
  \label{fig:spacing}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Result}

At $\Grtot=\gammaZero$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ probes are isolated (spacing \spacingNM~nm) and
number \nProbesFmol~fmol per electrode. The electrode-scale signal is
proportional to $\Grtot$ (Part~D), so every prediction of absolute current
inherits its uncertainty, but the cleavage readout of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} does not
(Part~F).

\section{Part B: Where the MB Label Sits and How Fast It Exchanges Electrons}
\label{sec:geom}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To determine the distribution of the distance $d$ of the MB label from
the gold and the dependence of the electron-transfer rate on $d$. \textbf{Result.} Modelling the
peptide as a freely jointed chain gives a half-Gaussian distribution of $d$, with the
label most probably within about 1~nm of the PEG6 tip. The electron-transfer rate constant
decreases by a factor of ten for every \kOrder~\AA{} (0.23~nm) of additional distance, so that
only labels close to the gold exchange electrons at the rate of the square wave.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The peptide behaves as a freely jointed chain (FJC).} A standard
  model for a flexible, unstructured polymer, supported for short unfolded
  peptides \cit{3}. A modelling choice, not a measurement on this peptide \FLAG.
\item \textbf{The PEG6 spacer sets a floor.} The peptide is treated as rooted at the
  PEG6 tip, at height $d_{\rm PEG}$ above the gold, and cannot pass below it.
\item \textbf{Isolated probe.} Justified by the spacing of Part~A; not tested on the
  real surface \FLAG.
\item \textbf{Electron transfer is tunnelling through the tether,} with a rate
  decaying exponentially with distance. The decay constant is taken from alkanethiols;
  no PEG-specific value is available \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Chain statistics.} The peptide has $N_{\rm res}=10$ residues, each
adding $l_0\approx0.38$~nm of contour length, and a Kuhn length $b\approx0.76$~nm
(two residues) \cit{3}: the length over which the chain has effectively forgotten
its direction. The chain is therefore equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  N_K=\frac{N_{\rm res}\,l_0}{b}=\frac{10\times0.38}{0.76}=\nKuhn
  \label{eq:NK}
\end{equation}
independent rigid segments of length $b$, each pointing in a random direction. Take $z$ as the
height above the PEG6 tip. A segment whose direction is uniformly random over the sphere has a
height component $X=b\cos\theta$. The fraction of the sphere's area between polar angles $\theta$ and $\theta+{\rm d}\theta$
is $\tfrac12\sin\theta\,{\rm d}\theta=\tfrac12|{\rm d}(\cos\theta)|$, so $u=\cos\theta$ is uniformly distributed on $[-1,1]$
with density $\tfrac12$. Therefore
\[
  \langle X\rangle=b\int_{-1}^{1}u\,\tfrac12\,{\rm d}u=0,\qquad
  \langle X^2\rangle=b^2\int_{-1}^{1}u^2\,\tfrac12\,{\rm d}u=b^2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{2}{3}=\frac{b^2}{3}.
\]
The end height is $S_N=X_1+\dots+X_N$, the sum of $N=N_K$ independent, identically distributed terms, with mean
$\langle S_N\rangle=N\langle X\rangle=0$ and, expanding the square,
$\langle S_N^2\rangle=\sum_i\langle X_i^2\rangle+\sum_{i\ne j}\langle X_i\rangle\langle X_j\rangle=Nb^2/3+0$ (the cross terms factorise
because the segments are independent, and vanish because $\langle X\rangle=0$). The
\emph{central limit theorem} states that if the $X_i$ have mean $\mu$ and variance $s^2$, then
\begin{equation}
  \lim_{N\to\infty}\;\Pr\!\left[\frac{S_N-N\mu}{s\sqrt N}\le x\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-y^2/2}\,{\rm d}y ,
  \label{eq:clt}
\end{equation}
that is, $S_N$ is approximately normal with mean $N\mu$ and variance $Ns^2$. Here $\mu=0$ and $s^2=b^2/3$, so
$S_N$ is approximately normal with mean zero and variance
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_z^2=\frac{N_K\,b^2}{3}=\frac{\langle R^2\rangle}{3},\qquad
  \langle R^2\rangle=N_Kb^2=5\times0.76^2=\Rsq~\text{nm}^2,\qquad \sigma_z=\sqrt{\Rsq/3}=\sigmaZ~\text{nm}.
  \label{eq:sigmaz}
\end{equation}
Here $\langle R^2\rangle$ is the mean square end-to-end distance. With the end-to-end vector $\mathbf R=\sum_i\mathbf b_i$,
$\langle R^2\rangle=\sum_i|\mathbf b_i|^2+\sum_{i\ne j}\langle\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j\rangle=N_Kb^2+0$: each segment has $|\mathbf b_i|^2=b^2$, and
the cross terms vanish because $\langle\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j\rangle=\langle\mathbf b_i\rangle\cdot\langle\mathbf b_j\rangle=0$ for independent isotropic segments. The factor 3
arises because, by isotropy, $\langle x^2\rangle=\langle y^2\rangle=\langle z^2\rangle$ and these sum to
$\langle R^2\rangle$. The same argument gives the sideways displacement: with $x$ and $y$ each of variance $\sigma_z^2$, the
root-mean-square distance of the label from the vertical through its anchor is
$\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle+\langle y^2\rangle}=\sqrt2\,\sigma_z=\lateralRms$~nm, as used in Part~A. Five segments is few, so the
approximation is checked directly: the exact distribution of the sum of five such terms
differs from the Gaussian by a maximum of \ksGauss{} in cumulative probability
(\figref{fig:chain}B), so the Gaussian is an excellent description.

\textbf{Reference height: the PEG6 spacer.} The spacer is six ethylene-glycol units of
length $l_{\rm EO}=0.35$~nm, or $n_{K,{\rm PEG}}=6\times0.35/0.76=2.76$ Kuhn segments. For a
chain in a good solvent, Flory's argument balances two contributions to the free energy in units of $k_BT$: the
elastic cost of stretching the chain to an end-to-end distance $R$, $R^2/(n_Kb^2)$, and the repulsion between segments,
which for $n_K$ segments in a volume $\sim R^3$ is $\sim b^3n_K^2/R^3$. Minimising the sum with respect to $R$: $\frac{d}{dR}\bigl[R^2/(n_Kb^2)\bigr]=2R/(n_Kb^2)$ and
$\frac{d}{dR}\bigl[b^3n_K^2R^{-3}\bigr]=-3b^3n_K^2R^{-4}$, so the condition $\frac{d}{dR}(\cdot)=0$ gives
$2R/(n_Kb^2)=3b^3n_K^2/R^4$. Multiplying both sides by $n_Kb^2R^4/2$ gives $R^5=\tfrac32\,b^5n_K^3$, hence
$R=(3/2)^{1/5}\,n_K^{3/5}\,b\approx1.08\,n_K^{3/5}b$; to scaling accuracy the numerical factor is dropped and
\begin{equation}
  d_{\rm PEG}=R_F=n_{K,{\rm PEG}}^{3/5}\,b=2.76^{0.6}\times0.76=\dPEG~\text{nm}.
  \label{eq:flory}
\end{equation}
This is an extrapolation of a long-chain law to a chain of under three segments and is
therefore uncertain \FLAG. The ideal-chain value $\sqrt{n_{K,{\rm PEG}}}\,b=1.26$~nm and the
fully stretched value $6\times0.35=2.1$~nm bracket it, and this range is carried into the
sensitivity analysis (Part~H).

\textbf{Distribution of $d$.} A normal distribution of mean zero and variance $\sigma_z^2$ has probability density
\begin{equation}
  f(z)=\frac{1}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{z^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,{\rm d}z=1.
  \label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
The label height relative to the tip is $z$, but the gold prevents the chain from lying below the tip, so
the label is at $d=d_{\rm PEG}+|z|$. Let $u=d-d_{\rm PEG}\ge0$. The event that $|z|$ lies between $u$ and $u+{\rm d}u$
consists of $z$ lying in $[u,u+{\rm d}u]$ or in $[-u-{\rm d}u,-u]$; these have equal probability, $f(u)\,{\rm d}u$, because
$f$ is even. Hence the density of $u$ is $2f(u)$:
\begin{equation}
  P(d)=2f(d-d_{\rm PEG})=\frac{2}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2/2\sigma_z^2}
  =\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{1}{\sigma_z}\,
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right],\qquad d\ge d_{\rm PEG},
  \label{eq:Pd}
\end{equation}
using $2/\sqrt{2\pi}=\sqrt{2/\pi}$. Three properties of this distribution are needed.

\emph{Normalisation.} Since $f$ is even,
\[
  \int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{\infty}P\,{\rm d}d=2\int_0^\infty f\,{\rm d}u=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,{\rm d}z .
\]
With $a=1/(2\sigma_z^2)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:int_gauss},
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^2/2\sigma_z^2}{\rm d}z=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_z^2}=\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}$, so
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}=1 ,
  \label{eq:int_norm}
\end{equation}
which confirms that $P(d)$ integrates to 1.

\emph{Mean distance.} $\langle d\rangle-d_{\rm PEG}=\int_0^\infty u\,P\,{\rm d}u=\frac{2}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u^2/2\sigma_z^2}{\rm d}u$.
Substitute $w=u^2/(2\sigma_z^2)$, so that ${\rm d}w=u\,{\rm d}u/\sigma_z^2$ and the limits $0$ and $\infty$ are unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u^2/2\sigma_z^2}\,{\rm d}u=\sigma_z^2\int_0^\infty e^{-w}\,{\rm d}w=\sigma_z^2 .
  \label{eq:int_u}
\end{equation}
Therefore $\langle d\rangle=d_{\rm PEG}+\frac{2\sigma_z^2}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}=d_{\rm PEG}+\sigma_z\sqrt{2/\pi}=\meanDana$~nm.

\emph{Tail beyond the contour length.} The chain cannot extend beyond its contour length, so the distribution is cut off at
$d_{\rm max}=d_{\rm PEG}+N_{\rm res}l_0=\dMax$~nm. The probability that $u=d-d_{\rm PEG}$ exceeds $U$ is
$\frac{2}{\sigma_z\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_U^\infty e^{-u^2/2\sigma_z^2}{\rm d}u$. Substituting $t=u/(\sqrt2\sigma_z)$,
${\rm d}u=\sqrt2\sigma_z\,{\rm d}t$, this becomes $\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{U/(\sqrt2\sigma_z)}^\infty e^{-t^2}{\rm d}t$. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:int_gauss}, $\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2}{\rm d}t=1$, so with the error function defined as
$\mathrm{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^xe^{-t^2}{\rm d}t$,
\begin{equation}
  \Pr(u>U)=1-\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{U}{\sqrt2\,\sigma_z}\right).
  \label{eq:int_tail}
\end{equation}
With $U=d_{\rm max}-d_{\rm PEG}=3.8$~nm and $\sigma_z=\sigmaZ$~nm the argument is $3.8/(\sqrt2\times\sigmaZ)=2.74$ and the probability is
\tailMass, which is negligible.

\textbf{Checking the folding.} Folding a Gaussian is not the same as a chain that is
prevented from entering the region below its root. Two exact alternatives were simulated
(\figref{fig:chain}C; $4\times10^5$ random chains). Folding the exact end-height
distribution gives a mean $d$ of \meanDfold~nm, indistinguishable from the half-Gaussian
(\meanDana~nm), and a predicted signal within \foldDiff\% of it. Requiring that \emph{no}
segment of the chain passes below the tip is stricter: only \wallAccept\% of random chains
satisfy it, the distribution shifts to larger $d$ (mean \meanDwall~nm), and the predicted peak
current falls by \wallDrop\% (\sigWall{} against \sigAna~nA). The half-Gaussian is therefore the
central estimate, and the stricter wall model is carried into the sensitivity analysis as a
lower bound on the geometry.

\textbf{Electron-transfer rate against distance.} MB exchanges electrons with the gold by
quantum-mechanical tunnelling through the tether. For an electron of energy $E_e$ meeting a barrier of height $\Phi>E_e$ and width $d$, the
Schr\"odinger equation inside the barrier, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\psi''+\Phi\psi=E_e\psi$, becomes
$\psi''=\gamma^2\psi$ with $\gamma=\sqrt{2m_e(\Phi-E_e)}/\hbar$, whose decaying solution is $\psi\propto e^{-\gamma x}$. The
transmission probability is the ratio of $|\psi|^2$ at the far and near faces, $e^{-2\gamma d}$ (writing $\Phi$ for the barrier height
measured from $E_e$). The electron-transfer rate is proportional to it, and the rate constant $\kzero$ (the rate at which
an MB molecule exchanges electrons when it is at its formal potential, in s$^{-1}$)
inherits this:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero(d)=\kzero(0)\,e^{-\beta d},\qquad \beta=2\gamma=\frac{2\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}}{\hbar}.
  \label{eq:k0d}
\end{equation}
Numerically, for $\Phi=1$~eV$=1.602\times10^{-19}$~J, $\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}=\sqrt{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times1.602\times10^{-19}}=5.40\times10^{-25}$~kg\,m\,s$^{-1}$, so
$\beta=2\times5.40\times10^{-25}/(1.055\times10^{-34})=1.02\times10^{10}$~m$^{-1}$, i.e.\ $\beta=1.025\sqrt{\Phi/\text{eV}}$~\AA$^{-1}$. A barrier of about 1~eV gives
$\beta=1.0$~\AA$^{-1}=\betaNM$~nm$^{-1}$, the accepted value for
tunnelling through alkane chains \cit{4}; no PEG-specific value is available, so this
is an assumed value \FLAG. The anchoring measurement is that of Dauphin-Ducharme
\emph{et al.}\ \cit{1}: MB on a six-carbon linker, with the label 8~\AA{} from the gold, has
$\kzero=5.0\times10^{4}$~s$^{-1}$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d} extrapolated back to zero distance gives
$\kzero(0)=5.0\times10^4\,e^{8}=\kZero$~s$^{-1}$, and evaluated at the PEG6 tip,
\begin{equation}
  \kref=\kzero(d_{\rm PEG})=\kZero\times e^{-\betaNM\times\dPEG}=\kZero\times e^{-13.98}=\kZero\times8.5\times10^{-7}=\kRef~\text{s}^{-1}.
  \label{eq:kref}
\end{equation}
Because $\kzero$ falls tenfold per 0.23~nm, a label 0.5~nm above the tip is already
$\sim150$ times slower. Which labels contribute at a square-wave frequency of 10 to 20~Hz is
therefore determined not by $P(d)$ alone but by the balance shown in \figref{fig:rate}, quantified in Parts~C and D.

\subsection{Result}

Labels are spread over $d=\dPEG$ to about $\dMax$~nm with mean \meanDana~nm
(Eq.~\ref{eq:Pd}), and their electron-transfer rate constants span more than fifteen
orders of magnitude. At the square-wave frequencies used, only a narrow
band, a few tenths of a nanometre above the PEG6 tip (Part~D), produces signal.

\section{Part C: Square-Wave Voltammetry and the Signal from One Probe}
\label{sec:core}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To determine the current produced by a single MB label at a fixed
distance from the gold under a square-wave potential program. \textbf{Result.} The reduced fraction
of MB relaxes exponentially towards the value set by the applied voltage at a rate set by the
electron-transfer rate constant. The current sampled at the end of each pulse is proportional
to the displacement from equilibrium at that instant, and is therefore large only when the
rate constant is comparable to the pulse rate. The single-probe signal has a maximum at
$\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ (\figref{fig:kappa}B).

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Butler--Volmer kinetics for surface-confined MB.} The standard
  textbook model for a redox species attached to an electrode \cit{13}; not
  specific to this device and not in question.
\item \textbf{Constant voltage within each pulse.} True by construction of the
  square wave; it is what makes the equation below exactly solvable.
\item \textbf{Sampling at the end of each pulse.} How the potentiostat samples the current
  affects the predictions at fast rates (Part~E).
\item \textbf{Two-electron exchange in one step,} with $\alpha=0.37$, $n_e=2$
  \cit{1,6} \CONF. The intermediate one-electron step of MB is not resolved in the model
  (the same simplification as the source \cit{1}).
\item \textbf{Room temperature, $T=298.15$~K.} The wound is at about 310~K; the
  measurement temperature has not been set \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Why square-wave voltammetry}

Any electrode voltage change also charges the electrical double layer at the gold
surface, which acts as a capacitor of about $C_{\rm dl}=\CdlUF~\mu$F\,cm$^{-2}$
(a typical value for gold in aqueous electrolyte, assumed here), so
$\Cdev~\mu$F on the 0.017~cm$^2$ electrode. This charging current is not the signal, and
in cyclic voltammetry (CV), where the voltage is swept steadily, it is present all the time.
The size of the wanted signal relative to it can be estimated. For a surface-bound couple in equilibrium with the electrode
the reduced fraction is given by the Nernst equation, $\theta_{\rm red}=1/(1+u)$ with $u=e^{\xi}$ and $\xi$ as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates}. In cyclic voltammetry (CV) the voltage is swept at a rate $v$, so
$E=E_0+vt$ and the current is
\[
  i=n_eFA\Grtot\,\frac{d\theta_{\rm red}}{dt}=n_eFA\Grtot\,v\,\frac{d\theta_{\rm red}}{dE}.
\]
Differentiating $\theta_{\rm red}=(1+u)^{-1}$ with $du/dE=u\,d\xi/dE=u\,n_eF/(RT)$:
\[
  \frac{d\theta_{\rm red}}{dE}=-\frac{1}{(1+u)^2}\frac{du}{dE}=-\frac{n_eF}{RT}\,\frac{u}{(1+u)^2},
\]
so $|i|=\dfrac{n_e^2F^2vA\Grtot}{RT}\,\dfrac{u}{(1+u)^2}$.
The function $u/(1+u)^2$ has derivative $(1-u)/(1+u)^3$, so it is maximal at $u=1$ (that is, at $E=\Eform$), where it equals $\tfrac14$, and the peak current is
$i_p=n_e^2F^2vA\Grtot/(4RT)$. The charging current of a capacitor $C_{\rm dl}A$ swept at rate $v$ is
$i_c=C_{\rm dl}Av$ (from $q=CE$). Their ratio is independent of $v$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{i_p}{i_c}=\frac{n_e^2F^2\Grtot}{4RT\,C_{\rm dl}}
  =\frac{4\times(96485)^2\times3\times10^{-12}}{4\times8.3145\times298.15\times20\times10^{-6}}
  =\cvRatio,
  \label{eq:cv_ratio}
\end{equation}
so the peak sits on a charging plateau of comparable size (\figref{fig:background}B),
and any drift in the plateau is a drift in the apparent peak.

In SWV the voltage is held constant during each pulse and the current is recorded only at
its end. After a voltage jump of size $\Delta E$ the charge $q$ on the capacitor obeys
$\Delta E=R_u\,dq/dt+q/C$. Let $y=q-C\Delta E$; then $dy/dt=dq/dt=(\Delta E-q/C)/R_u=-y/(R_uC)$, whose solution is
$y=y_0e^{-t/(R_uC)}$ with $y_0=q(0)-C\Delta E=-C\Delta E$. Hence $q=C\Delta E(1-e^{-t/(R_uC)})$ and the charging current is
$i=dq/dt=(\Delta E/R_u)\,e^{-t/(R_uC)}$, where
$R_u$ is the resistance of the solution between the electrode and reference (taken as
$\RuOhm~\Omega$, an assumed value). With $R_uC=\RCms$~ms, by the end of a 10~Hz half-pulse
($\halfPulseMs$~ms) the charging current has fallen by a factor of
$e^{-\chargeDecayExp}$. Meanwhile the wanted Faradaic current, which decays on the much
slower time scale of the electron-transfer kinetics, is still large. In addition, taking the
\emph{difference} between the forward and reverse currents cancels remaining slowly varying
background. Both steps remove background that CV retains, giving SWV its greater
sensitivity for a surface-bound probe \cit{5,11}.

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Rate constants.} Let $\Grred$ and $\Grox$ be the amounts of MB per unit area
in the reduced and oxidised forms (mol\,cm$^{-2}$), with $\Grtot=\Grred+\Grox$ fixed. At
applied voltage $E$, the Butler--Volmer model gives first-order rate constants for reduction
($k_f$) and oxidation ($k_b$):
\begin{equation}
  k_f=\kzero\exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha\,\xi\Bigr],\qquad
  k_b=\kzero\exp\!\Bigl[(1-\alpha)\,\xi\Bigr],\qquad
  \xi=\frac{n_eF\,(E-\Eform)}{RT},
  \label{eq:bv_rates}
\end{equation}
where $F=96485$~C\,mol$^{-1}$ is the Faraday constant, $R=8.3145$~J\,mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$
the gas constant, $\alpha$ the transfer coefficient (the fraction of the applied voltage
that helps the reaction go in one direction) and $\xi$ the overpotential in thermal units.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates} follows from transition-state theory, in which a rate is proportional to
$\exp(-\Delta G^\ddagger/RT)$ with $\Delta G^\ddagger$ the free-energy barrier. Changing the electrode voltage by
$E-\Eform$ changes the free energy of the transferred electrons by $n_eF(E-\Eform)$. A fraction $\alpha$ of this
change raises the barrier for reduction and the remaining fraction $1-\alpha$ lowers the barrier for oxidation:
$\Delta G^\ddagger_f=\Delta G^\ddagger_0+\alpha n_eF(E-\Eform)$ and
$\Delta G^\ddagger_b=\Delta G^\ddagger_0-(1-\alpha)n_eF(E-\Eform)$. At $E=\Eform$ both barriers equal
$\Delta G^\ddagger_0$, so both rates equal $\kzero\propto e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger_0/RT}$, and dividing out gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates}. Positive of $\Eform$, $k_b$ grows and $k_f$ shrinks, favouring the oxidised form, and
vice versa. At equilibrium the two rates balance, $k_f\Grox=k_b\Grred$, so
$\Grred/\Grox=k_f/k_b=\exp[-\alpha\xi-(1-\alpha)\xi]=e^{-\xi}$, the Nernst equation, independent of $\alpha$ and $\kzero$.

\textbf{Governing equation.} Reduced MB is formed from oxidised at rate $k_f\Grox$
and lost at rate $k_b\Grred$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt}=k_f\Grox-k_b\Grred=k_f\Grtot-K\,\Grred,\qquad K\equiv k_f+k_b.
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Solution.} Within a pulse $E$, hence $k_f$ and $k_b$, is constant, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode} is a first-order linear
equation with constant coefficients, $\dot\Gamma_{\rm red}+K\Grred=k_f\Grtot$. Multiply both sides by $e^{Kt}$. By the product rule,
$\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\Grred e^{Kt}\bigr)=\dot\Gamma_{\rm red}e^{Kt}+K\Grred e^{Kt}$, which is exactly the left-hand side, so
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\Grred\,e^{Kt}\bigr)=k_f\Grtot\,e^{Kt}.
\]
Integrate from $t=0$ to $t=\tau$. The left side integrates to $\Grred(\tau)e^{K\tau}-\Grred(0)$ and the right side to
$k_f\Grtot\,(e^{K\tau}-1)/K$:
\[
  \Grred(\tau)\,e^{K\tau}-\Grred(0)=\frac{k_f\Grtot}{K}\bigl(e^{K\tau}-1\bigr).
\]
Add $\Grred(0)$ and divide by $e^{K\tau}$:
\[
  \Grred(\tau)=\Grred(0)\,e^{-K\tau}+\frac{k_f\Grtot}{K}\bigl(1-e^{-K\tau}\bigr).
\]
The quantity $\Greq\equiv k_f\Grtot/K$ is the value at which $d\Grred/dt=0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode}, the equilibrium
coverage at this voltage. Substituting it and collecting the terms in $e^{-K\tau}$,
$\Grred(\tau)=\Grred(0)e^{-K\tau}+\Greq-\Greq e^{-K\tau}$, that is
\begin{equation}
  \Grred(\tau)=\Greq+\bigl[\Grred(0)-\Greq\bigr]\,e^{-K\tau}.
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
The coverage relaxes exponentially to equilibrium with time constant $1/K$. This solution is exact,
and agrees with a numerical ODE solver to $\odeMaxErr$ (\figref{fig:ode}A,B).

\textbf{Current.} The current is the charge transferred per second: each mole converted transfers $n_eF$ coulombs, the net rate of
conversion per unit area is $k_b\Grred-k_f\Grox$ (mol\,cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$), and the area is $A$, so
(in C\,mol$^{-1}\times$mol\,cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}\times$cm$^2$ = A)
\begin{equation}
  I=n_eFA\,\bigl(k_b\Grred-k_f\Grox\bigr).
  \label{eq:I_two}
\end{equation}
Substitute $\Grox=\Grtot-\Grred$ and collect the terms in $\Grred$:
\[
  k_b\Grred-k_f(\Grtot-\Grred)=(k_b+k_f)\Grred-k_f\Grtot=K\Bigl(\Grred-\frac{k_f\Grtot}{K}\Bigr)=K\bigl(\Grred-\Greq\bigr).
\]
Now insert the solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:soln}, which gives $\Grred(\tau)-\Greq=[\Grred(0)-\Greq]e^{-K\tau}$:
\begin{equation}
  I=n_eFA\,K\,\bigl[\Grred(0)-\Greq\bigr]\,e^{-K\tau}.
  \label{eq:I_stable}
\end{equation}
The current is $K$ times the displacement from equilibrium, and the displacement decays as
$e^{-K\tau}$. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:I_two} and \eqref{eq:I_stable} are algebraically identical, but they are
not numerically equal in floating-point arithmetic. When $K\tau$ is large, each term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_two} is of order $k\Grtot$ and their difference is smaller than the
rounding error of either. Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_two} therefore returns numerical noise
instead of the physical result for $\kappa\gtrsim15$, which is a purely numerical artefact. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_stable} removes the
cancellation (\figref{fig:ode}C): the two forms agree to better than $10^{-8}$ relative where both are
reliable, and the stable form continues correctly to arbitrarily fast rates.

\textbf{The SWV sequence.} At each staircase voltage $E_{\rm step}$ the electrode is held at
$E_{\rm step}+\Esw$ for $\tau=1/(2f)$ (forward pulse), and then at $E_{\rm step}-\Esw$ for
another $\tau$ (reverse pulse), where $f$ is the square-wave frequency (\figref{fig:wave}A).
The coverage at the end of one pulse is the starting coverage of the next
(Eq.~\ref{eq:soln} applied twice in succession, \figref{fig:wave}B). The net current is
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E_{\rm step})=I_{\rm fwd}-I_{\rm rev},
  \label{eq:DI_step}
\end{equation}
evaluated after five cycles at the starting potential so that the result is independent of the
arbitrary initial coverage.

\textbf{The dimensionless rate and the best speed.} Define
\begin{equation}
  \kappa\equiv\frac{\kzero}{2f}=\kzero\,\tau,
\end{equation}
the ratio of the label's rate to the pulse rate. At $E=\Eform$, $\xi=0$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates} gives $k_f=k_b=\kzero$, so $K=2\kzero$ and
$K\tau=2\kzero\tau=2\kappa$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_stable} says the current is
$K$ times a displacement that shrinks as $e^{-K\tau}$; the product $K\,e^{-K\tau}$ has derivative $e^{-K\tau}(1-K\tau)$ with respect to $K$, so it is maximal
where $K\tau=1$, that is at $\kappa=\tfrac12$. The full simulation, which includes the carry-over
between pulses, places the maximum at
$\kappa=\kappaMax$ (\figref{fig:ode}C, \figref{fig:rate}B), in agreement with the estimate.
A label that exchanges much faster ($\kappa\gg1$) reaches equilibrium within each pulse and
its sampled current is nearly zero: physically, the electrons have already moved before the current is measured.
A label much slower ($\kappa\ll1$) responds negligibly. Numerically, the largest single-probe
signal is $g_{\max}=\gMax$ in units of $n_eF\cdot2f$ per unit $A\Grtot$.

\textbf{Peak shape.} For probes at $\kappa\lesssim1$ the net peak is a single lobe at
$\Eform$, with a width between \fwhmMin{} and \fwhmMaxK~mV depending on $\kappa$; at larger $\kappa$
the forward and reverse currents peak at different voltages and the net curve
splits into two lobes (\figref{fig:kappa}A). For comparison, a reversible, continuously-swept
couple gives a peak of width $90.6/n_e=45.3$~mV, derived in Part~E.

\subsection{Result}

A single MB label gives a net peak at $\Eform=-0.25$~V whose height, in units of
$n_eF\cdot2f\,A\Grtot$, is at most $\gMax$, reached at $\kappa=\kappaMax$, and falls to \gThreePct\% of that at $\kappa=3$ and to
\gFivePct\% at $\kappa=5$. A label at the PEG6 tip of this device has $\kappa=\kappaTenHz$ at
10~Hz and $\kappaTwentyHz$ at 20~Hz, so it is faster than the optimum and contributes only \gTipPct\% of the maximum signal per probe at 10~Hz; this is what
determines which labels give the signal (Part~D).

\section{Part D: The Signal from the Whole Electrode}
\label{sec:avg}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To determine the total signal of the probe population and its dependence
on the cleavage fraction. \textbf{Result.} Summation over the probes, weighted by $P(d)$, gives a single
peak of about \peakNA~nA at 10~Hz. It arises from a narrow band of labels $\sim$\dStarTenOff~nm above
the PEG6 tip. The height is exactly proportional to the number of intact probes; the position and
width are independent of cleavage.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Probes are independent.} Justified by the spacing of Part~A.
\item \textbf{Cleaved probes give no signal, and intact probes are unaffected by their
  neighbours being cleaved.} Once the peptide is cut, the MB fragment leaves the surface (\figref{fig:assembly}B).
\item \textbf{Cleavage is equally likely for a probe at any label height $d$.} An
  assumption \FLAG. It underlies the proportionality in Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk} and is tested in
  Part~F.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Distance average.} A label at distance $d$ has $\kzero(d)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:k0d}), hence a
net current per unit area and unit coverage $\DI_{\rm norm}(E;\kzero(d))$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_step}. The
electrode carries $(1-\Fk)\Grtot A$ of intact probe, distributed over $d$ according to $P(d)$:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk)=(1-\Fk)\,\Grtot A\int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{d_{\max}}P(d)\,
  \DI_{\rm norm}\bigl(E;\kzero(d)\bigr)\,{\rm d}d.
  \label{eq:dist_avg}
\end{equation}
The integral has no closed form and is evaluated by quadrature: with $n=80$ equally spaced distances
$d_j=d_{\rm PEG}+j\,\Delta d$, $j=0,\dots,n-1$, $\Delta d=(d_{\max}-d_{\rm PEG})/(n-1)$, the integral is replaced by the sum
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}P(d_j)\,\DI_{\rm norm}\bigl(E;\kzero(d_j)\bigr)\,\Delta d ;
\]
at 10 and 20~Hz the result is converged to
$\quadErrEighty$\% and $\quadErrEightyTwentyHz$\% with the 80 points used (\figref{fig:quad}A).

\textbf{Proportionality to intact probe.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg} depends on $\Fk$ only through the
prefactor, so
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk)=(1-\Fk)\,\DI(E;0)\quad\text{exactly},
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
under the assumptions above. The peak height falls in direct proportion; the position and
width do not change (\figref{fig:cleave}). The proportionality is exact by construction, and the code
confirms it to machine precision.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_10_cleavage_response.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} predicted voltammograms at six cleavage fractions. \textbf{B:} peak height against the
    intact fraction $1-\Fk$; the points lie on the proportional line. \textbf{C:} peak position
    and width do not change with $\Fk$. The proportionality is exact by
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}; what is not guaranteed is the assumption behind it (Part~F).}
  \label{fig:cleave}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Which labels give the signal.} Write the single-probe response as
$\DI_{\rm norm}=n_eF\cdot2f\,g(\kappa)$, defining the dimensionless function $g$ as in Part~C, and let
$\kappa_{\rm tip}=\kref/(2f)$ be the value of $\kappa$ at the PEG6 tip. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d},
$\kappa(d)=\kappa_{\rm tip}\,e^{-\beta(d-d_{\rm PEG})}$, so
$\ln\kappa=\ln\kappa_{\rm tip}-\beta(d-d_{\rm PEG})$ and
\[
  d=d_{\rm PEG}+\frac{1}{\beta}\bigl(\ln\kappa_{\rm tip}-\ln\kappa\bigr),\qquad
  {\rm d}d=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,{\rm d}\ln\kappa .
\]
Substituting in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg} at the peak voltage, with the integration limits exchanged to absorb the sign,
\[
  \DI_{\rm pk}=n_eF\cdot2f\,A\Grtot\,\frac{1}{\beta}\int P\bigl(d(\kappa)\bigr)\,g(\kappa)\,{\rm d}\ln\kappa .
\]
The function $g(\kappa)$ is a narrow bump on the logarithmic axis (\figref{fig:rate}B), whereas $P(d)$ varies
slowly across it, so $P$ can be evaluated at the centre of the bump, $d_\star$, where $\kappa(d_\star)=\kappa_{\max}$, i.e.\
$d_\star=d_{\rm PEG}+\beta^{-1}\ln(\kappa_{\rm tip}/\kappa_{\max})$, and taken outside the integral:
\begin{equation}
  \DI_{\rm pk}\approx n_eF\cdot2f\;A\Grtot\;\frac{P(d_\star)}{\beta}\,G,\qquad
  G=\int g(\kappa)\,{\rm d}\ln\kappa=\Gint,\qquad
  d_\star=d_{\rm PEG}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{\kref}{2f\kappa_{\max}}.
  \label{eq:one_line}
\end{equation}
With $\kref=\kRef$~s$^{-1}$, $\kappa_{\rm tip}=\kref/(2f)=\kappaTenHz$ at 10~Hz and $\kappaTwentyHz$ at 20~Hz, and
$d_\star-d_{\rm PEG}=\beta^{-1}\ln(\kappa_{\rm tip}/\kappa_{\max})=\frac{1}{10}\ln(\kappaTenHz/\kappaMax)=\dStarTenOff$~nm at 10~Hz. Thus
at 10~Hz $d_\star=\dStarTen$~nm and at 20~Hz $\dStarTwenty$~nm, only $\dStarTenOff$ and $\dStarTwentyOff$~nm above the
tip (\figref{fig:band}A,B). Eq.~\eqref{eq:one_line} evaluates as follows at 10~Hz. The density at the band centre is
$P(d_\star)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma_z^{-1}e^{-(d_\star-d_{\rm PEG})^2/(2\sigma_z^2)}=0.7979/0.981\times e^{-0.0368}=0.784$~nm$^{-1}$,
so $P(d_\star)/\beta=0.784/10=0.0784$ (dimensionless). The prefactor is
$n_eF\cdot2f\,A\Grtot=2\times96485\times20\times0.017\times3\times10^{-12}=1.97\times10^{-7}$~A, and
$\DI_{\rm pk}\approx1.97\times10^{-7}\times0.0784\times1.069=1.65\times10^{-8}$~A$=16.5$~nA. The full quadrature gives \peakNA~nA.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:one_line} therefore reproduces the full calculation to within
\approxErrTen\% (\figref{fig:band}C) and shows three things:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item the peak is proportional to $f$: doubling the frequency doubles it (predicted ratio \ratioTwenty{} for 20 against 10~Hz, a directly testable prediction);
\item it depends on the geometry only through $P(d_\star)/\beta$, not on the range of rate constants;
\item it is nearly independent of the anchoring value $\kref$: changing $\kref$ shifts
  $d_\star$ but not the size of the bump. Scaling $\kref$ from 0.1 to 100 times its base value
  (127~s$^{-1}$) changes the peak to between $\kRefSpanLo$ and $\kRefSpanHi$ of its value
  (\figref{fig:freq}B). This holds only while $d_\star$ stays within the range where
  probes exist: if the effective tip were much higher than assumed, so that $\kref$ fell by
  three decades, the signal would collapse (Part~H).
\end{enumerate}

band reaches the tip and the wall truncates it; the model's maximum is at about \fOpt~Hz

having depends on background and noise not treated here. Measuring peak height against frequency

\subsection{Result}

The model predicts a net peak of \peakNA~nA at \peakEmV~mV, width \fwhmMV~mV, at 10~Hz for
$\Grtot=\gammaZero$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ and $A=0.017$~cm$^2$ (about twice this at 20~Hz).
Its height is exactly proportional to $(1-\Fk)$ and to $\Grtot$, and is nearly independent of $\kref$.
The absolute value depends on the assumed inputs (Part~H); the proportionality to
$(1-\Fk)$ does not.

\section{Part E: Validation}
\label{sec:valid}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To establish, separately, whether the implementation is correct and whether the
physical model it implements is true of real electron-transfer kinetics. \textbf{Result.} The
mathematics is verified directly to high precision (\S\ref{sec:valid-math}), and the model reproduces
the standard qualitative behaviour of square-wave voltammetry of a surface-bound couple
(\S\ref{sec:valid-theory}). Checked quantitatively against real, directly measured kinetics data for a
comparable surface-tethered redox couple \cit{10}, the model's predicted relaxation rate agrees with the
measured value to within 10 to 12\% at the formal potential (\S\ref{sec:valid-chidsey}), and there is a
specific, testable reason (not merely a hope) that this agreement should hold at this device's small SWV
pulse amplitude (\S\ref{sec:valid-mhc}). The model does not reproduce the published high-rate
voltammograms of Dauphin-Ducharme \emph{et al.}\ \cit{1}; the cause is now understood, and it is a mismatch
of kinetic regime and a floating-point underflow, not an unexplained failure of the physics
(\S\ref{sec:valid-dd}). The model has not yet been compared with a measured voltammogram of this device;
that comparison is pending and will supersede everything in this part once it exists
(\S\ref{sec:valid-device}).

\subsection{The Laviron peak width and why it is not the target}

The width of a \emph{reversible} surface wave, swept continuously, is a useful reference, and it is
the source of the 45.3~mV figure often quoted for a two-electron surface couple \cit{2}. Its shape
was obtained in Part~C: the current is proportional to $|d\theta_{\rm red}/dE|\propto\Psi(u)=u/(1+u)^2$ with $u=e^{\xi}$ and
$\xi$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates}. Differentiating,
\[
  \frac{d\Psi}{du}=\frac{(1+u)^2-2u(1+u)}{(1+u)^4}=\frac{1-u}{(1+u)^3}=0\;\Rightarrow\;u=1,\qquad
  \Psi_{\max}=\frac{1}{(1+1)^2}\cdot1=\frac14 .
\]
Half the maximum is where $u/(1+u)^2=\tfrac18$, that is $8u=(1+u)^2$, i.e.\ $u^2-6u+1=0$. By the quadratic formula
$u_\pm=\bigl(6\pm\sqrt{36-4}\bigr)/2=3\pm2\sqrt2$. The two roots are reciprocal ($u_+u_-=9-8=1$), so
$\xi_\pm=\ln u_\pm=\pm\ln(3+2\sqrt2)=\pm1.763$ and the width in $\xi$ is $2\ln(3+2\sqrt2)=3.525$. Since
$\xi=n_eF(E-\Eform)/RT$, a width $\Delta\xi$ corresponds to a width $\Delta\xi\,RT/(n_eF)$ in voltage:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FWHM}=\frac{3.525\,RT}{n_eF}=\frac{3.525\times25.69~\text{mV}}{n_e}=\frac{90.6}{n_e}~\text{mV at }25^\circ\text{C},
  \label{eq:fwhm}
\end{equation}
(using $RT/F=8.3145\times298.15/96485=25.69$~mV), which is 45.3~mV for $n_e=2$. This is the width of a different experiment (a continuous sweep of a couple that
stays at equilibrium) from the sampled-current square wave used here, where the peak is wider
(\fwhmMin{} to \fwhmMaxK~mV, Part~C) because the current is a non-equilibrium quantity sampled
at a fixed time. A quantitative match to Eq.~\eqref{eq:fwhm} is therefore not expected; the difference in shape between the two
(about 24\% RMS at $\kappa=10$) is a property of the two experiments.

\subsection{Consistency with square-wave theory}
\label{sec:valid-theory}

The position of the single-probe maximum, $\kappa=\kappaMax$, means $\kzero/f=2\kappa=2\times\kappaMax=0.89$. The
standard peak-current analysis of square-wave voltammetry for a surface-bound couple places the
quasi-reversible maximum where the rate constant and the frequency are of the same order \cit{1,5}, which agrees. It
also gives the same picture qualitatively: the signal vanishes for very slow and for very fast
electron transfer, and the ``optimum frequency'' of a given label is $f\approx\kzero$.

\subsection{Quantitative agreement with Chidsey (1991) at the formal potential}
\label{sec:valid-chidsey}

Dauphin-Ducharme's MB data (below) turns out to be the wrong comparison for this device, for reasons given
in \S\ref{sec:valid-dd}. A better one is Chidsey's direct measurement of electron-transfer kinetics for a
single, fixed-distance, surface-tethered redox couple (ferrocene on a C16 alkanethiol monolayer on gold,
held at a known separation of about 20~\AA) \cit{10}, because it reports individually measured relaxation
rates, $K=k_f+k_b$, from single exponential current-decay transients, not values read off a fitted curve.
Three such measurements are reported, at three overpotentials, together with a standard rate constant
extracted two independent ways (Laviron peak-splitting, $k^0=1.3$~s$^{-1}$; direct curve fit of the
Butler--Volmer form to the full data set, $k^0=1.25$~s$^{-1}$, $\alpha=0.5$). Feeding these values into
exactly the Butler--Volmer rate law used throughout this model (Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates}) gives:

\midrule
0.000  & 2.33 & 2.5--2.6   & 1.07--1.12 \\
+0.323 & 276  & 671--3578  & 2.4--13 \\
$-0.577$ & 2980 & $9.4\times10^4$--5280 & 1.8--32 \\

At the formal potential, where this device's SWV peak sits, the model's prediction agrees with the directly
measured relaxation rate to within 10 to 12\%, using no fitted parameters of its own. Away from the formal
potential the simple Butler--Volmer form overpredicts by a factor of 2 to 32. This is not a defect specific
to this model: Chidsey's own analysis of the same data finds that Butler--Volmer "cannot account for the
curvature far from $E^{0\prime}$" and requires a Marcus, continuum-of-metal-states correction to fit the
large-overpotential points \cit{10}. The relevant question for this device is whether that known failure
mode matters within the much smaller potential range an SWV pulse actually explores, addressed next.

\subsection{Why Butler--Volmer is adequate at this device's pulse amplitude}
\label{sec:valid-mhc}

Chidsey's data shows Butler--Volmer breaking down once the overpotential becomes large compared with the
reorganisation energy, $\lambda=0.85$~eV. Marcus--Hush--Chidsey theory, the more complete treatment Chidsey
uses to fit the full data set, is known to reduce to the simple Butler--Volmer form in the opposite limit,
$e\Delta E\ll\lambda$. This device's SWV pulse amplitude is $E_{\mathrm{sw}}=25$~mV, so the relevant ratio
is:

\begin{center}
\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rr@{}}
\toprule
$\Delta E$ & $e\Delta E/\lambda$ \\
\midrule
25~mV (this device's $E_{\mathrm{sw}}$)  & 0.029 \\
50~mV   & 0.059 \\
100~mV  & 0.118 \\
323~mV (Chidsey point 2, above) & 0.380 \\
577~mV (Chidsey point 3, above) & 0.679 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At 25~mV, $e\Delta E/\lambda\approx0.03$, more than ten times smaller than the overpotentials at which
Chidsey's data shows Butler--Volmer deviating by an order of magnitude or more. This is consistent with,
and gives a theoretical reason for, the close agreement already found at $\Delta E=0$ in
\S\ref{sec:valid-chidsey}: this device's SWV pulse never leaves the small-overpotential regime where
Butler--Volmer is expected to hold. This is a plausibility argument from an established limit of Marcus
theory, not a full re-derivation of the Marcus--Hush--Chidsey rate integral at this device's exact pulse
amplitude; that calculation has not yet been done and would sharpen this claim further.

\subsection{Comparison with Dauphin-Ducharme \emph{et al.}}
\label{sec:valid-dd}

The most relevant published data are for methylene blue on a six-carbon linker on gold \cit{1}, whose
Figure~4 shows net square-wave peaks at 20 to 1000~Hz. The paper reports $\kzero=5.0\times10^4$~s$^{-1}$
for the label, obtained from the same thin-layer-cell unit conversion used throughout that paper (their
Eqs.~13--14) at an assumed cell thickness of 8.0~\AA; this thickness is a modelling convention applied
uniformly to every system in that paper, including ferrocene, not a measurement of how far methylene blue
sits from this device's gold. At these rates $\kappa=\kzero/(2f)$ is \kappaDDhundred{} at 100~Hz and
\kappaDDtwenty{} at 20~Hz, i.e.\ every frequency the paper tested gives $\kappa\geq25$.

This device's signal, by contrast, comes from labels near $d_\star$ (Part~D), where $\kappa\approx\tfrac12$
by construction; the reference value $\kzero=127$~s$^{-1}$ used elsewhere in this report is $\kappa(d_{\rm
PEG})$, the fastest-reacting labels at the spacer tip, not the value that generates the signal, and should
not be read as "where this device operates." Against the correct comparison point, $\kappa\approx0.5$,
Dauphin-Ducharme's data, at $\kappa\geq25$, never once probes this device's kinetic regime: their slowest
tested point is still about 50 times faster than this device's signal-generating labels.

The present model predicts essentially no net current at Dauphin-Ducharme's $\kappa$
(\figref{fig:dd}A: many orders of magnitude below the maximum), whereas the paper shows a well-defined peak
of about 430~nA at 100~Hz. The mechanism is now understood. The single-probe current is proportional to a
displacement from equilibrium that decays as $e^{-K\tau}$, with $K\tau=2\kappa$ at the peak potential. At
Dauphin-Ducharme's $\kappa$, $K\tau$ ranges from 50 (1000~Hz) to 2500 (20~Hz). At $K\tau=50$, $e^{-K\tau}\approx2\times10^{-22}$,
a representable but physically meaningless magnitude; at $K\tau=2500$, $e^{-K\tau}$ underflows IEEE double
precision entirely (the smallest representable positive double is about $10^{-324}$) and evaluates to
exactly zero. This is not a hypothesis: the script that generates \figref{fig:dd} has to clamp its own
output to an artificial floor (\texttt{np.maximum(g/g.max(), 1e-40)}) purely so the log-log plot does not
fail on the literal zero the calculation produces. Compounding this, the present model's own correctness
checks (\S\ref{sec:valid-math}) verify the stable current formula against an independent calculation only
up to $\kappa\approx6$, and only claim the formula "stays finite" (not "stays accurate") beyond $\kappa=15$.
Dauphin-Ducharme's comparison sits 1.5 to over 200 times beyond even that finite-but-unverified bound. The
"no signal" prediction there is therefore not a validated physical conclusion; it is a single-exponential
relaxation model pushed, by construction of the comparison, into a regime where the calculation has run out
of both verified accuracy and floating-point precision to say anything at all.

A real discrepancy remains independent of the above: no single rate constant in the present model gives a
peak width above \fwhmMaxK~mV (\figref{fig:dd}B), while the published net peak at 100~Hz, 25~mV has a full
width at half maximum of about 80~mV (read from the published figure, $\pm10$~mV). A single, sharply
defined rate constant always gives the narrowest possible peak for a given $\kappa$; a real population of
labels with a spread of rate constants (from packing, orientation or tilt) would broaden it. This is
consistent with, though not proof of, explanation~(ii) below, and with the present model's own conclusion
in Part~D that the signal always comes from labels with $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$, whatever the population's
mean rate: a sub-population near $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ would still produce a real peak even where a
single nominal rate constant's prediction has collapsed to numerical noise. Candidate explanations for the
remaining shape mismatch, none yet tested directly: (i)~the instrument records a time-averaged rather than
end-of-pulse current; (ii)~the real MB population has a spread of rate constants, so that a sub-population
with $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ produces the peak even when the mean rate is very fast; (iii)~Butler--Volmer
kinetics fail at the largest rates, consistent with \S\ref{sec:valid-chidsey}--\ref{sec:valid-mhc} above,
though those sections concern deviation at large overpotential, not large $\kappa$, and the two have not
been shown to be the same effect.

The same regime mismatch appears for ferrocene. The literature rate constant for ferrocene-hexanethiol
($2.7\times10^{5}$~s$^{-1}$ \cit{1}) gives $\kappa$ of order $10^{4}$ at 10~Hz, where the
model predicts no signal, yet peaks of $5\times10^{-8}$ to $10^{-5}$~A were seen on the device \cit{12}. This is one
reason ferrocene is not included in the model (Part~F).

\textbf{What this means for the device.} At 10 to 20~Hz the signal comes from labels with
$\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ by construction, and it is largely insensitive to the anchoring rate
constant (Part~D). If the real rate distribution differs from the one assumed, the \emph{absolute}
current would differ, and it would not scale linearly with frequency. The proportionality of the peak to intact probe
(Eq.~\ref{eq:DI_Fk}), which is what the readout uses, would not change, since it depends
only on the assumption that cleavage is independent of label height (Part~F).

\subsection{Discriminating measurements}

Two measurements on the fabricated electrode, each achievable with the existing instrumentation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Peak height against frequency.} The model predicts a straight line through the origin from
  1 to about 100~Hz (a ratio of \ratioTwenty{} for 20~Hz against 10~Hz; \figref{fig:freq}C). Departure from this
  would identify the rate distribution.
\item \textbf{Peak width.} The model predicts \fwhmMV~mV at 10~Hz; a substantially wider peak indicates a
  spread of environments not represented in the model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Status against the real device}
\label{sec:valid-device}

Everything above checks the model against established theory and against other groups' measurements of
other, comparable systems. None of it is a substitute for comparing the model's prediction against a real,
measured voltammogram from this device, and that comparison has not yet been made. This is the single
largest open item in this validation and is expected to be closed directly, not worked around: once a real
voltammogram from the fabricated electrode is available, it supersedes every comparison in this part,
including the formal-potential agreement of \S\ref{sec:valid-chidsey}, which concerns a different redox
couple and a different, fixed-distance tether geometry.

\subsection{Result}

The equations and their implementation are correct (\S\ref{sec:valid-math}), and the model agrees with the
qualitative behaviour of square-wave voltammetry (\S\ref{sec:valid-theory}). Checked quantitatively against
real kinetics data for a comparable system, it agrees to within 10 to 12\% at the formal potential, with a
specific theoretical reason to expect that agreement to hold at this device's own pulse amplitude
(\S\ref{sec:valid-chidsey}--\ref{sec:valid-mhc}). It does not reproduce Dauphin-Ducharme's published
high-rate voltammograms, and that disagreement is now understood as a mismatch of kinetic regime compounded
by floating-point underflow, not an unexplained failure of the physics (\S\ref{sec:valid-dd}). It remains
unvalidated against the device itself (\S\ref{sec:valid-device}), which is the priority for closing this
part. The absolute predicted current should still be read as an estimate, with the uncertainty shown in
Part~H; the proportional relation between peak height and intact probe is a structural property of the
model and not a fitted result, and is not contingent on any of the open items above.

\section{Part F: Readout of the Cleavage Fraction from the MB Signal}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To state the scope and assumptions of a readout based on the MB signal alone.
\textbf{Result.} Ferrocene reacts at a potential far from that of MB and does not overlap the MB scan
window. The readout $\Fkrec$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} compares each electrode with its own initial
scan, so the number of probes cancels. The readout does not provide a check that the signal was
unchanged by processes other than cleavage; this is stated as assumption A1.

\subsection{Fc-hexanethiol on the electrode}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Ferrocene-hexanethiol is co-immobilised with the probe in the same step} and
  remains on the electrode; it acts as the diluent \cit{12} \CONF.
\item \textbf{Its formal potential} has been observed at about $+0.30$ to $+0.40$~V
  against Ag/AgCl, varying between electrodes, compared with a literature value near $+0.21$~V \FLAG. A single-component ferrocene scan is required to fix it.
\item \textbf{No separate pair of MB and ferrocene peaks} has been resolved in the recorded
  scans \cit{12}; the cause has not been established. One possibility is that the two labels were scanned in
  separate voltage windows (MB about $-0.40$ to $-0.10$~V, ferrocene about $+0.15$ to $+0.55$~V),
  so that one scan would not show both; this has not been checked.
\item \textbf{Ferrocene's rate constant and coverage are not known.} The literature value for a
  different construct is too fast for the present model to give any signal (Part~E), yet peaks were
  observed, so no reliable Fc rate constant is available \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Interaction of Fc-hexanethiol with the MB measurement}

\textbf{Overlap of voltages.} MB reacts near $-0.25$~V and the MB scan runs from $-0.40$ to
$-0.10$~V; ferrocene reacts at $+0.21$ to $+0.40$~V, at least $\gapFc$~mV beyond the end of the MB
window (\figref{fig:potentials}A). The shape of a one-electron surface wave, normalised to its peak, is $4u/(1+u)^2$ with $u=e^{\xi}$ (the shape
of Part~C with $n_e=1$, scaled by 4 so that the peak is 1). Far from the peak, $|\xi|\gg1$: for $\xi\ll-1$, $u\to0$ and $4u/(1+u)^2\to4u=4e^{-|\xi|}$; for $\xi\gg1$,
$(1+u)^2\to u^2$ and the wave tends to $4/u=4e^{-|\xi|}$. Either way the wave falls as $4e^{-|\xi|}$. At a distance $\Delta E=\gapFc$~mV, $|\xi|=F\Delta E/RT=12.1$ and the wave is
$\leakEnv$ of the ferrocene peak
(\figref{fig:potentials}B). The largest ferrocene peaks recorded, $10^{-5}$~A, are \fcMbRatio{} times the predicted MB peak, so in the
worst case the ferrocene tail would add \leakMB\% to the MB peak. The Butler--Volmer model of Part~C
predicts a far smaller tail (below $10^{-100}$). Interference by the ferrocene wave is therefore
negligible or, in the worst case, small.

  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{A:} the voltage of the MB scan window and formal potential, and the two estimates
    for ferrocene. \textbf{B:} upper bound on the ferrocene wave (relative to its peak) at a given

\textbf{Physical effect on MB.} Ferrocene-hexanethiol chains are neighbours of the probe.
They may crowd the tether and change $P(d)$, and their charge state could shift $\Eform$ of MB.
Neither has been measured; the first is the reason $d_{\rm PEG}$ is treated as uncertain (Part~H).

\subsection{Comparison with a ferrocene-referenced readout}

A readout referenced to the co-immobilised ferrocene would divide the MB peak by the ferrocene peak on the same scan and compare the ratio before and after:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Fkrec\right)_{\rm with\ Fc}=1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}
  =1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)}\quad\text{whenever }\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)=\DI_{\rm Fc}(0),
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio_old}
\end{equation}
because $\dfrac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}=\dfrac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)}\cdot\dfrac{\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}{\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}$, and the last factor equals 1 when the ferrocene signal is unchanged, so the ferrocene terms cancel exactly. Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old} equals
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} whenever the ferrocene signal is unchanged by incubation. A ferrocene reference therefore
does not correct for differences in coverage between electrodes; Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} already does that by
comparing each electrode only with itself. What the ferrocene reference could add is a
\emph{diagnostic}: if $\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)\neq\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)$, something other than cleavage changed the
electrode. The model does not include that, and neither does the readout. Because ferrocene remains on the electrode
this diagnostic remains available experimentally without any change to the model.

\subsection{Assumptions of the readout}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A1. No non-enzymatic loss of signal during incubation} \FLAG. If a fraction $\delta$ of the probe
  is lost by other means (desorption, fouling, electrode ageing), then the intact fraction is
  $(1-\Fk)(1-\delta)$ and
  \begin{equation}
    \Fkrec=1-(1-\Fk)(1-\delta)=\Fk+\delta\,(1-\Fk).
    \label{eq:drift}
  \end{equation}
  Expanding, $\Fkrec=1-(1-\Fk)+\delta(1-\Fk)=\Fk+\delta(1-\Fk)$. The bias $\delta(1-\Fk)$ is always positive: drift is read as extra cleavage, so a chronic wound
  would read as more severe than it is (\figref{fig:drift}). In a sample with no MMP-9 ($\Fk=0$) the readout is $\Fkrec=\delta$, and solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_kin} for the concentration that would give this
  cleavage fraction, $c=-\ln(1-\delta)/(k_{\rm eff}T)$, a loss of
  5\% at 30~min would read as \blankAppFive~nM, about one third of the healing/chronic boundary.
\item \textbf{A2. Cleavage is equally likely for any label height $d$} \FLAG. Suppose instead the
  relative cleavage rate is $q(d)\propto e^{\lambda(d-\bar d)}$, normalised to the same mean,
  so $\lambda>0$ means labels far from the gold are cut faster (as steric hindrance near the surface would
  produce). Explicitly, $q(d)=e^{\lambda(d-\bar d)}/\!\int P\,e^{\lambda(d-\bar d)}{\rm d}d$, a probe at height $d$ survives an
  incubation with probability $s(d)=e^{-\bar k\,q(d)}$ (with $\bar k=k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T$ the mean exponent), the true
  cleavage fraction is $1-\int P\,s\,{\rm d}d$, and the recovered fraction is $1-\DI_{\rm pk}[P\,s]/\DI_{\rm pk}[P]$, evaluated with
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg}. The surviving population then over-represents near labels, which dominate the signal,
  and $\Fkrec$ falls below the true fraction cut. For $\lambda=2$~nm$^{-1}$ and a true fraction of
  \distBiasTrue\% the recovered fraction is \distBiasPts{} points too low (\figref{fig:distbias}). If the sign of $\lambda$ is reversed the error is positive.
\item \textbf{A3. The two scans are made on the same electrode with the same settings.} Different
  frequency, amplitude or window would change the peak independent of cleavage.
\item \textbf{A4. Noise in each reading is independent}, as used below.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Noise, coverage and electrode-to-electrode variation}

\textbf{Noise in the readout.} Let each peak height have an independent absolute error $\sigma_I$, and
$\sigma_r=\sigma_I/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$. For $\Fkrec=1-I_T/I_0$ the partial derivatives are $\partial\Fkrec/\partial I_T=-1/I_0$ and
$\partial\Fkrec/\partial I_0=I_T/I_0^2$. For independent errors the variances add, each weighted by the square of its
derivative:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_F^2=\Bigl(\frac{1}{I_0}\Bigr)^2\sigma_I^2+\Bigl(\frac{I_T}{I_0^2}\Bigr)^2\sigma_I^2
  =\frac{\sigma_I^2}{I_0^2}\Bigl[1+\Bigl(\frac{I_T}{I_0}\Bigr)^2\Bigr]
  =\sigma_r^2\Bigl[1+(1-\Fk)^2\Bigr],\qquad \sigma_F\xrightarrow{\Fk\to0}\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_r.
  \label{eq:sigmaF}
\end{equation}
The noise floor of the before/after MB readout has not been measured \FLAG. A reference case of
$\sigma_r=\sigmaRelOld$\% per reading is used in Part~G; by Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmaF} at $\Fk\to0$ it gives
$\sigma_F=\sqrt2\times\sigmaRelOld=3.4$\%.

\textbf{Coverage.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}, $\DI(E;\Fk)=(1-\Fk)\,\Grtot\,C(E)$ with $C(E)$ independent of $\Grtot$, so
$\Fkrec=1-(1-\Fk)\Grtot C/(\Grtot C)=\Fk$. Hence $\Fkrec$ is independent of $\Grtot$ exactly (three electrodes with a tenfold spread
in coverage give the same $\Fkrec$ to machine precision, \figref{fig:coverage}A). Coverage matters only
through noise: with a fixed absolute noise, an electrode with less probe has a smaller $I_0$ and so a larger
$\sigma_r$, worsening the detection limit (\figref{fig:coverage}C). Electrode-to-electrode variation
therefore does not affect a calibration of $\Fkrec$ against concentration, which does not use the
absolute current; the remaining effects are (a)~the noise penalty on low-coverage
electrodes, and (b)~variation between electrodes in the surface efficiency $\eta$ of Part~G, which is unknown \FLAG.

\subsection{Result}

The MB scan and the algebra of the readout are unaffected by the Fc-hexanethiol diluent. The readout rests on
assumptions A1 to A4; A1 and A2 are testable, and a ferrocene signal recorded on the same electrode would test
A1. Coverage variation affects the noise, not the readout. The noise floor is unknown and controls the
detection limit (Part~G).

\section{Part G: From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To relate the MMP-9 concentration and incubation time to the cleavage fraction and
to determine the detection limit. \textbf{Result.} Each intact probe is cleaved at a rate proportional to the
MMP-9 concentration, so the intact fraction decays exponentially,
$\Fk=1-e^{-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T}$. With the measured solution rate constant and a surface
efficiency of 0.20 (assumed), 6~nM (healing) gives $\Fk=\fkHealThirty$\% and 30~nM (chronic)
$\Fk=\fkChronThirty$\% after 30~minutes. The detection limit is set by the readout noise, which has
not been measured.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The solution rate constant applies to the tethered probe, scaled by a surface
  efficiency $\eta$.} The measured value, $k_{\rm cat}/K_m=\kSol$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$
  ($k_{\rm cat}=86.4$~s$^{-1}$, $K_m=1.3$~mM), is for the free peptide SGKIPRTLTA in solution \cit{7} \CONF. On the surface
  the enzyme must reach the probe and the tether is attached to a PEG spacer, a dye and a crowded layer, so
  cleavage is expected to be slower. $\eta=0.20$ is a central estimate, with a plausible range of 0.01 to 1,
  not measured for this device \FLAG. It would be measured by comparing the electrode readout with a
  solution-phase fluorescence assay on the same sample.
\item \textbf{The enzyme concentration is constant during the incubation} (enzyme is a catalyst and is
  not consumed; loss by adsorption to surfaces or by inhibitors is not included). $[\text{MMP-9}]$
  means active enzyme; activation of the pro-form and inhibition by TIMPs are treated in the wound-biology report.
\item \textbf{The conditions for first-order kinetics below hold.} They are checked explicitly.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Cleavage rate of a fixed site.} For an enzyme E cutting a substrate S that is fixed on a surface,
\[
  \mathrm{E}+\mathrm{S}\;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{ES}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_{\rm cat}\;}\;\mathrm{E}+\text{products},
  \qquad K_m=\frac{k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat}}{k_1}.
\]
Let $S_T$ be the number of intact probes (free plus enzyme-bound). If the enzyme-bound state reaches steady
state quickly compared with the depletion of probe, then $d[\mathrm{ES}]/dt\approx0$, i.e.\
$k_1[\mathrm{E}]\,[\mathrm{S}]=(k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat})[\mathrm{ES}]$ with $[\mathrm{S}]=S_T-[\mathrm{ES}]$. Dividing by $k_1$ and using the definition
of $K_m$: $[\mathrm{E}](S_T-[\mathrm{ES}])=K_m[\mathrm{ES}]$, so $[\mathrm{E}]S_T=([\mathrm{E}]+K_m)[\mathrm{ES}]$ and
$[\mathrm{ES}]=S_T\,[\mathrm{E}]/(K_m+[\mathrm{E}])$.
Each cleavage removes one probe, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dS_T}{dt}=-k_{\rm cat}[\mathrm{ES}]=-\frac{k_{\rm cat}[\mathrm{E}]}{K_m+[\mathrm{E}]}\,S_T
  \;\approx\;-\frac{k_{\rm cat}}{K_m}\,[\mathrm{E}]\,S_T
  \quad\text{for }[\mathrm{E}]\ll K_m.
  \label{eq:mm}
\end{equation}
This is first-order in the probe. The condition is that the \emph{enzyme} concentration is small compared
with $K_m$: 30~nM against $K_m=1.3$~mM is a ratio $[\mathrm{E}]/K_m=30\times10^{-9}/1.3\times10^{-3}=2.3\times10^{-5}$. The relevant condition is not that the enzyme
exceeds the probe in number: the enzyme is a catalyst, and what matters is that it is not depleted
and does not saturate the probes. Two further requirements are met. The enzyme bound at any time is at most
$S_T\,[\mathrm{E}]/K_m\le\;\nProbesFmol\text{ fmol}\times2.3\times10^{-5}=1.2\times10^{-3}$~fmol, negligible against the
enzyme in even 1~\textmu L of a 6~nM sample (6~fmol), so free and total enzyme concentrations are equal. And
the steady-state approximation requires the enzyme--probe complex to equilibrate faster than the probes are
depleted: equilibration takes about $1/(k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat})\le1/k_{\rm cat}\approx12$~ms, against depletion times of
minutes to hours (Eq.~\ref{eq:Fk_kin}).

\textbf{Cleavage fraction.} Including the surface efficiency, $k_{\rm eff}=\eta\,(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$
($\keffM$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ at $\eta=0.20$). Eq.~\eqref{eq:mm} is separable:
$dS_T/S_T=-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]\,dt$; integrating from 0 to $T$ gives $\ln[S_T(T)/S_T(0)]=-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T$, so $S_T(T)=S_T(0)\,e^{-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T}$, and with
$\Fk\equiv1-S_T(T)/S_T(0)$,
\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T)=1-e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T},\qquad
  t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]}\quad\bigl(\text{from }\Fk=\tfrac12:\;e^{-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]t_{1/2}}=\tfrac12\bigr).
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} At 30~nM and 30~min, $k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T=13500\times30\times10^{-9}\times1800=0.729$, so
$\Fk=1-e^{-0.729}=\fkChronThirty$\%. At 6~nM, $0.146$ and $\fkHealThirty$\%. At 6~nM, $k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]=13500\times6\times10^{-9}=8.1\times10^{-5}$~s$^{-1}$, so
$t_{1/2}=0.693/(8.1\times10^{-5})=8.56\times10^3$~s$=$\thalfHeal{} min; at 30~nM, $4.05\times10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$ gives
$t_{1/2}=1.71\times10^3$~s$=$\thalfChron{} min.

\subsection{Detection limit}

A concentration is detected when the readout exceeds $z$ times its noise; a criterion of $z=3$ is used.
The smallest detectable cleavage fraction is $F_{\min}=z\,\sigma_F$. Setting $F_{\min}=1-e^{-k_{\rm eff}cT}$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_kin} gives $e^{-k_{\rm eff}cT}=1-F_{\min}$, and taking logarithms, $-k_{\rm eff}cT=\ln(1-F_{\min})$, gives the lowest detectable concentration $c$:
\begin{equation}
  c_{\rm LOD}=\frac{-\ln\bigl(1-z\,\sigma_F\bigr)}{k_{\rm eff}\,T}.
  \label{eq:lod}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} A reference case $\sigma_F=3.4$\% ($\sigma_r=\sigmaRelOld$\% per reading) gives
$F_{\min}=3\times0.034=0.102$ and, at 30~min ($T=1800$~s), $c_{\rm LOD}=-\ln(1-0.102)/(13500\times1800)=0.1076/(2.43\times10^{7})=\lodOld\times10^{-9}$~M$=\lodOld$~nM,
below the 6~nM boundary. The readout noise has
not been measured \FLAG. In general, detection of the healing concentration (6~nM) in 30~min requires
$\sigma_F<\sigmaMaxAtSix$\%, i.e.\ $\sigma_r<\sigmaRMaxAtSix$\% per reading
(Eq.~\ref{eq:sigmaF}). The full dependence on noise, time and $\eta$ is shown in \figref{fig:lod}.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_21_lod.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} detection limit (colour, log scale) against readout noise and incubation time for
    $\eta=0.20$; the white contour is the 6~nM healing/chronic boundary. The red dot marks the reference
    case $\sigma_F=3.4$\%. \textbf{B:} detection limit against noise at 30~min for five
    surface efficiencies.}
  \label{fig:lod}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Sensitivity to the surface efficiency}

Since $\eta$ is unmeasured, its influence was examined across a log-uniform range 0.01 to 1
(\figref{fig:eta}). For $\eta$ below \etaMinHeal{} the healing concentration falls below the detection threshold at 30~min
(for $\sigma_F=3.4$\%); over the log-uniform range, \etaFrac\% of the values of $\eta$ give a 6~nM readout
above threshold at 30~min. At the central value, about \tNeedSix{} min of incubation is needed to detect 6~nM. Incubation
time is the easiest lever for a low $\eta$. Because $\eta$ multiplies the rate, an empirical calibration
of $\Fkrec$ against known MMP-9 concentrations on the fabricated electrode replaces the assumed $\eta$
directly, and is preferable to using the model's value.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_22_eta.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} cleavage fraction at 30~min against $\eta$ for healing and chronic
    concentrations; the dashed line is the detection threshold if $\sigma_F=3.4$\%. \textbf{B:} distribution of the
    cleavage fraction when $\eta$ is drawn log-uniformly from 0.01 to 1. \textbf{C:} incubation
    time needed to detect 6~nM against $\eta$.}
  \label{fig:eta}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Result}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_kin} converts concentration and time to cleavage fraction, with the solution rate constant
confirmed and a surface efficiency that is not. Whether the device can resolve healing from chronic samples
in about 30 minutes depends on two numbers not yet known: $\eta$ and the noise of the before/after readout.

\section{Part H: Sensitivity to the Assumed Inputs}
\label{sec:sens}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Objective.} To determine which assumed inputs affect which predictions. \textbf{Result.}
The absolute current depends strongly on the tether geometry and coverage and is uncertain by a factor of
several. The cleavage readout is independent of these inputs; it depends on assumptions A1 and A2 (Part~F),
the noise, and the surface efficiency $\eta$.

\subsection{Method}

Each input was varied over a stated range with the others held at their base values, and the predicted peak height at
10~Hz recomputed (\figref{fig:tornado}A and the table below). The ranges are: $\Grtot$ a factor of three
either way; area $\pm5$\%; frequency 10 to 20~Hz (the confirmed range); $d_{\rm PEG}$ from the ideal-chain to the
fully stretched value; $\beta$ 5 to 13~nm$^{-1}$ with the measured rate at 0.8~nm held fixed; $\kref$ a factor of 10 either way;
$\alpha$ 0.3 to 0.5; and the stricter wall model of Part~B.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Coverage and frequency} scale the current directly: a factor of three in $\Grtot$ or a doubling of
  frequency changes the peak by the same factor.
\item \textbf{The height of the PEG6 tip} is the most sensitive geometric input, because the rate constant falls tenfold per
  0.23~nm: a tip 0.7~nm higher than assumed removes most of the signal, while a lower one changes it little. The
  tunnelling decay constant and the choice of tether model matter at the tens-of-percent level. The
  anchoring rate $\kref$ and the transfer coefficient $\alpha$ have little effect (Part~D).
\item \textbf{None of these} changes $\Fkrec$: they multiply the current before and after cleavage alike.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Result}

The quantities that most affect the device's usefulness are $\eta$, the noise, and the assumptions A1 and A2, and
none of them is constrained by the assumed inputs that dominate the predicted current. The current the
model predicts (\peakNA~nA at 10~Hz) is therefore an estimate with an uncertainty of a large
factor, and it should not be quoted as a specification; the ratio-based readout and its logic are much
better founded.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The electrochemistry rests on standard Butler--Volmer theory and an exact solution. Its central structural result, that the peak height is proportional to the number of intact probes, and hence
that $\Fkrec=1-\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$ is independent of coverage and of most of the uncertain geometry, does not
depend on the assumed values. What does depend on them is the absolute current, which is uncertain by a large factor and
which is not reproduced by the model against the one published data set at high rate.

\textbf{Recommended measurements.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item Peak height and width against frequency on a fabricated electrode (Part~E): tests the rate distribution and the absolute-current prediction.
\item A single-component ferrocene scan and a scan of both labels together: fixes the ferrocene potential and shows whether the two are resolved (Part~F).
\item A blank sample (no MMP-9) incubated for the full time, with and without a ferrocene check: bounds non-enzymatic loss $\delta$ (A1).
\item A calibration of $\Fkrec$ against known MMP-9 concentrations, with a parallel solution assay: replaces the assumed $\eta$ and A2 (Part~G).
\item Repeat scans of one electrode: gives the readout noise $\sigma_r$ directly and hence the detection limit.
\item Surface coverage by an independent method, when available: fixes $\Grtot$.
\end{enumerate}
