\noindent\textbf{Scope.}
This document presents the forward model that predicts what the electrode
records when a wound sample containing MMP-9 is placed on it: the chain from
enzyme concentration, through the fraction of surface probes that are cut, to
the height of a peak in a square-wave voltammogram. It is written for a reader
who knows GCSE-level biology, chemistry, physics and maths, and no
electrochemistry. Every term is defined where it first appears, and a
glossary of terms and symbols is given at the end (Appendix). It is
organised into a short introduction to the device and the measurement
(Section~I) followed by eight parts, A to H, each corresponding to one step
in the signal chain or one question about it. Each part follows a consistent
structure: a plain-language statement of the question and the answer; the
assumptions required for the stated result, given explicitly; the governing
equations, each with the meaning of every symbol, a full derivation and a
worked number; the resulting predictions, with figures; and the evidence
that the result is right. Values obtained from a published source or
confirmed on the device are annotated \CONF. Values that are working
assumptions not yet confirmed on the fabricated device are annotated
\FLAG. A component that is physically present but deliberately left out of
the model is annotated \NOTMOD.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What this report changes.} An earlier version described a
protocol that is no longer correct and contained errors that further
checking has corrected. (i)~The surface is prepared by one-pot
co-immobilisation of the methylene-blue peptide probe and ferrocene-hexanethiol,
with no mercaptohexanol (MCH); ferrocene is present on the electrode but is
not part of the model (Section~I and Part~F). (ii)~The model's numerical
breakdown at fast electron-transfer rates was a floating-point
cancellation in the way the current was evaluated, not a physical effect; it is
fixed (Part~C). With it fixed, the earlier statement that labels at the PEG6
tip, with $\kappa=3$ to 6, sit where the signal is largest is seen to be
incorrect: the maximum is at $\kappa\approx0.44$, so the signal comes from labels
a few tenths of a nanometre above the tip (Part~D), and the predicted frequency
dependence changes accordingly. (iii)~The condition for first-order cleavage kinetics is
different from the one stated earlier (Part~G).

\section{Introduction: the Device and the Measurement}
\label{sec:story}

\subsection{What the sensor is for}

In a chronic (non-healing) wound the enzyme matrix metalloproteinase-9
(MMP-9) is present at a higher concentration than in a healing wound;
representative values are about 6~nM in healing and about 30~nM in chronic wound
fluid \cit{7b} (1~nM is one nanomolar, $10^{-9}$ mol per litre). The aim of the device is to estimate the MMP-9 concentration in a
small sample of wound fluid from an electrical measurement on a disposable
gold electrode. This report models the electrical side: given an MMP-9
concentration and an incubation time, what should the electrode read out?

\subsection{The electrode and its probe}

The sensor is a gold electrode with a geometric area of 1.7~mm$^2$
(0.017~cm$^2$) \cit{12} \CONF, which supersedes an earlier 1.5~mm-diameter design
specification (0.0177~cm$^2$); the $\sim$4\% difference is too small to alter any
conclusion. Molecules are attached to the gold through a thiol group (a sulfur
atom, S--H), which forms a strong Au--S bond. Two kinds of molecule are attached
in the same preparation step (\figref{fig:assembly}A):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item the \textbf{probe}: a ten-residue peptide (a chain of ten amino acids), SGKIPRTLTA, which contains the
  MMP-9 cleavage site (the bond between residues 7 and 8), joined to the gold through a
  short flexible spacer of six ethylene-glycol units (PEG6), and carrying a
  redox label, \textbf{methylene blue} (MB), covalently attached at the
  other end;
\item \textbf{ferrocene-hexanethiol} (Fc-hexanethiol): a short six-carbon chain
  with a ferrocene group at its tip. It is co-deposited with the probe in
  one pot, with no mercaptohexanol (MCH), and occupies the gold between probes,
  acting as the diluent that keeps probes well separated \cit{12} \CONF.
\end{itemize}
A redox label is a molecule that can accept or give up electrons at a
characteristic voltage. MB is the reporter used here: it exchanges electrons
with the gold, and that exchange is the signal. \textbf{Ferrocene is present
on the electrode but is not part of the model} \NOTMOD; the reasons, and what
the omission does and does not affect, are the subject of Part~F.

\subsection{What is measured}

\textbf{Current, voltage and the voltammogram.} An electric current is a flow
of charge; here it is a flow of electrons between the gold and the MB label.
Whether an MB molecule tends to hold two extra electrons (reduced form) or not
(oxidised form) depends on the voltage applied to the electrode, measured
against a stable reference electrode (Ag/AgCl). The \textbf{formal potential}
$\Eform$ is the voltage at which the two forms are equally favoured; for
MB it is close to $-0.25$~V \cit{1}. As the applied voltage is swept through
$\Eform$, MB molecules switch form and a current flows. A
\textbf{voltammogram} is the graph of this current against the applied
voltage. It has a peak near $\Eform$ (\figref{fig:anatomy}A). Three features
of the peak matter: its \emph{height} (proportional to how many MB labels take
part), its \emph{position} (near $\Eform$) and its \emph{width}
(the full width at half the maximum height, FWHM).

\textbf{Why the peak shrinks on cleavage.} Only MB labels that are still tethered
to the gold can exchange electrons quickly enough to contribute. When MMP-9
cuts a probe, the MB-bearing fragment drifts away and that label no longer
contributes. So the peak height falls in proportion to the fraction of intact
probes (\figref{fig:anatomy}C). The \textbf{cleavage fraction} $\Fk$ is the
fraction of probes cut during an incubation.

\textbf{Square-wave voltammetry (SWV).} Rather than sweeping the voltage
smoothly, SWV steps it along a staircase and superimposes a square wave
of amplitude $\Esw=25$~mV, so the voltage alternates between a
forward pulse (higher) and a reverse pulse (lower) at each step. The current
is recorded at the end of each pulse and the \textbf{net current}
$\Delta I = I_{\rm fwd}-I_{\rm rev}$ is plotted against the staircase voltage
(\figref{fig:anatomy}B). The frequency of the square wave is 10 to 20~Hz
\cit{12} \CONF. Why this technique is used is explained in Part~C.

\subsection{The measurement protocol and the readout}

Each electrode is scanned twice: once \emph{before} incubation and once
\emph{after} the sample has been on it for a time $T$ (\figref{fig:protocol}). The
cleavage fraction is read from the pair of peak heights on the same electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \Fkrec = 1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)},
  \label{eq:Fk_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm MB}$ is the net peak height. Because each electrode is compared only
with its own earlier scan, the number of probes it happens to carry cancels
out of $\Fkrec$ (Part~F). The MMP-9 concentration is then obtained from
$\Fkrec$ using the cleavage kinetics of Part~G.

\section{Part A: The Electrode Surface}
\label{sec:sam}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} How many probes are on the electrode, and are they far enough
apart to be treated as independent? \textbf{Answer.} At the working coverage of
\gammaZero~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ there are about \nProbesFmol~fmol of probe on the electrode
(\nProbes{} molecules), one for every \areaPerProbeNM~nm$^2$ of gold. The mean
spacing is \spacingNM~nm, well beyond the distance below which the labels of
neighbouring probes could overlap (\overlapSpacing~nm), so each probe can be treated as isolated. The coverage is a
placeholder, but it only sets the absolute size of the current, not the readout
(Part~F).

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{One-pot deposition of MB-peptide-PEG6 and Fc-hexanethiol, no MCH.}
  This is the current protocol \cit{12} \CONF. Earlier versions of this report and
  of the related layer reports described a mercaptohexanol (MCH) backfill step and a
  three-component deposition; neither describes the electrodes in use.
\item \textbf{Total probe coverage $\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol\,cm$^{-2}$.}
  An engineering estimate within the range reported for a comparable
  peptide-PEG system \cit{8}, not a measurement on these electrodes \FLAG. Direct measurement is
  not possible with the instrumentation currently available to the team
  \cit{12}, so it is expected to remain a placeholder. The team's monolayers are
  also not yet reproducible from electrode to electrode.
\item \textbf{The surface mix is not the solution mix.} The peptide-PEG probe is
  expected to adsorb more strongly than the short Fc-hexanethiol, so the
  fraction of probe on the surface is not simply the fraction in the deposition
  solution \FLAG.
\item \textbf{Fc-hexanethiol fills the gold between probes.} Whether it forms a
  fully packed layer has not been checked \FLAG. If it does, little bare gold remains, and the
  earlier concern about non-specific protein adsorption onto bare gold is reduced,
  though not removed, since proteins can still adsorb on the layer itself.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations}

\textbf{Probes per area and spacing.} A coverage $\Grtot$ in mol\,cm$^{-2}$
corresponds to $\Grtot N_A$ molecules per cm$^2$ (1~pmol $=10^{-12}$~mol, 1~fmol $=10^{-15}$~mol), where $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}$~mol$^{-1}$ is
Avogadro's number. Dividing into the area gives the area per probe, and if probes were
placed on a square grid the centre-to-centre spacing would be its square root:
\begin{equation}
  a=\frac{1}{\Grtot N_A},\qquad s=\sqrt{a}.
  \label{eq:spacing}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} $\Grtot N_A = 3\times10^{-12}\times6.022\times10^{23}
=1.81\times10^{12}$~cm$^{-2}=1.81\times10^{-2}$~nm$^{-2}$ (using
1~cm$^2=10^{14}$~nm$^2$), so $a=\areaPerProbeNM$~nm$^2$ and $s=\spacingNM$~nm. For randomly
placed probes the mean distance to the nearest neighbour is smaller,
$1/(2\sqrt{\rho})=\nnRandom$~nm for a number density $\rho=1/a$. The total number of probes
is $\Grtot N_A A=\nProbes$, that is $\nProbesFmol$~fmol on the
$A=0.017$~cm$^2$ electrode.

\textbf{Fraction of a full monolayer.} A full alkanethiol monolayer on gold has one molecule
per 0.2165~nm$^2$, that is $\fullMono\times10^{-10}$~mol\,cm$^{-2}$. The probe therefore
occupies only \fractionMono\% of the sites, roughly 1 in \oneInN; the remaining sites carry
Fc-hexanethiol if the layer is fully packed.

\textbf{Independence of probes.} The label at the end of a tether is displaced
sideways from its anchor by a random amount with root-mean-square value
$\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_z=\lateralRms$~nm (derived in Part~B). Two labels can interfere
only if their anchors are closer than about twice this, $\overlapSpacing$~nm. The
spacing of \spacingNM~nm is well above this (\figref{fig:spacing}), which is the basis for
computing the signal of one probe and multiplying by the number of probes.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_04_spacing.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} mean probe spacing (Eq.~\ref{eq:spacing}) against coverage.
    Below the shaded band the sideways reach of neighbouring labels overlaps. The
    working value (dot) is well clear of it, and stays clear up to about
    \gammaOverlap~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$. \textbf{B:} a top view of a random arrangement of probes
    at the working coverage, with the sideways reach of each label shaded.}
  \label{fig:spacing}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Result}

At $\Grtot=\gammaZero$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ probes are isolated (spacing \spacingNM~nm) and
number \nProbesFmol~fmol per electrode. The electrode-scale signal is
proportional to $\Grtot$ (Part~D), so every prediction of absolute current
inherits its uncertainty, but the cleavage readout of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} does not
(Part~F).

\section{Part B: Where the MB Label Sits and How Fast It Exchanges Electrons}
\label{sec:geom}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} The MB label is at the free end of a flexible tether, so its
distance $d$ from the gold changes from probe to probe and from moment to moment. How
is $d$ distributed, and how does the speed of electron transfer depend on it?
\textbf{Answer.} Modelling the peptide as a freely jointed chain gives a
half-Gaussian distribution of $d$, with the label most likely within about 1~nm of
the PEG6 tip. The electron-transfer rate constant falls by a factor of ten for every
\kOrder~\AA{} (0.23~nm) of extra distance, so only labels close to the
gold can exchange electrons at the speed of the square wave.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The peptide behaves as a freely jointed chain (FJC).} A standard
  model for a flexible, unstructured polymer, supported for short unfolded
  peptides \cit{3}. A modelling choice, not a measurement on this peptide \FLAG.
\item \textbf{The PEG6 spacer sets a floor.} The peptide is treated as rooted at the
  PEG6 tip, at height $d_{\rm PEG}$ above the gold, and cannot pass below it.
\item \textbf{Isolated probe.} Justified by the spacing of Part~A; not tested on the
  real surface \FLAG.
\item \textbf{Electron transfer is tunnelling through the tether,} with a rate
  decaying exponentially with distance. The decay constant is taken from alkanethiols;
  no PEG-specific value is available \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Chain statistics.} The peptide has $N_{\rm res}=10$ residues, each
adding $l_0\approx0.38$~nm of contour length, and a Kuhn length $b\approx0.76$~nm
(two residues) \cit{3}: the length over which the chain has effectively forgotten
its direction. The chain is therefore equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  N_K=\frac{N_{\rm res}\,l_0}{b}=\frac{10\times0.38}{0.76}=\nKuhn
  \label{eq:NK}
\end{equation}
independent rigid segments of length $b$, each pointing in a random direction. Take $z$ as the
height above the PEG6 tip. A segment with a direction uniformly random over the sphere has a
height component $b\cos\theta$, where $\cos\theta$ is uniform between $-1$ and $1$, so each
segment's height has mean 0 and variance $b^2\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=b^2/3$. The end height
is the sum of $N_K$ independent such terms; by the central limit theorem it is approximately
normal with mean zero and variance
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_z^2=\frac{N_K\,b^2}{3}=\frac{\langle R^2\rangle}{3},\qquad
  \langle R^2\rangle=N_Kb^2=\Rsq~\text{nm}^2,\qquad \sigma_z=\sigmaZ~\text{nm}.
  \label{eq:sigmaz}
\end{equation}
Here $\langle R^2\rangle$ is the mean square end-to-end distance, and the factor 3
arises because it is shared equally among the three directions. Five segments is few, so the
approximation is checked directly: the exact distribution of the sum of five such terms
differs from the Gaussian by a maximum of \ksGauss{} in cumulative probability
(\figref{fig:chain}B), so the Gaussian is an excellent description.

\textbf{Reference height: the PEG6 spacer.} The spacer is six ethylene-glycol units of
length $l_{\rm EO}=0.35$~nm, or $n_{K,{\rm PEG}}=6\times0.35/0.76=2.76$ Kuhn segments. For a
short chain in a good solvent, Flory's scaling law for a self-avoiding chain gives its extent as
\begin{equation}
  d_{\rm PEG}=n_{K,{\rm PEG}}^{3/5}\,b=2.76^{0.6}\times0.76=\dPEG~\text{nm}.
  \label{eq:flory}
\end{equation}
This is an extrapolation of a long-chain law to a chain of under three segments and is
therefore uncertain \FLAG. The ideal-chain value $\sqrt{n_{K,{\rm PEG}}}\,b=1.26$~nm and the
fully stretched value $6\times0.35=2.1$~nm bracket it, and this range is carried into the
sensitivity analysis (Part~H).

\textbf{Distribution of $d$.} The label sits at $d=d_{\rm PEG}+|z|$, since the gold
prevents the chain from passing below the tip. Folding the Gaussian of $z$ onto positive values
and renormalising gives the half-Gaussian
\begin{equation}
  P(d)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{1}{\sigma_z}\,
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right],\qquad d\ge d_{\rm PEG}.
  \label{eq:Pd}
\end{equation}
The prefactor restores $\int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{\infty}P\,{\rm d}d=1$, since a Gaussian has exactly half
its area on each side of its mean. The mean distance is $d_{\rm PEG}+\sigma_z\sqrt{2/\pi}=\meanDana$~nm.
The chain cannot extend beyond its contour length, so the distribution is cut off at
$d_{\rm max}=d_{\rm PEG}+N_{\rm res}l_0=\dMax$~nm; the probability of exceeding it is
\tailMass, which is negligible.

\textbf{Checking the folding.} Folding a Gaussian is not the same as a chain that is
prevented from entering the region below its root. Two exact alternatives were simulated
(\figref{fig:chain}C; $4\times10^5$ random chains). Folding the exact end-height
distribution gives a mean $d$ of \meanDfold~nm, indistinguishable from the half-Gaussian
(\meanDana~nm), and a predicted signal within \foldDiff\% of it. Requiring that \emph{no}
segment of the chain passes below the tip is stricter: only \wallAccept\% of random chains
satisfy it, the distribution shifts to larger $d$ (mean \meanDwall~nm), and the predicted peak
current falls by \wallDrop\% (\sigWall{} against \sigAna~nA). The half-Gaussian is therefore the
central estimate, and the stricter wall model is carried into the sensitivity analysis as a
lower bound on the geometry.

\textbf{Electron-transfer rate against distance.} MB exchanges electrons with the gold by
quantum-mechanical tunnelling through the tether. The probability of tunnelling decays
exponentially with the distance travelled, and the rate constant $\kzero$ (the rate at which
an MB molecule exchanges electrons when it is at its formal potential, in s$^{-1}$)
inherits this:
\begin{equation}
  \kzero(d)=\kzero(0)\,e^{-\beta d}.
  \label{eq:k0d}
\end{equation}
The decay constant is $\beta=1.0$~\AA$^{-1}=\betaNM$~nm$^{-1}$, the accepted value for
tunnelling through alkane chains \cit{4}; no PEG-specific value is available, so this
is a placeholder \FLAG. The anchoring measurement is that of Dauphin-Ducharme
\emph{et al.}\ \cit{1}: MB on a six-carbon linker, with the label 8~\AA{} from the gold, has
$\kzero=5.0\times10^{4}$~s$^{-1}$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d} extrapolated back to zero distance gives
$\kzero(0)=5.0\times10^4\,e^{8}=\kZero$~s$^{-1}$, and evaluated at the PEG6 tip,
\begin{equation}
  \kref=\kzero(d_{\rm PEG})=\kZero\times e^{-\betaNM\times\dPEG}=\kRef~\text{s}^{-1}.
  \label{eq:kref}
\end{equation}
Because $\kzero$ falls tenfold per 0.23~nm, a label 0.5~nm above the tip is already
$\sim150$ times slower. Which labels can therefore keep up with a 10 to 20~Hz square wave is
decided not by $P(d)$ alone but by the balance in \figref{fig:rate}, quantified in Parts~C and D.

    lines mark the rate at which a single probe gives its largest signal at 10 and 20~Hz
    (Part~C). \textbf{B:} the single-probe peak signal against $\kappa=\kzero/(2f)$, the ratio
    of the label's rate to the pulse rate (defined in Part~C); the maximum is at

\end{figure}

\subsection{Result}

Labels are spread over $d=\dPEG$ to about $\dMax$~nm with mean \meanDana~nm
(Eq.~\ref{eq:Pd}), and their electron-transfer rate constants span more than fifteen
orders of magnitude. At the square-wave frequencies used, only a thin slice of this
range, a band a few tenths of a nanometre above the PEG6 tip (Part~D), produces signal.

\section{Part C: Square-Wave Voltammetry and the Signal from One Probe}
\label{sec:core}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} What current does a single MB label at a fixed distance from the
gold give in a square-wave scan? \textbf{Answer.} The fraction of MB in its reduced form
relaxes exponentially towards the value set by the applied voltage, at a rate set by the
label's electron-transfer speed. The current sampled at the end of each pulse is
proportional to how far the label is from that equilibrium at that moment. This is
large only if the label's rate is comparable to the pulse rate: too slow and
nothing happens, too fast and equilibrium is reached long before sampling. There is a
single best speed, $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ (\figref{fig:kappa}B).

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Butler--Volmer kinetics for surface-confined MB.} The standard
  textbook model for a redox species attached to an electrode \cit{13}; not
  specific to this device and not in question.
\item \textbf{Constant voltage within each pulse.} True by construction of the
  square wave; it is what makes the equation below exactly solvable.
\item \textbf{Sampling at the end of each pulse.} How the potentiostat samples the current
  affects the predictions at fast rates (Part~E).
\item \textbf{Two-electron exchange in one step,} with $\alpha=0.37$, $n_e=2$
  \cit{1,6} \CONF. The intermediate one-electron step of MB is not resolved in the model
  (the same simplification as the source \cit{1}).
\item \textbf{Room temperature, $T=298.15$~K.} The wound is at about 310~K; the
  measurement temperature has not been set \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Why square-wave voltammetry}

Any electrode voltage change also charges the electrical double layer at the gold
surface, which acts as a capacitor of about $C_{\rm dl}=\CdlUF~\mu$F\,cm$^{-2}$
(a typical value for gold in aqueous electrolyte, assumed here), so
$\Cdev~\mu$F on the 0.017~cm$^2$ electrode. This charging current is not the signal, and
in cyclic voltammetry (CV), where the voltage is swept steadily, it is present all the time.
The size of the wanted signal relative to it can be estimated. In CV at sweep rate $v$ a
surface-bound couple gives a peak current $i_p=n_e^2F^2vA\Grtot/(4RT)$ \cit{13}, while
charging gives $i_c=C_{\rm dl}Av$. Their ratio is independent of $v$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{i_p}{i_c}=\frac{n_e^2F^2\Grtot}{4RT\,C_{\rm dl}}
  =\frac{4\times(96485)^2\times3\times10^{-12}}{4\times8.3145\times298.15\times20\times10^{-6}}
  =\cvRatio,
  \label{eq:cv_ratio}
\end{equation}
so the peak sits on a charging plateau of comparable size (\figref{fig:background}B),
and any drift in the plateau is a drift in the apparent peak.

In SWV the voltage is held constant during each pulse and the current is recorded only at
its end. After a voltage jump the charging current decays as $e^{-t/(R_uC)}$, where
$R_u$ is the resistance of the solution between the electrode and reference (taken as
$\RuOhm~\Omega$, an assumed value). With $R_uC=\RCms$~ms, by the end of a 10~Hz half-pulse
($\halfPulseMs$~ms) the charging current has fallen by a factor of
$e^{-\chargeDecayExp}$. Meanwhile the wanted Faradaic current, which decays on the much
slower time scale of the electron-transfer kinetics, is still large. In addition, taking the
\emph{difference} between the forward and reverse currents cancels remaining slowly varying
background. Both steps remove background that CV retains, giving SWV its greater
sensitivity for a surface-bound probe \cit{5,11}.

    \textbf{C:} charging current per unit of Faradaic current at the moment of measurement.}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Rate constants.} Let $\Grred$ and $\Grox$ be the amounts of MB per unit area
in the reduced and oxidised forms (mol\,cm$^{-2}$), with $\Grtot=\Grred+\Grox$ fixed. At
applied voltage $E$, the Butler--Volmer model gives first-order rate constants for reduction
($k_f$) and oxidation ($k_b$):
\begin{equation}
  k_f=\kzero\exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha\,\xi\Bigr],\qquad
  k_b=\kzero\exp\!\Bigl[(1-\alpha)\,\xi\Bigr],\qquad
  \xi=\frac{n_eF\,(E-\Eform)}{RT},
  \label{eq:bv_rates}
\end{equation}
where $F=96485$~C\,mol$^{-1}$ is the Faraday constant, $R=8.3145$~J\,mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$
the gas constant, $\alpha$ the transfer coefficient (the fraction of the applied voltage
that helps the reaction go in one direction) and $\xi$ the overpotential in thermal units.
Positive of $\Eform$, $k_b$ grows and $k_f$ shrinks, favouring the oxidised form, and
vice versa. At $E=\Eform$ both equal $\kzero$ (for $\alpha=\tfrac12$).

\textbf{Governing equation.} Reduced MB is formed from oxidised at rate $k_f\Grox$
and lost at rate $k_b\Grred$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt}=k_f\Grox-k_b\Grred=k_f\Grtot-K\,\Grred,\qquad K\equiv k_f+k_b.
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Solution.} Within a pulse $E$, hence $k_f$ and $k_b$, is constant, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode} is a first-order linear equation with constant coefficients.
Multiply by the integrating factor $e^{Kt}$:
$\frac{d}{dt}(\Grred e^{Kt})=k_f\Grtot\,e^{Kt}$. Integrate from 0 to $\tau$ and divide by $e^{K\tau}$:
\[
  \Grred(\tau)=\Grred(0)\,e^{-K\tau}+\frac{k_f\Grtot}{K}\bigl(1-e^{-K\tau}\bigr).
\]
The quantity $\Greq\equiv k_f\Grtot/K$ is the value at which $d\Grred/dt=0$, the equilibrium
coverage at this voltage. In terms of it,
\begin{equation}
  \Grred(\tau)=\Greq+\bigl[\Grred(0)-\Greq\bigr]\,e^{-K\tau}.
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
The coverage relaxes exponentially to equilibrium with time constant $1/K$. This solution is exact,
and agrees with a numerical ODE solver to $\odeMaxErr$ (\figref{fig:ode}A,B).

\textbf{Current.} The current is the charge transferred per second, $n_eF$ per mole
converted, times the area:
\begin{equation}
  I=n_eFA\,\bigl(k_b\Grred-k_f\Grox\bigr).
  \label{eq:I_two}
\end{equation}
Substituting $\Grox=\Grtot-\Grred$ gives $I=n_eFA[(k_b+k_f)\Grred-k_f\Grtot]
=n_eFA\,K\,(\Grred-\Greq)$, so
\begin{equation}
  I=n_eFA\,K\,\bigl[\Grred(0)-\Greq\bigr]\,e^{-K\tau}.
  \label{eq:I_stable}
\end{equation}
The current is $K$ times the displacement from equilibrium, and the displacement decays as
$e^{-K\tau}$. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:I_two} and \eqref{eq:I_stable} are algebraically identical, but they are
not numerically equal in floating-point arithmetic. When $K\tau$ is large, each term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_two} is of order $k\Grtot$ and their difference is smaller than the
rounding error of either. Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_two} therefore returns numerical noise
instead of the physical result for $\kappa\gtrsim15$; the earlier report recognised this as numerical
noise but left the range unusable. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_stable} removes the
cancellation (\figref{fig:ode}C): the two forms agree to better than $10^{-8}$ relative where both are
reliable, and the stable form continues correctly to arbitrarily fast rates.

\textbf{The SWV sequence.} At each staircase voltage $E_{\rm step}$ the electrode is held at
$E_{\rm step}+\Esw$ for $\tau=1/(2f)$ (forward pulse), and then at $E_{\rm step}-\Esw$ for
another $\tau$ (reverse pulse), where $f$ is the square-wave frequency (\figref{fig:wave}A).
The coverage at the end of one pulse is the starting coverage of the next
(Eq.~\ref{eq:soln} applied twice in succession, \figref{fig:wave}B). The net current is
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E_{\rm step})=I_{\rm fwd}-I_{\rm rev},
  \label{eq:DI_step}
\end{equation}
evaluated after five cycles at the starting potential so that the result is independent of the
arbitrary initial coverage.

\textbf{The dimensionless rate and the best speed.} Define
\begin{equation}
  \kappa\equiv\frac{\kzero}{2f}=\kzero\,\tau,
\end{equation}
the ratio of the label's rate to the pulse rate. At $E=\Eform$, $K=2\kzero$ (for
$\alpha=\tfrac12$) so $K\tau=2\kappa$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_stable} says the current is
$K$ times a displacement that shrinks as $e^{-K\tau}$; the product $K\,e^{-K\tau}$ is maximal
where $K\tau=1$, that is at $\kappa=\tfrac12$. The full simulation, which includes the carry-over
between pulses, places the maximum at
$\kappa=\kappaMax$ (\figref{fig:ode}C, \figref{fig:rate}B), close to the simple estimate.
A label that exchanges much faster ($\kappa\gg1$) reaches equilibrium within each pulse and
its sampled current is nearly zero: physically, the electrons have already moved before the current is measured.
A label much slower ($\kappa\ll1$) barely responds. Numerically, the largest single-probe
signal is $g_{\max}=\gMax$ in units of $n_eF\cdot2f$ per unit $A\Grtot$.

\textbf{Peak shape.} For probes at $\kappa\lesssim1$ the net peak is a single lobe at
$\Eform$, with a width between \fwhmMin{} and \fwhmMaxK~mV depending on $\kappa$; at larger $\kappa$
the forward and reverse currents peak at different voltages and the net curve
splits into two lobes (\figref{fig:kappa}A). For comparison, a reversible, continuously-swept
couple gives a peak of width $90.6/n_e=45.3$~mV, derived in Part~E.

    split into two lobes. \textbf{B:} full width at half maximum of the tallest lobe

\subsection{Result}

A single MB label gives a net peak at $\Eform=-0.25$~V whose height, in units of
$n_eF\cdot2f\,A\Grtot$, is at most $\gMax$, reached at $\kappa=\kappaMax$, and falls to \gThreePct\% of that at $\kappa=3$ and to
\gFivePct\% at $\kappa=5$. A label at the PEG6 tip of this device has $\kappa=\kappaTenHz$ at
10~Hz and $\kappaTwentyHz$ at 20~Hz, so it is faster than the optimum and contributes only \gTipPct\% of the maximum signal per probe at 10~Hz; this is what
determines which labels give the signal (Part~D).

\section{Part D: The Signal from the Whole Electrode}
\label{sec:avg}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} The electrode carries many probes, with labels at different distances.
What is the total signal, and how does it change when probes are cut? \textbf{Answer.} Adding up the
probes, weighted by $P(d)$, gives a single peak of about \peakNA~nA at 10~Hz. It comes almost
entirely from a narrow band of labels $\sim$\dStarTenOff~nm above the PEG6 tip. Its
height is exactly proportional to the number of intact probes, and its position and width do not
change on cleavage.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Probes are independent.} Justified by the spacing of Part~A.
\item \textbf{Cleaved probes give no signal, and intact probes are unaffected by their
  neighbours being cleaved.} Once the peptide is cut, the MB fragment leaves the surface (\figref{fig:assembly}B).
\item \textbf{Cleavage is equally likely for a probe at any label height $d$.} An
  assumption \FLAG. It underlies the proportionality in Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk} and is tested in
  Part~F.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Distance average.} A label at distance $d$ has $\kzero(d)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:k0d}), hence a
net current per unit area and unit coverage $\DI_{\rm norm}(E;\kzero(d))$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_step}. The
electrode carries $(1-\Fk)\Grtot A$ of intact probe, distributed over $d$ according to $P(d)$:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk)=(1-\Fk)\,\Grtot A\int_{d_{\rm PEG}}^{d_{\max}}P(d)\,
  \DI_{\rm norm}\bigl(E;\kzero(d)\bigr)\,{\rm d}d.
  \label{eq:dist_avg}
\end{equation}
The integral has no closed form and is evaluated by quadrature; at 10 and 20~Hz the result is converged to
$\quadErrEighty$\% and $\quadErrEightyTwentyHz$\% with the 80 points used (\figref{fig:quad}A).

\textbf{Proportionality to intact probe.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg} depends on $\Fk$ only through the
prefactor, so
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk)=(1-\Fk)\,\DI(E;0)\quad\text{exactly},
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
under the assumptions above. The peak height falls in direct proportion; the position and
width do not change (\figref{fig:cleave}). An earlier version reported this as verified numerically
to 2\%; it is exact by construction, and the code confirms it to machine precision (no deviation at double precision).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_10_cleavage_response.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} predicted voltammograms at six cleavage fractions. \textbf{B:} peak height against the
    intact fraction $1-\Fk$; the points lie on the proportional line. \textbf{C:} peak position
    and width do not change with $\Fk$. The proportionality is exact by
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}; what is not guaranteed is the assumption behind it (Part~F).}
  \label{fig:cleave}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Which labels give the signal.} Change variable in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist_avg} from $d$ to
$\kappa$. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:k0d}, $\kappa=\kappa(d)$ falls by a factor $e^{\beta\,{\rm d}d}$
over ${\rm d}d$, so $|{\rm d}d|=|{\rm d}\kappa|/(\beta\kappa)=\frac{1}{\beta}\,|{\rm d}\ln\kappa|$. Write the single-probe
response as $\DI_{\rm norm}=n_eF\cdot2f\,g(\kappa)$ (defining $g$ as in Part~C). Because
$g(\kappa)$ is a narrow bump on a logarithmic axis (\figref{fig:rate}B) and $P(d)$ varies slowly compared with
it, $P(d)$ can be evaluated at the centre of the bump, $d_\star$, where
$\kappa(d_\star)=\kappa_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}
  \DI_{\rm pk}\approx n_eF\cdot2f\;A\Grtot\;\frac{P(d_\star)}{\beta}\,G,\qquad
  G=\int g(\kappa)\,{\rm d}\ln\kappa=\Gint,\qquad
  d_\star=d_{\rm PEG}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{\kref}{2f\kappa_{\max}}.
  \label{eq:one_line}
\end{equation}
At 10~Hz $d_\star=\dStarTen$~nm and at 20~Hz $\dStarTwenty$~nm, only $\dStarTenOff$ and $\dStarTwentyOff$~nm above the
tip (\figref{fig:band}A,B). Eq.~\eqref{eq:one_line} reproduces the full calculation to within
\approxErrTen\% (\figref{fig:band}C) and shows three things:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item the peak is proportional to $f$: doubling the frequency doubles it (predicted ratio \ratioTwenty{} for 20 against 10~Hz, a directly testable prediction);
\item it depends on the geometry only through $P(d_\star)/\beta$, not on the huge range of rate constants;
\item it is nearly independent of the anchoring value $\kref$: changing $\kref$ shifts
  $d_\star$ but not the size of the bump. Scaling $\kref$ from 0.1 to 100 times its base value
  (127~s$^{-1}$) changes the peak to between $\kRefSpanLo$ and $\kRefSpanHi$ of its value
  (\figref{fig:freq}B). This holds only while $d_\star$ stays within the range where
  probes exist: if the effective tip were much higher than assumed, so that $\kref$ fell by
  three decades, the signal would collapse (Part~H).
\end{enumerate}

    narrow band. \textbf{B:} the centre of the band $d_\star$ against frequency: numerical
    (points) and Eq.~\eqref{eq:one_line} (line). \textbf{C:} peak height from the full model
    (points) and the one-line formula (line).}
  \label{fig:band}

\textbf{Frequency response.} Because $\DI_{\rm pk}\propto f$ while the band lies above the
tip, the predicted peak rises linearly with frequency from 1~Hz up to about $\kref/(2\kappa_{\max})\approx140$~Hz, where the
band reaches the tip and the wall truncates it; the model's maximum is at about \fOpt~Hz
(\figref{fig:freq}A). The operating range of 10 to 20~Hz is well below this, so the model predicts a larger
signal at higher frequency (a fourfold gain from 10 to 40~Hz, \ratioForty{}); whether it is worth
having depends on background and noise not treated here. Measuring peak height against frequency
on a real electrode is the most direct available test of the whole rate model (Part~E).

\subsection{Result}

The model predicts a net peak of \peakNA~nA at \peakEmV~mV, width \fwhmMV~mV, at 10~Hz for
$\Grtot=\gammaZero$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ and $A=0.017$~cm$^2$ (about twice this at 20~Hz).
Its height is exactly proportional to $(1-\Fk)$ and to $\Grtot$, and is nearly independent of $\kref$.
The absolute value depends on the placeholder inputs (Part~H); the proportionality to
$(1-\Fk)$ does not.

\section{Part E: Validation}
\label{sec:valid}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} How do we know the model is right? \textbf{Answer.} The mathematics is verified
directly and to high precision. The model reproduces the standard behaviour of square-wave
voltammetry for a surface-bound couple. It does \emph{not} reproduce the published
high-rate voltammograms of Dauphin-Ducharme \emph{et al.}\ \cit{1}, and the reason is not
established; this limits the confidence in the \emph{absolute} current but not in the cleavage
readout, and a specific measurement on the fabricated device would settle it. Nothing in the
model has yet been compared with a measured voltammogram of this device.

\subsection{The Laviron peak width and why it is not the target}

The width of a \emph{reversible} surface wave, swept continuously, is a useful reference, and it is
the source of the 45.3~mV figure often quoted for a two-electron surface couple \cit{2}. Its shape is
$|\Psi(\eta)|=\eta/(1+\eta)^2$ with $\eta=e^{\xi}$ and $\xi$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bv_rates}.
The maximum is where $d|\Psi|/d\eta=0$: $(1+\eta)^2-2\eta(1+\eta)=0$, so $\eta=1$ and $|\Psi|_{\max}=\tfrac14$.
Half the maximum is where $\eta/(1+\eta)^2=\tfrac18$, i.e.\ $\eta^2-6\eta+1=0$, so $\eta_\pm=3\pm2\sqrt2$. The two
roots are reciprocal ($9-8=1$), so $\xi_\pm=\pm\ln(3+2\sqrt2)$ and the width in $\xi$ is
$2\ln(3+2\sqrt2)=3.525$. Converting to voltage with $\xi=n_eF(E-\Eform)/RT$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FWHM}=\frac{3.525\,RT}{n_eF}=\frac{90.6}{n_e}~\text{mV at }25^\circ\text{C},
  \label{eq:fwhm}
\end{equation}
which is 45.3~mV for $n_e=2$. This is the width of a different experiment (a continuous sweep of a couple that
stays at equilibrium) from the sampled-current square wave used here, where the peak is wider
(\fwhmMin{} to \fwhmMaxK~mV, Part~C) because the current is a non-equilibrium quantity sampled
at a fixed time. A quantitative match to Eq.~\eqref{eq:fwhm} is therefore not expected, and none is claimed. (An earlier
version listed the difference, about 24\% RMS in shape, as an unresolved gap; it is the expected difference between two
experiments.)

\subsection{Consistency with square-wave theory}

The position of the single-probe maximum, $\kappa=\kappaMax$, means $\kzero/f=2\kappa=0.89$. The
standard peak-current analysis of square-wave voltammetry for a surface-bound couple places the
quasi-reversible maximum where the rate constant and the frequency are of the same order \cit{1,5}, which agrees. It
also gives the same picture qualitatively: the signal vanishes for very slow and for very fast
electron transfer, and the ``optimum frequency'' of a given label is $f\approx\kzero$.

\subsection{Comparison with Dauphin-Ducharme \emph{et al.}}

The most relevant published data are for methylene blue on a six-carbon linker on gold \cit{1}, whose
Figure~4 shows net square-wave peaks at 20 to 1000~Hz. The paper reports $\kzero=5.0\times10^4$~s$^{-1}$
for the label at 8~\AA, from a numerical simulation fitted to the data. At these rates
$\kappa=\kzero/(2f)$ is \kappaDDhundred{} at 100~Hz and \kappaDDtwenty{} at 20~Hz. The present model
predicts essentially no net current there (\figref{fig:dd}A: many orders of magnitude below the maximum, at $\kappa\gtrsim25$), whereas the paper
shows a well-defined peak of about 430~nA at 100~Hz. Two points of contact can be checked:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Height and existence of the peak at high $\kappa$.} Not reproduced. This is a disagreement
  between the present model and the published simulation at $\kappa\gg1$, not a numerical instability of
  the present one (Part~C).
\item \textbf{Peak width.} The published net peak at 100~Hz, 25~mV has a full width at half
  maximum of about 80~mV (read from the published figure, $\pm10$~mV). No single rate constant in the present model gives a
  width above \fwhmMaxK~mV (\figref{fig:dd}B).
\end{itemize}
The paper notes that a peak-height analysis of the Lovri\'c type, which is the class of model used
here, places the rate constant about two orders of magnitude lower than the full numerical simulation and
attributes the difference to background at high frequency \cit{1}. That is not sufficient
to explain a peak that the present model puts at less than $10^{-19}$ of its maximum. The cause has
not been established. Candidate explanations, none tested, are: (i)~the instrument records a time-averaged rather than
end-of-pulse current; (ii)~the real MB population has a spread of rate constants (from packing,
orientation or tilt), so that a sub-population with $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ produces the peak even when the
mean rate is very fast; (iii)~Butler--Volmer kinetics fail at the largest rates. Explanation (ii)
is consistent with the present model's own conclusion in Part~D: the signal always comes from
labels with $\kappa\approx\kappaMax$, whatever the mean.

The same mismatch appears for ferrocene. The literature rate constant for ferrocene-hexanethiol
($2.7\times10^{5}$~s$^{-1}$ \cit{1}) gives $\kappa$ of order $10^{4}$ at 10~Hz, where the
model predicts no signal, yet peaks of $5\times10^{-8}$ to $10^{-5}$~A were seen on the device \cit{12}. This is one
reason ferrocene is not modelled (Part~F).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/swvr_15_dd.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} the single-probe peak signal against $\kappa$ (curve) with the range of
    $\kappa$ corresponding to the Dauphin-Ducharme MB data (red) and to a label at the PEG6
    tip of this device (green). \textbf{B:} full width at half maximum of the modelled peak

  \label{fig:dd}

\textbf{What this means for the device.} At 10 to 20~Hz the signal comes from labels with
$\kappa\approx\kappaMax$ by construction, and it is largely insensitive to the anchoring rate
constant (Part~D). If the real rate distribution differs from the one assumed, the \emph{absolute}
current would differ, and it would not scale linearly with frequency. The proportionality of the peak to intact probe
(Eq.~\ref{eq:DI_Fk}), which is what the readout uses, would not change, since it depends
only on the assumption that cleavage is independent of label height (Part~F).

\subsection{What would settle it}

Two measurements on the fabricated electrode, each within the team's existing instrumentation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Peak height against frequency.} The model predicts a straight line through the origin from
  1 to about 100~Hz (a ratio of \ratioTwenty{} for 20~Hz against 10~Hz; \figref{fig:freq}C). Departure from this
  would identify the rate distribution.
\item \textbf{Peak width.} The model predicts \fwhmMV~mV at 10~Hz; a substantially wider peak indicates a
  spread of environments not in the model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Result}

The equations and their implementation are correct; the model agrees with the qualitative
behaviour of square-wave voltammetry; it does not reproduce published high-rate voltammograms, and it is
unvalidated against the device itself. The absolute predicted current should be read as an estimate, with the
uncertainty shown in Part~H; the proportional relation between peak height and intact probe is a
structural property of the model and not a fitted result.

\section{Part F: Reading Out Cleavage with MB Alone}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} Ferrocene is on the electrode but is not in the model. What does the model
assume by leaving it out, and does the readout still work? \textbf{Answer.} Ferrocene reacts at a
voltage far from MB's, so it does not interfere with the MB scan. The readout
$\Fkrec$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} does not need ferrocene: it compares each electrode with its own earlier
scan, so the number of probes cancels. What ferrocene could have provided, and the model does not,
is a check that nothing other than MMP-9 changed the signal during incubation.

\subsection{What is known about ferrocene on this device}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Ferrocene-hexanethiol is co-immobilised with the probe in the same step} and
  remains on the electrode; it acts as the diluent \cit{12} \CONF.
\item \textbf{Its formal potential} has been observed at about $+0.30$ to $+0.40$~V
  against Ag/AgCl, varying between electrodes, compared with the value near $+0.21$~V used in
  earlier versions of this model \cit{12} \FLAG. A single-component ferrocene scan is needed to fix it.
\item \textbf{No separate pair of MB and ferrocene peaks} has been resolved in the team's recorded
  scans \cit{12}. Why is not established. One possibility is that the two labels were scanned in
  separate voltage windows (MB about $-0.40$ to $-0.10$~V, ferrocene about $+0.15$ to $+0.55$~V),
  so that one scan would not show both; this has not been checked.
\item \textbf{Ferrocene's rate constant and coverage are not known.} The literature value for a
  different construct is too fast for the present model to give any signal (Part~E), yet peaks were
  observed, so no reliable Fc rate constant is available \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Does omitting ferrocene change the MB scan?}

\textbf{Overlap of voltages.} MB reacts near $-0.25$~V and the MB scan runs from $-0.40$ to
$-0.10$~V; ferrocene reacts at $+0.21$ to $+0.40$~V, at least $\gapFc$~mV beyond the end of the MB
window (\figref{fig:potentials}A). The tail of a one-electron wave falls as $4e^{-\xi}/(1+e^{-\xi})^2$
(the shape of Eq.~\ref{eq:fwhm} with $n_e=1$); at $\gapFc$~mV this is $\leakEnv$ of the ferrocene peak
(\figref{fig:potentials}B). The largest ferrocene peaks recorded, $10^{-5}$~A, are \fcMbRatio{} times the predicted MB peak, so in the
worst case the ferrocene tail would add \leakMB\% to the MB peak. The Butler--Volmer model of Part~C
predicts a far smaller tail (below $10^{-100}$). Interference by the ferrocene wave is therefore
negligible or, in the worst case, small.

  \caption{\textbf{A:} the voltage of the MB scan window and formal potential, and the two estimates
    for ferrocene. \textbf{B:} upper bound on the ferrocene wave (relative to its peak) at a given
    distance from its peak; the dotted line is the distance from the ferrocene literature

\textbf{Physical effect on MB.} Ferrocene-hexanethiol chains are neighbours of the probe.
They may crowd the tether and change $P(d)$, and their charge state could shift $\Eform$ of MB.
Neither has been measured; the first is the reason $d_{\rm PEG}$ is treated as uncertain (Part~H).

\subsection{What the earlier ratio did, and did not, do}

An earlier design divided the MB peak by the ferrocene peak on the same scan and compared the ratio before and after:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Fkrec\right)_{\rm with\ Fc}=1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}
  =1-\frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)}\quad\text{whenever }\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)=\DI_{\rm Fc}(0),
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio_old}
\end{equation}
because the ferrocene terms cancel exactly. Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old} equals
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} whenever the ferrocene signal is unchanged by incubation. The ferrocene reference therefore
never corrected for differences in coverage between electrodes; Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} already does that by
comparing each electrode only with itself. What the ferrocene reference could add is a
\emph{diagnostic}: if $\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)\neq\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)$, something other than cleavage changed the
electrode. The model does not include that, and neither does the readout. Because ferrocene remains on the electrode
this diagnostic remains available experimentally without any change to the model.

\subsection{What the MB-only readout assumes}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A1. No non-enzymatic loss of signal during incubation} \FLAG. If a fraction $\delta$ of the probe
  is lost by other means (desorption, fouling, electrode ageing), then the intact fraction is
  $(1-\Fk)(1-\delta)$ and
  \begin{equation}
    \Fkrec=1-(1-\Fk)(1-\delta)=\Fk+\delta\,(1-\Fk).
    \label{eq:drift}
  \end{equation}
  The bias $\delta(1-\Fk)$ is always positive: drift is read as extra cleavage, so a chronic wound
  would read as more severe than it is (\figref{fig:drift}). In a sample with no MMP-9, a loss of
  5\% at 30~min would read as \blankAppFive~nM, a third of the healing/chronic boundary.
\item \textbf{A2. Cleavage is equally likely for any label height $d$} \FLAG. Suppose instead the
  relative cleavage rate is $q(d)\propto e^{\lambda(d-\bar d)}$, normalised to the same mean,
  so $\lambda>0$ means labels far from the gold are cut faster (as steric hindrance near the surface would
  produce). The surviving population then over-represents near labels, which dominate the signal,
  and $\Fkrec$ falls below the true fraction cut. For $\lambda=2$~nm$^{-1}$ and a true fraction of
  \distBiasTrue\% the recovered fraction is \distBiasPts{} points too low (\figref{fig:distbias}). If the sign of $\lambda$ is reversed the error is positive.
\item \textbf{A3. The two scans are made on the same electrode with the same settings.} Different
  frequency, amplitude or window would change the peak independent of cleavage.
\item \textbf{A4. Noise in each reading is independent}, as used below.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Noise, coverage and electrode-to-electrode variation}

\textbf{Noise in the readout.} Let each peak height have an independent absolute error $\sigma_I$, and
$\sigma_r=\sigma_I/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$. Propagating errors through Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple}, with
$\Fkrec=1-I_T/I_0$:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_F^2=\frac{\sigma_I^2}{I_0^2}+\frac{I_T^2\sigma_I^2}{I_0^4}
  =\sigma_r^2\Bigl[1+(1-\Fk)^2\Bigr],\qquad \sigma_F\xrightarrow{\Fk\to0}\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_r.
  \label{eq:sigmaF}
\end{equation}
The noise floor for the before/after MB readout has not been measured. The earlier 3.4\% figure
came from a ratio of four readings and does not apply. If the per-reading noise is unchanged, and 3.4\% was
$2\sigma_r$ (four equal contributions), then $\sigma_F=\sqrt2\sigma_r=\sigmaRelOld$\%; this is conditional and
unverified \FLAG.

\textbf{Coverage.} $\Fkrec$ is independent of $\Grtot$ exactly (three electrodes with a tenfold spread
in coverage give the same $\Fkrec$ to machine precision, \figref{fig:coverage}A). Coverage matters only
through noise: with a fixed absolute noise, an electrode with less probe has a smaller $I_0$ and so a larger
$\sigma_r$, worsening the detection limit (\figref{fig:coverage}C). The earlier worry that
electrode-to-electrode variation would make a single calibration curve unusable applies to absolute
current, which the readout does not use; what does remain are (a)~the noise penalty on low-coverage
electrodes, and (b)~variation between electrodes in the surface efficiency $\eta$ of Part~G, which is unknown \FLAG.

  \label{fig:coverage}

\subsection{Result}

Leaving ferrocene out does not change the MB scan or the algebra of the readout. The readout rests on
assumptions A1 to A4; A1 and A2 are testable, and an experimental ferrocene reference on the same electrode would test
A1. Coverage variation affects the noise, not the readout. The noise floor is unknown and controls the
detection limit (Part~G).

\section{Part G: From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} For a given MMP-9 concentration and incubation time, what fraction of probes is
cut, and what is the smallest concentration the device could detect? \textbf{Answer.} Each intact probe is
cut at a constant rate proportional to the MMP-9 concentration, so the intact fraction decays
exponentially: $\Fk=1-e^{-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T}$. With the measured solution rate, and a
surface efficiency of 0.20 that has not been measured, 6~nM (healing) gives $\Fk=\fkHealThirty$\% and 30~nM
(chronic) gives $\Fk=\fkChronThirty$\% after 30~minutes. The detection limit is set by the noise in the readout,
which is not yet known.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The solution rate constant applies to the tethered probe, scaled by a surface
  efficiency $\eta$.} The measured value, $k_{\rm cat}/K_m=\kSol$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$
  ($k_{\rm cat}=86.4$~s$^{-1}$, $K_m=1.3$~mM), is for the free peptide SGKIPRTLTA in solution \cit{7} \CONF. On the surface
  the enzyme must reach the probe and the tether is attached to a PEG spacer, a dye and a crowded layer, so
  cleavage is expected to be slower. $\eta=0.20$ is a central estimate, with a plausible range of 0.01 to 1,
  not measured for this device \FLAG. It would be measured by comparing the electrode readout with a
  solution-phase fluorescence assay on the same sample.
\item \textbf{The enzyme concentration is constant during the incubation} (enzyme is a catalyst and is
  not consumed; loss by adsorption to surfaces or by inhibitors is not modelled). $[\text{MMP-9}]$
  means active enzyme; activation of the pro-form and inhibition by TIMPs are treated in the wound-biology report.
\item \textbf{The conditions for first-order kinetics below hold.} They are checked explicitly.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations and derivation}

\textbf{Cleavage rate of a fixed site.} For an enzyme E cutting a substrate S that is fixed on a surface,
\[
  \mathrm{E}+\mathrm{S}\;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{ES}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_{\rm cat}\;}\;\mathrm{E}+\text{products},
  \qquad K_m=\frac{k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat}}{k_1}.
\]
Let $S_T$ be the number of intact probes (free plus enzyme-bound). If the enzyme-bound state reaches steady
state quickly compared with the depletion of probe, $k_1[\mathrm{E}]\,[\mathrm{S}]=(k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat})[\mathrm{ES}]$
with $[\mathrm{S}]=S_T-[\mathrm{ES}]$, which gives $[\mathrm{ES}]=S_T\,[\mathrm{E}]/(K_m+[\mathrm{E}])$.
Each cleavage removes one probe, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dS_T}{dt}=-k_{\rm cat}[\mathrm{ES}]=-\frac{k_{\rm cat}[\mathrm{E}]}{K_m+[\mathrm{E}]}\,S_T
  \;\approx\;-\frac{k_{\rm cat}}{K_m}\,[\mathrm{E}]\,S_T
  \quad\text{for }[\mathrm{E}]\ll K_m.
  \label{eq:mm}
\end{equation}
This is first-order in the probe. The condition is that the \emph{enzyme} concentration is small compared
with $K_m$: 30~nM against $K_m=1.3$~mM is a ratio of $2\times10^{-5}$. (The earlier version of this
report justified first-order kinetics by saying MMP-9 is ``in large excess over the surface probe''. That is
not the relevant condition: an enzyme is a catalyst, and what matters is that it is not depleted and does not
saturate the probes.) Two further requirements are met. The enzyme bound at any time is at most
$S_T\,[\mathrm{E}]/K_m\le\;\nProbesFmol\text{ fmol}\times2\times10^{-5}\approx10^{-3}$~fmol, negligible against the
enzyme in even 1~\textmu L of a 6~nM sample (6~fmol), so free and total enzyme concentrations are equal. And
the steady-state approximation requires the enzyme--probe complex to equilibrate faster than the probes are
depleted: equilibration takes about $1/(k_{-1}+k_{\rm cat})\le1/k_{\rm cat}\approx12$~ms, against depletion times of
minutes to hours (Eq.~\ref{eq:Fk_kin}).

\textbf{Cleavage fraction.} Including the surface efficiency, $k_{\rm eff}=\eta\,(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$
($\keffM$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ at $\eta=0.20$). Eq.~\eqref{eq:mm} is separable:
$dS_T/S_T=-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]\,dt$, so $S_T(T)=S_T(0)\,e^{-k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T}$, and with
$\Fk\equiv1-S_T(T)/S_T(0)$,
\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T)=1-e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T},\qquad
  t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]}.
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} At 30~nM and 30~min, $k_{\rm eff}[\text{MMP-9}]T=13500\times30\times10^{-9}\times1800=0.729$, so
$\Fk=1-e^{-0.729}=\fkChronThirty$\%. At 6~nM, $0.146$ and $\fkHealThirty$\%. The half-time of the probe is
\thalfHeal{} min at 6~nM and \thalfChron{} min at 30~nM.

\subsection{Detection limit}

A concentration is detected when the readout exceeds $z$ times its noise; the usual choice is $z=3$.
The smallest detectable cleavage fraction is $F_{\min}=z\,\sigma_F$, and inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_kin}
gives the lowest detectable concentration:
\begin{equation}
  c_{\rm LOD}=\frac{-\ln\bigl(1-z\,\sigma_F\bigr)}{k_{\rm eff}\,T}.
  \label{eq:lod}
\end{equation}
\emph{Worked example.} The earlier noise figure $\sigma_F=3.4$\% gives $F_{\min}=\fMinOld$\% and, at 30~min,
$c_{\rm LOD}=\lodOld$~nM, below the 6~nM boundary. That figure came from a ratio of four readings and is not applicable
to the MB-only readout (Part~F). The noise of the MB-only readout has not been measured. If the per-reading noise
is unchanged, $\sigma_F=\sigmaRelOld$\% and the limit is \lodMBonly~nM; this is conditional and unverified \FLAG. In general, to
detect the healing concentration (6~nM) in 30~min requires $\sigma_F<\sigmaMaxAtSix$\%, i.e.\ $\sigma_r<\sigmaRMaxAtSix$\% per reading
(Eq.~\ref{eq:sigmaF}). The full dependence on noise, time and $\eta$ is shown in \figref{fig:lod}.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the surface efficiency}

Since $\eta$ is unmeasured, its influence was examined across a log-uniform range 0.01 to 1
(\figref{fig:eta}). For $\eta$ below \etaMinHeal{} the healing concentration falls below the detection threshold at 30~min
(for $\sigma_F=3.4$\%); over the log-uniform range, \etaFrac\% of the values of $\eta$ give a 6~nM readout
above threshold at 30~min. At the central value, about \tNeedSix{} min of incubation is needed to detect 6~nM. Incubation
time is the easiest lever for a low $\eta$. Because $\eta$ multiplies the rate, an empirical calibration
of $\Fkrec$ against known MMP-9 concentrations on the fabricated electrode replaces the assumed $\eta$
directly, and is preferable to using the model's value.

    time needed to detect 6~nM against $\eta$.}

\subsection{Result}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_kin} converts concentration and time to cleavage fraction, with the solution rate constant
confirmed and a surface efficiency that is not. Whether the device can resolve healing from chronic samples
in about 30 minutes depends on two numbers not yet known: $\eta$ and the noise of the before/after readout.

\section{Part H: Sensitivity to the Uncertain Inputs}
\label{sec:sens}

\subsection{Overview}

\textbf{Question.} Which of the unconfirmed inputs matter, and for which prediction? \textbf{Answer.}
The \emph{absolute} current depends strongly on the tether geometry and coverage and is uncertain by
factors of several. The \emph{readout} of cleavage does not depend on any of them; it depends on the
assumptions A1 and A2 (Part~F), the noise, and $\eta$.

\subsection{Method}

Each input was varied over a stated range with the others held at their base values, and the predicted peak height at
10~Hz recomputed (\figref{fig:tornado}A and the table below). The ranges are: $\Grtot$ a factor of three
either way; area $\pm5$\%; frequency 10 to 20~Hz (the confirmed range); $d_{\rm PEG}$ from the ideal-chain to the
fully stretched value; $\beta$ 5 to 13~nm$^{-1}$ with the measured rate at 0.8~nm held fixed; $\kref$ a factor of 10 either way;
$\alpha$ 0.3 to 0.5; and the stricter wall model of Part~B.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Coverage and frequency} scale the current directly: a factor of three in $\Grtot$ or a doubling of
  frequency changes the peak by the same factor.
\item \textbf{The height of the PEG6 tip} is the most sensitive geometric input, because the rate constant falls tenfold per
  0.23~nm: a tip 0.7~nm higher than assumed removes most of the signal, while a lower one changes it little. The
  tunnelling decay constant and the choice of tether model matter at the tens-of-percent level. The
  anchoring rate $\kref$ and the transfer coefficient $\alpha$ hardly matter (Part~D).
\item \textbf{None of these} changes $\Fkrec$: they multiply the current before and after cleavage alike.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Result}

The quantities that most affect the device's usefulness are $\eta$, the noise, and the assumptions A1 and A2, and
none of them is constrained by the placeholder inputs that dominate the predicted current. The current the
model predicts (\peakNA~nA at 10~Hz) is therefore an estimate with an uncertainty of a large
factor, and it should not be quoted as a specification; the ratio-based readout and its logic are much
better founded.

\textbf{Bottom line.} The electrochemistry rests on standard Butler--Volmer theory and an exact solution, now evaluated
stably. Its central structural result, that the peak height is proportional to the number of intact probes, and hence
that $\Fkrec=1-\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$ is independent of coverage and of most of the uncertain geometry, does not
depend on the placeholders. What does depend on them is the absolute current, which is uncertain by a large factor and
which the model does not reproduce against the one published data set at high rate.

\textbf{What would settle the open questions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item Peak height and width against frequency on a fabricated electrode (Part~E): tests the rate distribution and the absolute-current prediction.
\item A single-component ferrocene scan and a scan of both labels together: fixes the ferrocene potential and shows whether the two are resolved (Part~F).
\item A blank sample (no MMP-9) incubated for the full time, with and without a ferrocene check: bounds non-enzymatic loss $\delta$ (A1).
\item A calibration of $\Fkrec$ against known MMP-9 concentrations, with a parallel solution assay: replaces the assumed $\eta$ and A2 (Part~G).
\item Repeat scans of one electrode: gives the readout noise $\sigma_r$ directly and hence the detection limit.
\item Surface coverage by an independent method, when available: fixes $\Grtot$.
\end{enumerate}
