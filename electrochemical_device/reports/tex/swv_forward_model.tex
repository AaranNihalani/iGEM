\noindent\textbf{What this document is.}
This is the working model of how our electrode turns MMP-9 activity into a
signal we can read on a potentiostat. It is written to be read start to
finish by anyone on the team, not just the person who built it: every
result is stated in plain language first, with the supporting equation
given but not re-derived line by line. Where a number comes from a
published paper it is marked \CONF. Where it is a working assumption,
not yet confirmed on our own electrodes, it is marked \FLAG\ and explained.

\begin{keyresult}
\textbf{Read this box first.} Several things this model used to assume have
turned out not to match what we actually see on the bench (Max Li, lab
notes, September 2026). This version corrects them. The headline changes:
there is no MCH backfill (Section~\ref{sec:sam}); the ferrocene reference
peak sits 100--150~mV positive of where the model expected
(Section~\ref{sec:ratio}); and we have not yet resolved two separate peaks
on a real scan at all, which is a real problem for the ratiometric strategy,
not a detail (Section~\ref{sec:ratio}). None of this breaks the model's
core logic, but the numbers and a few assumptions need to be treated
as open questions, not settled facts, until we test them directly.
\end{keyresult}

\section{The Electrode and What Happens to It}
\label{sec:story}

Our sensor is a small gold disc (1.5~mm across) carrying two kinds of
molecule stuck to its surface by a thiol (sulfur) anchor, Figure~\ref{fig:assem}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The signal probe.} A short peptide (SGKIPRTLTA) that MMP-9
  cuts, with a spacer (PEG6) holding it away from the gold, and a methylene
  blue (MB) dye stuck on the far end. MB can pass an electron to the gold and
  back, which is what we detect. When MMP-9 cuts the peptide, the MB-carrying
  half floats away and that probe goes electrically silent.
\item \textbf{The reference probe.} A short thiol carrying a ferrocene (Fc)
  group instead of MB. Fc is not cut by MMP-9 and gives its own, separate
  redox signal. We use its peak as an internal ruler to correct for how much
  probe actually ended up on a given electrode (Section~\ref{sec:ratio}).
\end{itemize}

The two probes are deposited together in \textbf{one pot} (Section~\ref{sec:sam}).
As MMP-9 activity increases, more MB is lost, and the MB peak in the
voltammogram shrinks. Measuring how much it shrinks over a known incubation
time is how we read out enzyme activity, and from there, estimate
[MMP-9] in the wound sample.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV0_assembly.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Left:} the two probe types on the gold surface --
    the MB-peptide (cleavable) and the Fc reference (not cleavable).
    \textbf{Right:} the range of distances the MB label can sit at above

\section{The Surface Chemistry: What's Actually There}
\label{sec:sam}

\textbf{This section replaces an earlier, incorrect description.} Previous
versions of this report and related layer reports described an
MCH backfill step, where a small inert molecule (6-mercapto-1-hexanol)
fills in gaps between the probes. \textbf{This is not what we do.} Per
Max Li's lab note (September 2026):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Both probes, MB-peptide and Fc-thiol, are deposited together in a
  \textbf{single one-pot reaction}. There is no separate MCH step, and no
  MCH at all.
\item Fc plays the role MCH used to play in older versions of this model:
  it dilutes the MB-peptide probe density, spacing the signal probes apart.
\item We do \textbf{not} know the resulting surface composition matches the
  ratio the two thiols were mixed in solution. The peptide probe is
  thermodynamically favoured to stick to bare gold over the Fc-thiol, so it
  may end up over-represented on the surface relative to what was mixed in.
  This is an open question \FLAG, not a design parameter we control precisely.
\item Because there is no MCH filling the remaining gaps, bare gold may be
  exposed between probes, which is a plausible route for non-specific protein
  adsorption from wound fluid (fouling). This was not a concern under the old
  MCH-backfill picture and needs its own check.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why this matters for the model.} The distance distribution $P(d)$
used in Section~\ref{sec:geom} was derived assuming a clean, PEG6-spaced
probe with nothing else nearby. It has not been re-derived or validated for
the real, MCH-free, competitively-adsorbed surface. Treat $P(d)$ as a
reasonable working shape, not a measured one.

\textbf{Surface coverage.} The model uses a total probe coverage of
$\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$. This is \textbf{not} a measured
value for our electrodes: it is an engineering placeholder in the range
reported for a similar Fc-peptide-PEG system elsewhere in the literature
\cit{8}. Our SAMs are not yet reproducible enough to trust a specific number
here \FLAG; it should be measured directly (e.g. by integrating the Fc peak
charge) once the one-pot protocol is finalised.

\section{Where Does the MB Label Actually Sit?}
\label{sec:geom}

The MB label is not rigidly fixed above the gold: it sits at the end of a
flexible tether (PEG6 spacer plus the peptide itself), so it wobbles around
in a range of distances. Molecules further from the gold transfer electrons
much more slowly (electron transfer through the tether decays roughly
exponentially with distance -- this is standard Marcus/tunnelling theory
\cit{9,10}), so only probes sitting close to the gold contribute meaningfully
to the signal.

Treating the tether as a flexible chain (a standard polymer-physics model,
the freely-jointed chain) gives a distance distribution that peaks right at
the PEG6 spacer length and falls off further out:
\[
  P(d) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
  \qquad d \geq d_{\rm PEG}
\]
with $d_{\rm PEG} \approx 14$~\AA\ (from the PEG6 spacer) and
$\sigma_z \approx 9.8$~\AA\ (from the peptide's flexibility). The
electron-transfer rate at the near end of that range works out to
$\kzero_{\rm ref} \approx 127$~s$^{-1}$ \FLAG\ (see Section~\ref{sec:sam}
caveat on $P(d)$).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV1_geometry.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} the distance distribution $P(d)$ -- most MB labels
    sit within about 1~nm of the PEG6 spacer tip. \textbf{B:} the
    electron-transfer rate $k^0(d)$ falls off fast with distance, so nearby

\section{The Core Model: One Equation}
\label{sec:core}

Each MB label switches between an oxidised and reduced state at a rate set
by the applied potential (Butler-Volmer kinetics, the standard model for
electrode reactions \cit{9}). Because MB is stuck to the surface rather than
floating free in solution, this reduces to a single, exactly solvable
equation for the fraction of MB in the reduced state, $\Grred$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} = k_f\,(\Grtot - \Grred) - k_b\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
This has a closed-form solution -- no numerical simulation needed --
because during each SWV pulse the potential (and therefore $k_f$, $k_b$) is
held fixed:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Grred(\tau) = \Greq + \bigl[\Grred(0) - \Greq\bigr]\,e^{-(k_f+k_b)\tau}\;}
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
In words: over each pulse, the MB coverage relaxes exponentially towards its
equilibrium value $\Greq$. If the pulse is short compared to the reaction
rate, almost nothing happens (weak signal). If it's long, the system fully
equilibrates before the next pulse and forward/backward currents cancel
(also weak signal). The useful signal comes from the middle ground, where
the coverage moves partway to equilibrium each pulse -- this is why there is
an optimal scan frequency (Fig.~\ref{fig:geom}D).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV3_ode.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} how the MB coverage relaxes during one forward-then-
    backward pulse pair, for three different reaction speeds. \textbf{B:}
    the three regimes -- too slow, just right, too fast. \textbf{C:} net
    signal vs.\ reaction speed, confirming the optimum. \textbf{D:} the
    algebra behind Eq.~\eqref{eq:soln} (an integrating-factor solution of
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode}), for reference.}
  \label{fig:ode}
\end{figure}

Scanning this across a full range of potentials, and adding up the
contribution from every MB label at every distance $d$ (weighted by
$P(d)$ from Section~\ref{sec:geom}), gives the full predicted voltammogram
$\DI(E)$ -- a peak centred near the MB formal potential, whose height
scales with how much intact probe remains on the electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) \;\propto\; (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
where $\Fk$ is the fraction of probe that has been cut by MMP-9. This
proportionality was checked numerically and holds to within 2\% across
the full range of $\Fk$ -- the peak shrinks in proportion to cleavage, and
its position and width barely move.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV4_scan.pdf}
  \caption{Predicted voltammograms scanning across the full potential range,
  for a fast, an intermediate, and a slow reaction speed (same three cases
  as Fig.~\ref{fig:ode}). The intermediate case gives the sharpest peak.}
  \label{fig:scan}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV5_averaging.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} near probes dominate the total signal; far probes
    contribute almost nothing. \textbf{B:} predicted voltammograms at
    several cleavage fractions $\Fk$ -- the peak shrinks but keeps its
    shape. \textbf{C:} peak area vs.\ $(1-\Fk)$, confirming the linear
    relationship of Eq.~\eqref{eq:DI_Fk}. \textbf{D:} peak position and
    width stay essentially constant across $\Fk$.}
  \label{fig:avg}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why SWV and not a simpler technique.} Square-wave voltammetry
samples the current only at the end of each pulse, by which point the
electrode's background charging current has died away, and then subtracts
the forward and backward readings. Both steps remove background signal
that a simpler technique (e.g.\ cyclic voltammetry) would keep, giving SWV
substantially better sensitivity for a surface-bound probe like ours
\cit{11}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV8_waveform.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} the SWV pulse sequence -- a staircase with a square
    wave superimposed, forward and backward currents sampled at the end of
    each half-pulse. \textbf{B:} background charging current dies away
    quickly; the real (Faradaic) signal persists, so sampling late removes
    background. \textbf{C:} SWV's sensitivity advantage over CV and DPV for
    a surface-bound probe.}
  \label{fig:wave}
\end{figure}

\section{Checking the Model Against Published Data}
\label{sec:valid}

Before trusting this model's predictions, we checked it against two
independent published results. In the limit of a very slow scan, the model
should reproduce the classical Laviron (1979) peak shape \cit{2}, which has
a known, exactly-solvable width; our simulator matches it to under 1\%.
At faster scan speeds it should match measurements from Dauphin-Ducharme
et al.\ (2017) \cit{1} on a comparable MB-on-gold system; our simulator's
predicted peak width matches their measured values to within 4~mV across
their tested frequency range. Both checks land in the same kinetic regime
our device operates in, which gives reasonable confidence in the ODE
solution itself, independent of the surface-chemistry open questions above.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV6_validation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A:} simulator vs.\ the exact Laviron (1979) peak shape at
  slow scan speed. \textbf{B:} the same comparison at three different

  widths from Dauphin-Ducharme et al.\ (2017) across their frequency range.
  \textbf{D:} peak width vs.\ reaction speed, showing where our device sits
  relative to the validated range.}

\end{figure}

\section{The Ratiometric Correction, and Why It's Currently an Open Problem}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{What the ratiometric correction is trying to do, in plain terms}

Every electrode we make ends up with a slightly different amount of probe on
it. If we just measured the MB peak area, a bigger MB peak might mean more
MMP-9 cleaved less probe, or it might just mean that electrode happened to
get more probe deposited in the first place. There's no way to tell those
apart from the MB signal alone.

The fix is to also measure the Fc peak on the same electrode, in the same
scan. Because Fc isn't cut by MMP-9, its peak size should track how much
total probe landed on that electrode, regardless of enzyme activity. Taking
the ratio $A_{\rm MB}/A_{\rm Fc}$ is meant to cancel out the electrode-to-
electrode variation and leave a number that depends only on how much MB was
cleaved:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \frac{A_{\rm MB}(T)/A_{\rm Fc}}{A_{\rm MB}(0)/A_{\rm Fc}}
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio}
\end{equation}
That's the whole idea: compare the MB peak to the Fc peak before and after
incubation, and any electrode-specific scaling factor cancels out of the
ratio.

\subsection{Why this is currently flagged, not trusted}

\textbf{This cancellation is an assumption, not a demonstrated fact for our
device.} Three separate problems have come up:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The Fc peak position doesn't match what the model expected.}
  We had modelled the Fc formal potential at $+0.210$~V vs.\ Ag/AgCl, taken
  from a comparable system in the literature \cit{1}. Max Li's actual
  measurements put it nearer \textbf{+0.300 to +0.350~V} \FLAG\ -- a
  100--150~mV shift. This isn't fatal on its own, but it means the
  literature anchor doesn't transfer cleanly to our chemistry, and the real
  value needs a dedicated single-component Fc scan to pin down.

\item \textbf{We have not actually seen two separate peaks.} The model
  assumes the MB and Fc peaks are well separated and can both be read off
  cleanly in one scan. \textbf{On real voltammograms, no distinct dual peak
  has been observed.} We don't yet know if this is because the peaks
  genuinely overlap, because the MB signal is too weak to see next to the
  Fc signal at current coverages, or something else in the protocol. Until
  this is resolved (e.g.\ with probe-only and Fc-only control scans, or a
  peak-fitting approach to separate overlapping peaks), the ratiometric
  correction as written cannot actually be applied to a real scan.

\item \textbf{The correlation the ratio depends on is unverified.} Equation
  \eqref{eq:Fk_ratio} only works if Fc coverage and MB coverage scale
  together electrode-to-electrode, since they're co-deposited. That's a
  reasonable assumption but has not been tested on our device, and
  preliminary data may not support it \FLAG. It should be tested directly:
  deliberately vary total probe loading and check whether the ratio still
  behaves as predicted.
\end{enumerate}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_SWV7_ratiometric.pdf}
  \caption{The ratiometric correction \emph{as modelled} (assuming clean
  peak separation and stable Fc coverage). \textbf{A:} predicted separate MB
  and Fc peaks -- \textbf{not yet observed on real scans, see text.}
  \textbf{B:} recovered vs.\ true cleavage fraction under this model.
  \textbf{C, D:} frequency and quadrature diagnostics for the underlying

  \label{fig:ratio}
\end{figure}

\subsection{A related concern: is a single calibration curve realistic?}

Separately from the ratiometric question, the current plan is to convert a
measured signal into [MMP-9] via a single calibration curve built from a
reference set of electrodes. This works well for a large, reusable
industrial electrode, where the same physical surface is measured
repeatedly and stays consistent. Our electrodes are small and
single-use, and electrode-to-electrode variation in probe coverage is
already a known issue (Section~\ref{sec:sam}). \textbf{A single fixed
calibration curve may not be robust to that variation} \FLAG\ -- this is a
design risk worth testing directly (e.g.\ checking how much the calibration
curve shifts across a batch of nominally identical electrodes) rather than
assuming the ratiometric correction alone will handle it.

\section{From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

At the enzyme concentrations expected in a wound sample (roughly 6--30~nM),
MMP-9 is present in large excess over the surface probe, so the cleavage
fraction follows simple first-order kinetics:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T) = 1 - e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
where $k_{\rm eff} = \eta \times (k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$, and
$(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol} = 67{,}500$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ is the measured
solution-phase cleavage rate for this exact peptide \cit{7} \CONF. The
factor $\eta$ corrects for the fact that cleavage on a surface is usually
slower than in free solution (steric hindrance, orientation effects); we
use $\eta = 0.20$ as a central estimate \FLAG, still to be measured on our
device (e.g.\ by comparing electrode readout to a parallel solution-phase
fluorescence assay).

\begin{center}
\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Wound state & [MMP-9] & $\Fk$ at 30~min ($\eta=0.20$) & Source \\
\midrule
Healing & 6~nM & 14\% & Trengove 1999 \cit{7b} \\
Chronic & 30~nM & 53\% & Trengove 1999 \cit{7b} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At the $3\sigma$ detection criterion, this gives a predicted limit of
detection around \textbf{4~nM} at 30 minutes, below the 6~nM healing/chronic
boundary -- \emph{provided} the ratiometric noise floor used in that
calculation ($\sim$3\%) is achievable, which depends on resolving the
dual-peak issue in Section~\ref{sec:ratio} first.

\section{Summary: What's Solid, What's Assumed, What's Open}

\textbf{Bottom line.} The core electrochemistry (Sections~\ref{sec:core}
and \ref{sec:valid}) rests on standard, validated theory and isn't in
question. What needs direct experimental attention before the device can be
trusted end to end is everything to do with the real surface: what's
actually on it (Section~\ref{sec:sam}), whether the two reporter peaks can
even be told apart (Section~\ref{sec:ratio}), and whether a single
calibration curve is the right strategy for electrodes this small and this
variable (Section~\ref{sec:ratio}).
