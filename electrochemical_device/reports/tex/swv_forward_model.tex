\noindent\textbf{What this document is.}
This is the working model of how our electrode turns MMP-9 activity into a
signal we can read on a potentiostat. It is organised as six self-contained
parts, one per physical step in the chain from probe to peak. Each part
follows the same pattern: what it does, in plain language; what we're
assuming to get there, stated explicitly and justified; the equation, with
its physical meaning given before it appears, not after; the parameters it
needs; and the result. Where a number comes from a published paper it is
marked \CONF. Where it is a working assumption, not yet confirmed on our
own electrodes, it is marked \FLAG.

\section{Overview: The Electrode and What Happens to It}
\label{sec:story}

Our sensor is a small gold electrode with a geometric area of
1.7~mm$^2$ (0.017~cm$^2$) \cit{12} \CONF -- confirmed against the
fabricated device, superseding an earlier 1.5~mm-diameter design spec
(0.0177~cm$^2$), a $\sim$4\% difference too small to change any
qualitative conclusion in this report. It carries two kinds of molecule
stuck to its surface by a thiol (sulfur) anchor, Figure~\ref{fig:assem}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The signal probe.} A short peptide (SGKIPRTLTA) that MMP-9
  cuts, with a spacer (PEG6) holding it away from the gold, and a methylene
  blue (MB) dye stuck on the far end. MB can pass an electron to the gold and
  back, which is what we detect. When MMP-9 cuts the peptide, the MB-carrying
  half floats away and that probe goes electrically silent.
\item \textbf{The reference probe.} A short thiol carrying a ferrocene (Fc)
  group instead of MB. Fc is not cut by MMP-9 and gives its own, separate
  redox signal. We use its peak as an internal ruler to correct for how much
  probe actually ended up on a given electrode (Part~E).
\end{itemize}

The two probes are deposited together in \textbf{one pot} (Part~B). As
MMP-9 activity increases, more MB is lost, and the MB peak in the
voltammogram shrinks. Measuring how much it shrinks over a known incubation
time is how we read out enzyme activity, and from there, estimate
[MMP-9] in the wound sample.

The document is organised as six parts, each covering one physical step in
that chain:

\section{Part A: The Surface Chemistry}
\label{sec:sam}

\subsection{Overview}

Before anything electrochemical can be modelled, we need to know what is
physically on the electrode. \textbf{This section replaces an earlier,
incorrect description.} Previous versions of this report and related layer
reports described an MCH backfill step, where a small inert molecule
(6-mercapto-1-hexanol) fills in gaps between the probes. \textbf{This is
not what we do.} The current protocol \cit{12} co-deposits both probes,
MB-peptide and Fc-thiol, together in a single one-pot reaction. There is no
separate MCH step, and no MCH at all. Fc plays the role MCH used to play in
older versions of this model: it dilutes the MB-peptide probe density,
spacing the signal probes apart.

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{One-pot, two-component deposition (no MCH).} Confirmed
  protocol \cit{12}; this is a fact about the fabrication process, not a
  modelling choice.
\item \textbf{Surface composition may not match the solution mixing ratio.}
  The peptide probe is thermodynamically favoured to stick to bare gold
  over the Fc-thiol, so it may end up over-represented on the surface
  relative to what was mixed in solution \FLAG. This is an open question,
  not a design parameter we control precisely, and has not been measured.
\item \textbf{Total probe coverage $\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$.}
  This is \emph{not} a measured value for our electrodes: it is an
  engineering placeholder in the range reported for a comparable
  Fc-peptide-PEG system in the literature \cit{8} \FLAG. This is not
  measurable with the instrumentation currently available to the team
  \cit{12}, so it remains a placeholder for the foreseeable future rather
  than a near-term fix; any prediction that depends sensitively on the
  exact value of $\Grtot$ should be treated with that in mind.
\item \textbf{Bare gold exposure between probes.} Because there is no MCH
  filling the remaining surface, bare gold may be exposed between probes,
  which is a plausible route for non-specific protein adsorption from
  wound fluid (fouling). This was not a concern under the old MCH-backfill
  picture and has not been checked \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\textbf{Why this matters downstream.} The distance distribution $P(d)$
used in Part~\ref{sec:geom} was derived assuming a clean, PEG6-spaced probe
with nothing else nearby. It has not been re-derived or validated for the
real, MCH-free, competitively-adsorbed surface described above. Treat
$P(d)$ as a reasonable working shape, not a measured one.

\section{Part B: Where Does the MB Label Sit?}
\label{sec:geom}

\subsection{Overview}

The MB label is not rigidly fixed above the gold: it sits at the end of a
flexible tether (PEG6 spacer plus the peptide itself), so it wobbles around
in a range of distances. Molecules further from the gold transfer electrons
much more slowly (electron transfer through the tether decays roughly
exponentially with distance -- this is standard Marcus/tunnelling theory
\cit{9,10}), so only probes sitting close to the gold contribute meaningfully
to the signal.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{The tether behaves as a freely-jointed chain (FJC).} This is
  a standard polymer-physics model for a flexible, non-rigid linker, used
  here for the peptide segment of the tether. It is a modelling choice, not
  a measurement on our specific peptide.
\item \textbf{This geometry has not been validated on the real (MCH-free)
  surface.} See Part~\ref{sec:sam}: the calculation below assumes a clean,
  isolated PEG6-spaced probe, which may not hold once competitive
  peptide/Fc adsorption is accounted for \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equation and parameters}

Treating the tether as an FJC gives a distance distribution that peaks
right at the PEG6 spacer length and falls off further out:
\[
  P(d) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
  \qquad d \geq d_{\rm PEG}
\]
with $d_{\rm PEG} \approx 14$~\AA\ (from the PEG6 spacer) and
$\sigma_z \approx 9.8$~\AA\ (from the peptide's flexibility). The
electron-transfer rate at the near end of that range works out to
$\kzero_{\rm ref} \approx 127$~s$^{-1}$ \FLAG\ (carries the same $P(d)$
caveat above). At the team's confirmed operating frequency of 10--20~Hz
\cit{12}, this gives $\kappa_{\rm tip} = \kzero_{\rm ref}/(2f) = 6.4$ at
10~Hz or $3.2$ at 20~Hz -- both comfortably in the quasi-reversible range
(Part~\ref{sec:core}) where signal is strongest, though the exact value
depends on which frequency is actually used for a given scan. (An earlier
version of this report used an assumed 15~Hz, giving $\kappa_{\rm
tip}=4.2$; this has been superseded by the confirmed frequency.)

\section{Part C: The Core Signal Model}
\label{sec:core}

\subsection{Overview}

Each MB label switches between an oxidised and reduced state at a rate set
by the applied potential (Butler-Volmer kinetics, the standard model for
electrode reactions \cit{9}). Because MB is stuck to the surface rather than
floating free in solution, this reduces to a single, exactly solvable
equation -- no numerical simulation needed -- for the fraction of MB in the
reduced state.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Butler-Volmer kinetics apply to surface-confined MB.} This is
  the standard, textbook model for a redox species attached to an
  electrode, not specific to our device, and is not in question here.
\item \textbf{The potential is held fixed within each SWV half-pulse.} This
  is true by construction of the SWV waveform (Part~\ref{sec:wave}) and is
  what makes the equation below exactly solvable.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations}

The rate of change of reduced-MB coverage $\Grred$ follows:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} = k_f\,(\Grtot - \Grred) - k_b\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
Because $k_f$, $k_b$ are constant during a half-pulse, this has a
closed-form solution:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Grred(\tau) = \Greq + \bigl[\Grred(0) - \Greq\bigr]\,e^{-(k_f+k_b)\tau}\;}
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
In words: over each pulse, the MB coverage relaxes exponentially towards its
equilibrium value $\Greq$. If the pulse is short compared to the reaction
rate, almost nothing happens (weak signal). If it's long, the system fully
equilibrates before the next pulse and forward/backward currents cancel
(also weak signal). The useful signal comes from the middle ground, where
the coverage moves partway to equilibrium each pulse -- this is why there is
an optimal scan frequency (Fig.~\ref{fig:geom}D).

Scanning this across a full range of potentials, and adding up the
contribution from every MB label at every distance $d$ (weighted by $P(d)$
from Part~\ref{sec:geom}), gives the full predicted voltammogram $\DI(E)$ --
a peak centred near the MB formal potential, whose height scales with how
much intact probe remains on the electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) \;\propto\; (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
where $\Fk$ is the fraction of probe that has been cut by MMP-9. This
proportionality was checked numerically and holds to within 2\% across
the full range of $\Fk$ -- the peak shrinks in proportion to cleavage, and
its position and width barely move.

\subsection{Validation against published data}

Before trusting this model's predictions, we checked it against two
independent published results. In the limit of a very slow scan, the model
should reproduce the classical Laviron (1979) peak shape \cit{2}, which has
a known, exactly-solvable width; our simulator matches it to under 1\%.
At faster scan speeds it should match measurements from Dauphin-Ducharme
et al.\ (2017) \cit{1} on a comparable MB-on-gold system; our simulator's
predicted peak width matches their measured values to within 4~mV across
their tested frequency range. Both checks land in the same kinetic regime
our device operates in, which gives reasonable confidence in the ODE
solution itself, independent of the surface-chemistry open questions in
Parts~\ref{sec:sam}--\ref{sec:geom}.

\section{Part D: Why Square-Wave Voltammetry}
\label{sec:wave}

\subsection{Overview}

Square-wave voltammetry samples the current only at the end of each pulse,
by which point the electrode's background charging current has died away,
and then subtracts the forward and backward readings. Both steps remove
background signal that a simpler technique (e.g.\ cyclic voltammetry)
would keep, giving SWV substantially better sensitivity for a
surface-bound probe like ours \cit{11}.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{SWV's sensitivity advantage over CV/DPV for surface-bound
  probes is standard electroanalytical theory} \cit{11}, not specific to
  our device.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Operating parameters}

For completeness, the specific SWV settings this model uses: pulse
amplitude $\Esw = 25$~mV \cit{6,12} \CONF\ (matches the Baker 2006
literature value exactly, and confirmed against the fabricated device and
potentiostat settings); operating frequency 10--20~Hz \cit{12} \CONF;
electrode area 1.7~mm$^2$ (Overview) \CONF; transfer coefficient
$\alpha = 0.37$ and $n_e = 2$ for MB \cit{1} \CONF; $\alpha = 0.5$ and
$n_e = 1$ for Fc, the standard textbook assumption for a fast, symmetric
one-electron couple rather than a measured value for this specific
construct.

\subsection{Result}

\section{Part E: The Ratiometric Correction}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{Overview: what it's trying to do, in plain terms}

Every electrode we make ends up with a slightly different amount of probe on
it. If we just measured the MB signal, a bigger MB peak might mean more
MMP-9 cleaved less probe, or it might just mean that electrode happened to
get more probe deposited in the first place. There's no way to tell those
apart from the MB signal alone.

The fix is to also measure the Fc peak on the same electrode, in the same
scan. Because Fc isn't cut by MMP-9, its peak size should track how much
total probe landed on that electrode, regardless of enzyme activity. Taking
the ratio of the two peaks is meant to cancel out the electrode-to-electrode
variation and leave a number that depends only on how much MB was cleaved.
That's the whole idea: compare the MB peak to the Fc peak before and after
incubation, and any electrode-specific scaling factor cancels out of the
ratio.

\subsection{Equation}

\begin{equation}
  \Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}}
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm MB}$ and $\DI_{\rm Fc}$ are the net SWV peak \emph{heights}
(the $\DI_{\rm peak}$ quantity used throughout Parts~\ref{sec:core}--\ref{sec:wave}),
not the integrated peak area used in earlier versions of this report -- see
the first assumption below for why that change matters.

\subsection{Assumptions -- and why this is currently an open problem, not a
trusted result}

\textbf{This cancellation is an assumption, not a demonstrated fact for our
device.} Five separate problems have come up:

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Peak height, not peak area, is the right quantity here --
  and even height isn't fully clean.} Earlier versions of this report
  defined the ratio using integrated peak \emph{area} (height times width),
  by analogy with CV, where integrated charge is a physically meaningful
  quantity directly tied to coverage by Faraday's law. That analogy doesn't
  hold for SWV: the net SWV peak has no equivalent charge interpretation,
  and its width tracks the kinetic regime $\kappa$ (Part~\ref{sec:core}),
  not coverage. On small, low-reproducibility SAM electrodes, fouling can
  change the electron-transfer kinetics at the Fc reference itself, not
  just the amount of Fc present -- which distorts peak \emph{shape}
  (width) independently of coverage. An area-based ratio cannot
  distinguish "coverage changed" from "kinetics/shape changed due to
  fouling," so it silently assumes fouling only ever scales peak
  amplitude. Peak height is closer to the standard SWV observable and
  more directly tied to coverage at the peak potential, but it is still
  affected by $\kappa$, so it is not immune to this problem either --
  distinguishing a real coverage change from a fouling-driven kinetics
  shift requires comparing height \emph{and} width separately, not folding
  them into one number \FLAG. This has not yet been checked on our
  electrodes.
\item \textbf{The Fc peak position doesn't match what the model expected.}
  We had modelled the Fc formal potential at $+0.210$~V vs.\ Ag/AgCl, taken
  from a comparable system in the literature \cit{1}. Our own electrode
  measurements put it nearer \textbf{+0.300 to +0.400~V} \cit{12} \FLAG\ -- a
  90--190~mV shift, and the value itself varies electrode-to-electrode
  rather than sitting at one fixed potential. This isn't fatal on its own,
  but it means the literature anchor doesn't transfer cleanly to our
  chemistry, and the real value needs a dedicated single-component Fc scan
  to pin down more precisely.
\item \textbf{The borrowed Fc rate constant likely does not apply to this
  device either.} $k^0_{\rm Fc} = 2.7\times10^5$~s$^{-1}$ was borrowed from
  a different construct (Dauphin-Ducharme 2017, MB-DNA electrode), the same
  way $k^0_{\rm ref}$ for MB was. At that rate and the team's confirmed
  operating frequency (10--20~Hz, not the 15~Hz this model previously
  assumed \cit{12}), $\kappa_{\rm Fc}$ works out to 6{,}750--13{,}500 --
  so far into the reversible limit that the BV-ODE (Part~\ref{sec:core})
  predicts an essentially zero net SWV signal, mathematically, regardless
  of coverage. That contradicts direct observation: a real Fc peak has
  been seen experimentally, with height varying from
  $5\times10^{-8}$ to $10^{-5}$~A and width varying substantially between
  scans \cit{12} \FLAG.

  Back-calculating from the worst-case observed height
  ($5\times10^{-8}$~A) at $f=20$~Hz, using the model's own internal Fc
  coverage estimate $\Gamma_{\rm Fc}=1.5\times10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$ and
  the now-confirmed electrode area (1.7~mm$^2$) and SWV amplitude
  (25~mV), gives an optimal $k^0_{\rm Fc} \approx 23$~s$^{-1}$
  ($\kappa_{\rm Fc}\approx0.6$, near the quasi-reversible optimum) --
  roughly four orders of magnitude slower than the borrowed value. At
  that corrected $k^0_{\rm Fc}$ and the current coverage assumption, the
  model cannot reach the upper end of the observed range
  ($10^{-5}$~A) at \emph{any} $k^0$: coverage, not kinetics, sets a hard
  ceiling on peak height, and $10^{-5}$~A is roughly 400$\times$
  above that ceiling. One coherent (but unconfirmed) explanation is that
  real Fc coverage on a well-formed SAM is $\sim$400$\times$ higher
  than the current placeholder -- which would also help explain the
  $\sim$100$\times$ electrode-to-electrode signal variation already noted
  above, if coverage varies similarly. \textbf{This is still not
  confirmed}: it depends on whether the observed heights were genuinely
  isolated Fc peaks rather than a combined or contaminated signal, which
  has not been checked. Electrode area and SWV amplitude are no longer
  open questions here (both confirmed above), so the remaining uncertainty
  is narrower than before, but this should still be treated as a specific,
  testable hypothesis, not a corrected parameter.
\item \textbf{We have not actually seen two separate peaks.} The model
  assumes the MB and Fc peaks are well separated and can both be read off
  cleanly in one scan. \textbf{On real voltammograms, no distinct dual peak
  has been observed} \FLAG. We don't yet know if this is because the peaks
  genuinely overlap, because the MB signal is too weak to see next to the
  Fc signal at current coverages, or something else in the protocol. Until
  this is resolved (e.g.\ with probe-only and Fc-only control scans, or a
  peak-fitting approach to separate overlapping peaks), the ratiometric
  correction as written cannot actually be applied to a real scan.
\item \textbf{The correlation the ratio depends on is unverified.} Equation
  \eqref{eq:Fk_ratio} only works if Fc coverage and MB coverage scale
  together electrode-to-electrode, since they're co-deposited. That's a
  reasonable assumption but has not been tested on our device, and
  preliminary data may not support it \FLAG. It should be tested directly:
  deliberately vary total probe loading and check whether the ratio still
  behaves as predicted.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{A related concern: is a single calibration curve realistic?}

Separately from the ratiometric question, the current plan is to convert a
measured signal into [MMP-9] via a single calibration curve built from a
reference set of electrodes. This works well for a large, reusable
industrial electrode, where the same physical surface is measured
repeatedly and stays consistent. Our electrodes are small and single-use,
and electrode-to-electrode variation in probe coverage is already a known
issue (Part~\ref{sec:sam}): measured signal amplitude has varied by up to
a factor of 100 between nominally identical electrodes \cit{12}.
\textbf{A single fixed calibration curve is very unlikely to be robust to
variation of this size} \FLAG. This is the strongest argument in this
report for relying on the ratiometric correction (Eq.~\ref{eq:Fk_ratio})
rather than a fixed calibration curve, once the dual-peak problem above is
resolved -- a within-electrode ratio is far less exposed to
electrode-to-electrode variation than a curve built from a separate
reference batch.

\section{Part F: From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Overview}

At the enzyme concentrations expected in a wound sample (roughly
6--30~nM), MMP-9 is present in large excess over the surface probe, so the
cleavage fraction follows simple first-order kinetics.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{MMP-9 is in large excess over surface probe, so cleavage is
  pseudo-first-order.} Consistent with clinical concentration ranges
  (6--30~nM) against a surface probe density orders of magnitude lower.
\item \textbf{Surface efficiency $\eta = 0.20$.} A central estimate, not a
  measured value for our device \FLAG. It corrects for cleavage on a
  surface typically being slower than in free solution (steric hindrance,
  orientation effects), and still needs to be measured directly (e.g.\ by
  comparing electrode readout to a parallel solution-phase fluorescence
  assay).
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equation and parameters}

\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T) = 1 - e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
where $k_{\rm eff} = \eta \times (k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$, and
$(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol} = 67{,}500$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ is the measured
solution-phase cleavage rate for this exact peptide \cit{7} \CONF.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{center}
\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Wound state & [MMP-9] & $\Fk$ at 30~min ($\eta=0.20$) & Source \\
\midrule
Healing & 6~nM & 14\% & Trengove 1999 \cit{7b} \\
Chronic & 30~nM & 53\% & Trengove 1999 \cit{7b} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At the $3\sigma$ detection criterion, this gives a predicted limit of
detection around \textbf{4~nM} at 30 minutes, below the 6~nM healing/chronic
boundary -- \emph{provided} the ratiometric noise floor used in that
calculation ($\sim$3\%) is achievable, which depends on resolving the
dual-peak issue in Part~\ref{sec:ratio} first.
