\noindent\textbf{What this document is.}
This is the working model of how our electrode turns MMP-9 activity into a
signal we can read on a potentiostat. It is organised as six self-contained
parts, one per physical step in the chain from probe to peak. Each part
follows the same pattern: what it does, in plain language; the assumptions
required to reach that result, stated explicitly and justified; the equation, with
its physical meaning given before it appears, not after; the parameters it
needs; and the result. Where a number comes from a published paper it is
marked \CONF. Where it is a working assumption, not yet confirmed on our
own electrodes, it is marked \FLAG.

\section{Overview: Electrode Structure and Operating Principle}
\label{sec:story}

Our sensor is a small gold electrode with a geometric area of
1.7~mm$^2$ (0.017~cm$^2$) \cit{12} \CONF, superseding an earlier
1.5~mm-diameter design spec (0.0177~cm$^2$), a $\sim$4\% difference too
small to change any qualitative conclusion in this report. It carries a
single probe type
attached to its surface via a thiol (sulfur) anchor, Figure~\ref{fig:assem}:
a short peptide (SGKIPRTLTA) that MMP-9 cleaves, with a spacer (PEG6) holding
it away from the gold, and a methylene blue (MB) dye conjugated to the far
end. MB is capable of transferring an electron to and from the gold
surface, which constitutes the measured signal. When MMP-9 cleaves the
peptide, the MB-bearing fragment is released into solution and the probe
becomes electrochemically silent. \textbf{An earlier version of this
device also carried a ferrocene (Fc) reference probe alongside MB, used
for a ratiometric correction; Fc has since been removed from the protocol
entirely} \cit{12} -- see Part~E for the rationale and its consequences.

As MMP-9 activity increases, more MB is lost, and the MB peak in the
voltammogram shrinks. Measuring how much it shrinks over a known incubation
time, on the same electrode before and after incubation, is how we read
out enzyme activity, and from there, estimate [MMP-9] in the wound sample.

The document is organised as six parts, each covering one physical step in
that chain:

\section{Part A: The Surface Chemistry}
\label{sec:sam}

\subsection{Overview}

Before anything electrochemical can be modelled, we need to know what is
physically on the electrode. \textbf{This section replaces an earlier,
incorrect description.} Previous versions of this report and related layer
reports described an MCH backfill step, where a small inert molecule
(6-mercapto-1-hexanol) fills in gaps between the probes, and a
two-component deposition alongside a ferrocene (Fc) reference probe.
\textbf{Neither is what we do now.} The current protocol \cit{12} deposits
a single probe type, MB-peptide only, with no MCH and no Fc.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Single-component deposition (no MCH, no Fc).} Confirmed
  protocol \cit{12}; this is a fact about the fabrication process, not a
  modelling choice.
\item \textbf{Total probe coverage $\Grtot = 3\times10^{-12}$~mol~cm$^{-2}$.}
  This is \emph{not} a measured value for our electrodes: it is an
  engineering placeholder in the range reported for a comparable
  peptide-PEG system in the literature \cit{8} \FLAG. This is not
  measurable with the instrumentation currently available to the team
  \cit{12}, so it remains a placeholder for the foreseeable future rather
  than a near-term fix; any prediction that depends sensitively on the
  exact value of $\Grtot$ should be treated with that in mind.
\item \textbf{Bare gold exposure between probes.} With a single probe type
  and no MCH filling the remaining surface, bare gold is likely exposed
  between probes, which is a plausible route for non-specific protein
  adsorption from wound fluid (fouling). This has not been checked \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\textbf{Why this matters downstream.} The distance distribution $P(d)$
used in Part~\ref{sec:geom} was derived assuming a clean, PEG6-spaced probe
with nothing else nearby. It has not been re-derived or validated for the
real, MCH-free surface described above. Treat $P(d)$ as a reasonable
working shape, not a measured one.

\section{Part B: Position of the MB Label Above the Electrode}
\label{sec:geom}

\subsection{Overview}

The MB label is not rigidly fixed above the gold: it sits at the end of a
flexible tether (PEG6 spacer plus the peptide itself), and consequently
samples a range of distances. Molecules further from the gold transfer electrons
much more slowly (electron transfer through the tether decays roughly
exponentially with distance -- this is standard Marcus/tunnelling theory
\cit{9,10}), so only probes sitting close to the gold contribute meaningfully
to the signal.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{The tether behaves as a freely-jointed chain (FJC).} This is
  a standard polymer-physics model for a flexible, non-rigid linker, used
  here for the peptide segment of the tether. It is a modelling choice, not
  a measurement on our specific peptide.
\item \textbf{This geometry has not been validated on the real (MCH-free)
  surface.} See Part~\ref{sec:sam}: the calculation below assumes a clean,
  isolated PEG6-spaced probe, which has not been directly confirmed on the
  fabricated electrode \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equation and parameters}

Treating the tether as an FJC gives a distance distribution that peaks
right at the PEG6 spacer length and falls off further out:
\[
  P(d) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(d-d_{\rm PEG})^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
  \qquad d \geq d_{\rm PEG}
\]
with $d_{\rm PEG} \approx 14$~\AA\ (from the PEG6 spacer) and
$\sigma_z \approx 9.8$~\AA\ (from the peptide's flexibility). The
electron-transfer rate at the near end of that range works out to
$\kzero_{\rm ref} \approx 127$~s$^{-1}$ \FLAG\ (carries the same $P(d)$
caveat above). At the team's confirmed operating frequency of 10--20~Hz
\cit{12}, this gives $\kappa_{\rm tip} = \kzero_{\rm ref}/(2f) = 6.4$ at
10~Hz or $3.2$ at 20~Hz -- both comfortably in the quasi-reversible range
(Part~\ref{sec:core}) where signal is strongest, though the exact value
depends on which frequency is actually used for a given scan. (An earlier
version of this report used an assumed 15~Hz, giving $\kappa_{\rm
tip}=4.2$; this has been superseded by the confirmed frequency.)

\section{Part C: The Core Signal Model}
\label{sec:core}

\subsection{Overview}

Each MB label switches between an oxidised and reduced state at a rate set
by the applied potential (Butler-Volmer kinetics, the standard model for
electrode reactions \cit{9}). Because MB is confined to the surface rather
than diffusing freely in solution, this reduces to a single, exactly solvable
equation -- no numerical simulation needed -- for the fraction of MB in the
reduced state.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Butler-Volmer kinetics apply to surface-confined MB.} This is
  the standard, textbook model for a redox species attached to an
  electrode, not specific to our device, and is not in question here.
\item \textbf{The potential is held fixed within each SWV half-pulse.} This
  is true by construction of the SWV waveform (Part~\ref{sec:wave}) and is
  what makes the equation below exactly solvable.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equations}

The rate of change of reduced-MB coverage $\Grred$ follows:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Grred}{dt} = k_f\,(\Grtot - \Grred) - k_b\,\Grred
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
Because $k_f$, $k_b$ are constant during a half-pulse, this has a
closed-form solution:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Grred(\tau) = \Greq + \bigl[\Grred(0) - \Greq\bigr]\,e^{-(k_f+k_b)\tau}\;}
  \label{eq:soln}
\end{equation}
In words: over each pulse, the MB coverage relaxes exponentially towards its
equilibrium value $\Greq$. If the pulse is short compared to the reaction
rate, almost nothing happens (weak signal). If it is long, the system fully
equilibrates before the next pulse and forward/backward currents cancel
(also weak signal). The useful signal comes from the middle ground, where
the coverage moves partway to equilibrium each pulse -- this is why there is
an optimal scan frequency (Fig.~\ref{fig:geom}D).

Scanning this across a full range of potentials, and adding up the
contribution from every MB label at every distance $d$ (weighted by $P(d)$
from Part~\ref{sec:geom}), gives the full predicted voltammogram $\DI(E)$ --
a peak centred near the MB formal potential, whose height scales with how
much intact probe remains on the electrode:
\begin{equation}
  \DI(E;\Fk) \;\propto\; (1-\Fk)\,\Grtot
  \label{eq:DI_Fk}
\end{equation}
where $\Fk$ is the fraction of probe that has been cut by MMP-9. This
proportionality was checked numerically and holds to within 2\% across
the full range of $\Fk$ -- the peak shrinks in proportion to cleavage, and
its position and width barely move.

\subsection{Validation against published data}

This subsection was rewritten after a direct audit found that an earlier
version overstated what had actually been checked -- specifically, a
claimed "under 1\%" match to the Laviron (1979) shape and a claimed
"within 4~mV" match to Dauphin-Ducharme (2017) both turned out to have no
real calculation behind them (one was a hardcoded placeholder, the other
compared our own simulator's output to itself). What follows is what is
actually true, checked directly against both papers.

\textbf{Laviron (1979) \cit{2}.} The FWHM formula used throughout this
report, $3.53\,RT/(n_eF) = 45.3$~mV at $n_e=2$, is Laviron's own
closed-form result (his Eq.~6, $|\Psi|=\eta/(1+\eta)^2$, width at
mid-height $90.6/n$ at 25\textdegree C) -- confirmed by direct reading of
the paper, and used here exactly as published, not re-derived by our
simulator. What is \emph{not} established is that our finite-$\kappa$
BV-ODE simulator reproduces the full \emph{shape} of that curve by
extrapolation. It should not be expected to: Laviron's formula describes an
idealised continuously-swept, always-at-equilibrium system, not a
discretely-pulsed SWV protocol sampled at one instant per half-cycle.
Checking this directly, the simulator's shape differs from Laviron's by a
large margin (24\% RMS at $\kappa=10$) and does not converge towards it as
$\kappa$ increases -- if anything, the simulator becomes numerically
unstable above $\kappa\approx15$ (peak position and shape become erratic,
a floating-point cancellation artefact in the $I_f - I_b$ subtraction at
high $\kappa$, not a real physical prediction). \textbf{This does not
threaten our own predictions}: MB's operating $\kappa_{\rm tip}$ is
3.2--6.4 (Part~\ref{sec:geom}), safely inside the range where the
simulator is well-behaved -- but a quantitative shape match to the
Laviron limit itself is not something this report can currently claim.

\textbf{Dauphin-Ducharme et al.\ (2017) \cit{1}.} This paper directly
characterises methylene blue tethered to gold via a hexanethiol linker
(Figure 4 in that paper) -- a genuinely comparable system, general
chemistry aside from the specific tether -- and reports $k^0 = 1.5\pm0.5$~cm~s$^{-1}$,
converted in their own text to $k^0_{x=8\text{\AA}} = 5.0(\pm1.7)\times10^4$~s$^{-1}$,
with $\alpha=0.37\pm0.02$: both numbers confirmed by direct reading of the
paper. An earlier version of this model's code had transcribed this as
$5\times10^3$~s$^{-1}$ (misattributed to a different figure in the same
paper) -- a real 10$\times$ error, now fixed. However, at their reported
$k^0$ and their operating frequencies (20--1000~Hz), $\kappa$ is of order
$10$--$10^3$ -- squarely in the numerically unstable regime identified
above. \textbf{A quantitative comparison to their data is therefore not
currently possible}, not because the paper is not relevant (it is directly
relevant) but because our simulator cannot yet be trusted at the $\kappa$
their measurements operate at. Resolving this would need a numerically
stable reformulation of the high-$\kappa$ current calculation -- real
work, not yet attempted, and flagged here rather than papered over.

\section{Part D: Rationale for Square-Wave Voltammetry}
\label{sec:wave}

\subsection{Overview}

Square-wave voltammetry samples the current only at the end of each pulse,
by which point the electrode's background charging current has died away,
and then subtracts the forward and backward readings. Both steps remove
background signal that a simpler technique (e.g.\ cyclic voltammetry)
would keep, giving SWV substantially better sensitivity for a
surface-bound probe like ours \cit{11}.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{SWV's sensitivity advantage over CV/DPV for surface-bound
  probes is standard electroanalytical theory} \cit{11}, not specific to
  our device.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Operating parameters}

For completeness, the specific SWV settings this model uses: pulse
amplitude $\Esw = 25$~mV \cit{6,12} \CONF\ (matches the Baker 2006
literature value exactly, and confirmed against the fabricated device and
potentiostat settings); operating frequency 10--20~Hz \cit{12} \CONF;
electrode area 1.7~mm$^2$ (Overview) \CONF; transfer coefficient
$\alpha = 0.37$ and $n_e = 2$ for MB \cit{1} \CONF.

\subsection{Result}

\section{Part E: Removal of the Reference Reporter and Its Implications}
\label{sec:ratio}

\subsection{Protocol Change: Removal of Fc}

Earlier versions of this model included a ferrocene (Fc) reference probe
co-deposited alongside the MB-peptide, used to build a ratiometric
correction intended to cancel electrode-to-electrode variation. \textbf{The
team has since removed Fc from the protocol entirely} \cit{12}: the
electrode now carries a single probe type, MB-peptide only. This section
replaces the earlier ratiometric-correction description with an honest
account of what that removes, what it does not, and what is now the central
open risk.

This is not simply a loss. Several of the problems flagged in earlier
versions of this report were specifically about Fc not behaving as
assumed -- its formal potential did not match the literature anchor, its
borrowed rate constant predicted a vanishing signal that contradicted
direct observation, and no distinct dual peak was ever seen on a real
scan. Removing Fc resolves all of these problems simultaneously, because
the component they concerned is no longer part of the device.

\subsection{Reassessment: The Actual Contribution of Fc}

The measurement protocol (Overview) already takes an SWV scan on each
electrode \emph{before} incubation and again \emph{after} -- a baseline
and a follow-up reading on the same physical electrode, not a comparison
between different electrodes. Without Fc, the cleavage fraction is read
directly from that pair:
\begin{equation}
  \Fk^{\rm rec} = 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)}
  \label{eq:Fk_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\DI_{\rm MB}$ is the net SWV peak height (Parts~\ref{sec:core}--\ref{sec:wave}).

It is worth being precise about what the old Fc-based ratio (used in
earlier versions of this report) actually contributed, because the honest
answer is narrower than earlier drafts implied. Algebraically,
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Fk^{\rm rec}\right)_{\rm old\ (with\ Fc)} = 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)/\DI_{\rm Fc}(0)}
  \;=\; 1 - \frac{\DI_{\rm MB}(T)}{\DI_{\rm MB}(0)} \quad\text{\emph{whenever} } \DI_{\rm Fc}(T) = \DI_{\rm Fc}(0),
  \label{eq:Fk_ratio_old}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the Fc terms cancel exactly and Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old} collapses
to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} whenever Fc coverage is stable during incubation.
\textbf{This means Fc was never correcting for baseline electrode-to-electrode
coverage differences} -- Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} already self-normalises
against that, since each electrode is compared only to its own earlier
reading, not to any other electrode or to a reference curve. An earlier
version of this document stated that removing Fc would make the
$\sim$100$\times$ electrode-to-electrode signal variation (Part~\ref{sec:sam})
the central unavoidable problem for calibration; on closer inspection that
overstated the loss, and is corrected here.

\subsection{Consequences of Removal}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{No way to detect non-cleavage signal drift during
  incubation.} Fc's real value was catching cases where $\DI_{\rm
  Fc}(T) \neq \DI_{\rm Fc}(0)$ -- i.e.\ where something \emph{other} than
  MMP-9 cleavage (non-specific fouling, partial probe desorption,
  electrode degradation) changed the signal during the incubation window.
  Without Fc, any such drift is indistinguishable from real cleavage and
  is read as extra $\Fk$. The direction of this risk is not neutral: it
  biases $\Fk^{\rm rec}$ \emph{upward}, i.e.\ towards over-reading
  cleavage -- a chronic wound could read as more severe than it is, but a
  healing wound is unlikely to read as falsely reassuring \FLAG. This has
  not been quantified and is the single most important open question this
  change creates.
\item \textbf{No real-time electrode-health check.} A dead, poorly
  contacted, or badly fabricated electrode would previously show up as a
  missing or anomalous Fc signal, independent of the MB read. That
  diagnostic is gone; a failed electrode and a fully-cleaved electrode may
  now be harder to tell apart from the MB scan alone \FLAG.
\item \textbf{The 3.4\% noise floor used in the LOD calculation
  (Part~\ref{sec:conc}) no longer applies as derived.} That number came
  from error propagation through the ratio-of-ratios in
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_ratio_old}. The noise floor for the simpler
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple} has not been separately derived, and there is
  no principled reason to assume it is the same number -- removing a
  measured quantity from the calculation could plausibly reduce propagated
  noise (fewer error sources) or increase it (if Fc had been absorbing a
  systematic error term, such as amplifier gain drift, that Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple}
  no longer cancels) \FLAG. Until re-derived, every LOD and clinical
  prediction number in Part~\ref{sec:conc} that depends on that 3.4\%
  figure should be treated as provisional.
\item \textbf{Low-coverage electrodes still have worse precision, even
  though $\Fk^{\rm rec}$ itself is unbiased by coverage.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fk_simple}
  correctly cancels the \emph{electrode-to-electrode baseline coverage
  difference} algebraically, but an electrode with low absolute coverage
  still has a smaller absolute $\DI_{\rm MB}(0)$, and therefore a worse
  signal-to-noise ratio against any fixed absolute noise floor (electronic
  noise, baseline drift) when trying to resolve a small $\Fk$. This is a
  real, distinct effect from the (now-resolved) baseline-cancellation
  question above, and the $\sim$100$\times$ coverage variation
  (Part~\ref{sec:sam}) is still relevant to it \FLAG.
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\section{Part F: From MMP-9 Concentration to Predicted Signal}
\label{sec:conc}

\subsection{Overview}

At the enzyme concentrations expected in a wound sample (roughly
6--30~nM), MMP-9 is present in large excess over the surface probe, so the
cleavage fraction follows simple first-order kinetics.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assump}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{MMP-9 is in large excess over surface probe, so cleavage is
  pseudo-first-order.} Consistent with clinical concentration ranges
  (6--30~nM) against a surface probe density orders of magnitude lower.
\item \textbf{Surface efficiency $\eta = 0.20$.} A central estimate, not a
  measured value for our device \FLAG. It corrects for cleavage on a
  surface typically being slower than in free solution (steric hindrance,
  orientation effects), and still needs to be measured directly (e.g.\ by
  comparing electrode readout to a parallel solution-phase fluorescence
  assay).
\end{itemize}
\end{assump}

\subsection{Equation and parameters}

\begin{equation}
  \Fk(T) = 1 - e^{-k_{\rm eff}\,[\text{MMP-9}]\,T}
  \label{eq:Fk_kin}
\end{equation}
where $k_{\rm eff} = \eta \times (k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol}$, and
$(k_{\rm cat}/K_m)_{\rm sol} = 67{,}500$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ is the measured
solution-phase cleavage rate for this exact peptide \cit{7} \CONF.

\subsection{Results}

At the $3\sigma$ detection criterion, this gives a predicted limit of
detection around \textbf{4~nM} at 30 minutes, below the 6~nM healing/chronic
boundary -- \emph{provided} a noise floor of $\sim$3\% is achievable.
\textbf{That number is no longer confirmed} \FLAG: it was derived for the
Fc-based ratiometric measurement, which has since been removed
(Part~\ref{sec:ratio}). The noise floor for the simpler before/after MB-only
comparison now in use has not been separately derived, so this 4~nM figure
should be treated as provisional pending that re-derivation, not as a
settled prediction.

\textbf{Bottom line.} The core electrochemistry (Parts~\ref{sec:core} and
\ref{sec:wave}) rests on standard Butler-Volmer theory and an exact,
independently-verifiable closed-form solution -- the \emph{equations}
are not in question. Whether this specific numerical implementation
reproduces a real published curve is a separate question, and is
currently open (Part~\ref{sec:core}, "Validation against published
data") rather than confirmed. What needs direct experimental attention
before the device can be trusted end to end is everything to do with the
real surface: what is actually on
it (Part~\ref{sec:sam}), and, now that Fc has been removed, whether
non-cleavage signal drift during incubation can be ruled out or bounded
(Part~\ref{sec:ratio}) -- this is the principal failure mode the device
can no longer detect on its own.
