\section{What This Sensor Does}

The sensor detects MMP-9 in fluid from a chronic wound. MMP-9 levels above 17~ng/mL indicate
a wound that is failing to heal. The device works by coating a gold electrode with short
peptide chains that MMP-9 can cut. The more MMP-9 is present, the more chains get cut, and
the lower the electrical signal. A second electrode coated with an MMP-9-resistant chain acts
as a reference: the ratio of the two signals cancels out background noise from temperature,
salt, and fouling. The final output is $F_k$ (cleavage fraction) -- the proportion of probe
chains cut during a fixed incubation window.

This report models every physical step from the wound to the electrical reading. Each section
begins with a plain description of what that step calculates and why it matters, followed by
the underlying equations. \colorbox{boxamber}{\textbf{\textcolor{ambertext}{Amber boxes}}}
mark values that are currently estimated rather than directly measured; the full list of
measurements needed, with suggested sources, is in Table~1 (Section~\ref{sec:litgaps}).
\colorbox{boxgreen}{\textbf{\textcolor{confgreen}{Green boxes}}} summarise confirmed findings.

\section{Layer 0: How Much MMP-9 is in the Wound?}


This step models the chemistry happening in the wound fluid itself. Two types of enzymes
are present: MMP-9 (the target we detect) and HNE (used for the second sensor channel).
Both are partially or fully blocked by natural inhibitors. This layer predicts how much of
each enzyme is in its free, active form at the time of swabbing.


The clinical range of 17--200~ng/mL refers to \textit{total} MMP-9 in wound fluid. But the
sensor only responds to the \textit{free} fraction that can reach and cleave the probe. If
TIMP-1 sequesters most of the MMP-9, the sensor signal will be much lower than the total
concentration implies.

\begin{eqbox}
\textbf{MMP-9 activation and inhibition} (9-state ODE):
\begin{align*}
\frac{d[\text{proMMP-9}]}{dt} &= -\left(\frac{k_\text{cat}}{K_m}\right)_\text{MMP-3}[\text{MMP-3}]
    [\text{proMMP-9}] - \left(\frac{k_\text{cat}}{K_m}\right)_\text{plasmin}[\text{plasmin}][\text{proMMP-9}]\\
\frac{d[\text{MMP-9}]_\text{free}}{dt} &= \text{activation} - k_{\text{on,T1}}[\text{MMP-9}][\text{TIMP-1}]
    + k_{\text{off,T1}}[\text{complex}]\\
\frac{d[\text{HNE}]_\text{free}}{dt} &= -k_{\text{on,A1AT}}[\text{HNE}][\text{A1AT}]
    - k_{\text{on,SLPI}}[\text{HNE}][\text{SLPI}]
\end{align*}
\confirmed{Confident parameters}: $k_{\text{on,A1AT}} = 2\times10^7$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ (Beatty 1980;
Bieth 1984); $K_i(\text{TIMP-1/MMP-9}) = 0.1$~nM (Nagase \& Woessner 1999).
\end{eqbox}

\begin{needsbox}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
\item $(k_\text{cat}/K_m)$ for MMP-3 activating pro-MMP-9 (currently 5,000~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, estimated) --
  search MEROPS database and Suzuki 1990 \textit{J Biochem}.
\item $(k_\text{cat}/K_m)$ for plasmin activating pro-MMP-9 (currently 10,000~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, estimated) --
  search fibrinolysis/MMP activation literature.
\item $[\text{TIMP-1}]_\text{wound} = 200$~ng/mL from Trengove 1999 -- confirm whether this is
  free TIMP-1 or total (including TIMP-1 already bound to MMP-9). Free fraction matters here.
\item $[\text{A1AT}]_\text{wound} = 100$~$\mu$g/mL -- this is estimated from serum dilution.
  Need a wound-fluid specific measurement. Proteomics or ELISA on chronic wound exudate.
\item $k_{\text{on,SLPI}}$ for SLPI binding HNE (currently 10$^5$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, estimated) --
  SLPI is a slow tight-binding inhibitor; check serpinDB.
\item $k_{\text{on,TIMP-1}}$ for TIMP-1 binding MMP-9 (currently 2$\times$10$^5$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$,
  Langton 1998) -- confirm this is TIMP-1, not TIMP-2.
\item See Table~1 (Section~\ref{sec:litgaps}) for the full list with priority rankings.
\end{itemize}
\end{needsbox}

\begin{findingbox}
\textbf{Critical finding: the HNE channel will not function in real wound fluid.}

At a wound-fluid A1AT concentration of 100~$\mu$g/mL (1.92~$\mu$M), the pseudo-first-order
inhibition half-life of HNE is:
\[
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k_{\text{on,A1AT}} \cdot [\text{A1AT}]} = \frac{0.693}{2\times10^7 \times 1.92\times10^{-6}} \approx 0.018~\text{s}
\]
HNE is irreversibly neutralised within approximately 0.02 seconds of entering the wound fluid.
The model predicts free HNE at all time points beyond $t = 1$~s is effectively zero. No HNE
reaches the sensor in measurable quantities.

\textbf{Consequence for the sensor design.} The device was designed as a ratiometric MMP-9/HNE
sensor, with HNE providing the second channel. This ratiometric architecture is not viable in
real wound fluid because the HNE signal will be zero regardless of wound state. Three
alternative approaches should be evaluated before fabrication begins:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=3pt]
\item Replace the HNE active channel with a scrambled, protease-resistant peptide that serves
  as a purely electrochemical reference (cancels fouling and drift with no enzymatic activity).
\item Use a second MMP-9 substrate with a different cleavage rate to enable internal
  ratiometric calibration of MMP-9 activity alone.
\item Measure free HNE directly using a competitive immunoassay channel if HNE information is
  clinically required, rather than a cleavage-based electrode.
\end{enumerate}
This conclusion is conditional on $[\text{A1AT}]_\text{wound} = 100~\mu$g/mL being correct.
If the true chronic-wound A1AT concentration is substantially lower ($<$1~$\mu$g/mL, as might
occur in a severely proteolytic environment), the HNE channel could recover. Measuring
$[\text{A1AT}]$ in patient-derived chronic wound exudate is therefore \textbf{CRITICAL priority}
before committing to the HNE architecture.
\end{findingbox}

\section{Layer 1: Getting the Sample into the Device}


The wound is swabbed to collect 30~$\mu$L of fluid, then the swab is placed in 60~$\mu$L of
buffer to release the proteins (elution). This means the sample is diluted 2-fold before
entering the sensor. Each protein elutes with a different efficiency $\eta$.


The dilution factor and elution efficiency together determine the actual concentration
each species reaches inside the sensor. The more inhibitor (TIMP-1) co-elutes relative
to MMP-9, the more the signal is suppressed inside the device.

\begin{equation}
[E]_\text{chamber} = [E]_\text{wound} \times \eta_E / D_f, \qquad D_f = 2
\end{equation}

\begin{needsbox}
All species-specific elution efficiencies $\eta$ listed below are estimated or borrowed from
protein recovery studies in different matrices. Each needs a spike-and-recovery experiment
on the actual swab/buffer combination:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
\item $\eta_\text{MMP-9} = 0.70$ (estimated); $\eta_\text{TIMP-1} = 0.75$; $\eta_\text{A1AT} = 0.80$;
  $\eta_\text{SLPI} = 0.85$ (Ashcroft 2000); $\eta_\text{HNE} = 0.70$ (estimated).
\end{itemize}
\end{needsbox}

\section{Layer 2: Filling the Sensor Channel}


When the eluted sample is placed at the sensor inlet, it wicks into the channel by capillary
action (the same force that makes paper absorb water). This step checks that the channel fills
completely before measurement begins, and that the time to fill is much shorter than the
incubation time.


If the channel does not fill completely, some electrodes will not be exposed to the sample.
If filling takes longer than a minute, it eats into the usable incubation window.

\begin{equation}
x(t) = \sqrt{\frac{r_h\,\gamma\cos\theta}{2\eta_\text{fluid}} \cdot t},
\quad r_h = \frac{2wh}{w+h}
\end{equation}

The Damkohler number Da $\approx 556 \gg 1$ confirms that enzyme transport to the electrode is
not the slow step -- kinetics (how fast MMP-9 cuts) dominate.

\begin{needsbox}
Contact angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ on PDMS/gold surface -- measure on the actual chip.
\end{needsbox}

\section{Layers 3a--3c: How the Sample Changes Inside the Device}

\subsection{3a: Enzyme diffusion to the electrode}


Once inside the channel, MMP-9 diffuses toward the electrode surface where the probes are. This
step checks that MMP-9 reaches the surface quickly relative to the incubation time, and that
MMP-9 depletion at the surface is negligible (the concentration stays approximately uniform).

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial [E]}{\partial t} = D_E \frac{\partial^2 [E]}{\partial z^2}, \quad
D_\text{MMP-9} = 4.7\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}
\end{equation}

MMP-9 diffusivity is calculated from the Stokes-Einstein equation using a radius of 3.5~nm.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_B3_mass_transport.pdf}
\caption{Layer~3a: MMP-9 diffusion in the sensor channel. The enzyme concentration remains
nearly uniform throughout (surface depletion $<$1\%) confirming diffusion is not the
rate-limiting step.}
\end{figure}

\subsection{3b: Inhibitor binding inside the device}


After elution, TIMP-1 and MMP-9 are both in solution inside the device. TIMP-1 continues to
bind MMP-9 during the incubation period, progressively reducing the free MMP-9 available to
cleave probes. This is the \textit{single largest source of signal suppression} in the model.

At 17~ng/mL MMP-9 (0.19~nM) and 200~ng/mL TIMP-1 (7~nM in wound fluid, $\sim$2.6~nM in
chamber after dilution), TIMP-1 is in $\sim$13-fold molar excess. Over 70\% of MMP-9 is
captured within 30~min.

\begin{needsbox}
$k_\text{on,TIMP-1} = 2\times10^5$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ -- confirm this applies to TIMP-1 (not TIMP-2).
Confirm whether Trengove 1999 reports free or total TIMP-1 in wound fluid. If total, the model
overestimates in-device inhibition.
\end{needsbox}

\subsection{3c: Surface fouling}


Wound fluid contains many non-specific proteins that can stick to the electrode surface,
forming a physical barrier. This layer models how quickly that barrier builds up and how much
it slows enzyme access to the probes.

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\Theta_f}{dt} = k_f\,[P]_\text{bulk}(1 - \Theta_f) - k_d\,\Theta_f, \qquad
\alpha_\text{foul} = \frac{h_\text{channel}}{h_\text{channel} + h_{f,\text{max}}\Theta_f}
\end{equation}

The model predicts fouling has $<$0.01\% effect on $F_k$ because the fouling layer
($h_f \sim 10$~nm) is negligible relative to the 100~$\mu$m channel height. The ratiometric
design also cancels fouling if both active and control electrodes foul equally.

\begin{needsbox}
$k_f = 10^3$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, $k_d = 10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$ -- both estimated. Measure by QCM in
wound-fluid matrix. The protein diffusivity through a dense fouling layer is also assumed

-- 250~mV margin. Oxidative loss at 10~$\mu$M H$_2$O$_2$ over 60~min: 3.5\% -- acceptable.

\section{Layer 8: Can MMP-9 Actually Reach the Probe?}


MMP-9 is a large enzyme (4~nm radius). If the probes are packed too densely, MMP-9 cannot
physically squeeze between them to cleave the probe it is trying to reach. This layer
calculates the fraction of probes that are accessible.


At the target density of 3~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$, only 9\% of probes are accessible to MMP-9.
This dramatically reduces the signal. If density is lowered to 1~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$, accessibility
rises to $\sim$70\%.

\begin{equation}
f_\text{steric} = 1 - \frac{\Gamma_p}{\Gamma_\text{max}^{\text{steric}}}, \qquad
\Gamma_\text{max}^{\text{steric}} = \frac{1}{\pi r_E^2 N_A} \approx 3.3~\text{pmol\,cm}^{-2}
\end{equation}

At $\Gamma_p = 3$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$: $f_\text{steric} = 9\%$. At $\Gamma_p = 1.5$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$:
$f_\text{steric} = 54\%$. \textbf{Design recommendation: aim for $\Gamma_p \leq 2$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$.}

\begin{findingbox}
This is one of the most important design parameters. Packing the probes too densely causes
the enzyme to bounce off without cleaving. The model suggests the optimal probe density is
around 1.5--2 pmol/cm$^2$, not 3 pmol/cm$^2$.
\end{findingbox}

\section{Layer 9: Surface Enzyme Kinetics}


This is the central calculation of the entire pipeline. It models how fast MMP-9 cuts through
the probe chains on the electrode surface, and how this depends on the enzyme concentration
and incubation time.


The speed of cutting sets the LOD, the required incubation time, and the shape of the
calibration curve. All downstream calculations depend on this layer.

\subsection{How the cleavage fraction evolves over time}

Two approximate regimes exist depending on how probe density $\Gamma$ compares to the surface
Michaelis constant $K_{m,\text{surf}}$:

\begin{eqbox}
\textbf{Michaelis-Menten surface ODE (dimensionally consistent):}
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\Gamma}{dt} = -\left(\frac{k_\text{cat}}{K_m}\right)_\text{eff}
  [E] \cdot \frac{K_{m,\text{surf}}\,\Gamma}{K_{m,\text{surf}} + \Gamma}
\end{equation}
where $(k_\text{cat}/K_m)_\text{eff} = 56{,}400$~M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ (solution value $\times\,\alpha = 0.3$),
$K_{m,\text{surf}} = K_m^\text{sol} \times \delta \times 10^{-3} / f_\text{steric}
  \approx 3.4$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$.

\textbf{Two regimes:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Zeroth order} ($\Gamma \gg K_{m,\text{surf}}$, early assay): cutting rate is
  \textit{constant} -- $F_k$ rises linearly with time.
\item \textbf{First order} ($\Gamma \ll K_{m,\text{surf}}$, late assay): cutting rate slows
  as probes are depleted -- $F_k = 1 - e^{-k_\text{obs}\,T}$ where $k_\text{obs} = (k_\text{cat}/K_m)_\text{eff}[E]$.
\end{itemize}

At $\Gamma_0 = 3$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$: $\Gamma_0/K_{m,\text{surf}} = 0.88$ -- near the crossover.
The device operates in the \textit{intermediate-to-first-order regime} throughout the assay.
\end{eqbox}

\begin{findingbox}
\textbf{Inconsistency between Layer~8 and Layer~9 steric factors -- must resolve before finalising kinetics.}

The symbol $f_\text{steric}$ is used for two distinct quantities that are currently assigned
different values without justification:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Layer~8 (geometric accessibility):} $f_\text{steric} = 1 - \Gamma_p/\Gamma_\text{max}^\text{steric} = 9\%$
  at $\Gamma_p = 3$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$. This is the fraction of probes physically reachable by a
  4~nm-radius MMP-9 molecule.
\item \textbf{Layer~9 (Km conversion):} $f_\text{steric} = 0.60$ is used in
  $K_{m,\text{surf}} = K_m^\text{sol} \times \delta / f_\text{steric}$ to convert the solution Michaelis
  constant to a surface density. This is a different physical quantity (how the effective binding
  affinity changes at the interface) and has no derivation in the current model.
\end{itemize}

If the Layer~8 value (9\%) were substituted into the Layer~9 $K_{m,\text{surf}}$ formula, the result
changes significantly: $K_{m,\text{surf}} \approx 22.6$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$, giving
$\Gamma_0/K_{m,\text{surf}} = 0.13$ -- firmly first-order throughout the assay, which simplifies the
calibration considerably. However, the appropriate $f_\text{steric}$ for the $K_{m,\text{surf}}$
conversion is a matter of debate in the literature (Ting et al.\ 2016; Plaxco group 2020) and
should not simply inherit the geometric value without justification.

\textbf{Action required:} define $f_\text{steric}$ consistently in both layers, or replace with
distinct symbols (e.g.\ $f_\text{geo}$ for geometric accessibility, $f_\text{Km}$ for the
Michaelis correction) and derive each from first principles or experiment.
\end{findingbox}

\begin{needsbox}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Surface correction factor $\alpha = 0.3$} is the most critical unknown in the
  entire model. All LOD and $F_k$ values scale proportionally with $\alpha$. Measure
  empirically by comparing solution kcat/Km with on-electrode cleavage rate at known $\Gamma_0$.
  Literature range: $\alpha = 0.03$--$0.5$ (Plaxco group 2015, ACS Nano).
\item \textbf{Initial probe density $\Gamma_0 = 3$~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$} -- this is a design target,
  not a measured value. Confirm by integrating the methylene blue oxidation peak in CV.
\end{itemize}
\end{needsbox}

\section{Layer 10: Electron Transfer}


The methylene blue (MB) reporter at the end of each probe transfers electrons to the electrode.
Intact probes hold MB further away; cleaved probes release it. Electron transfer rate depends
exponentially on distance -- a small change in distance produces a large change in current.

\begin{equation}
k_\text{ET}(d) = k_\text{ET}^0\,e^{-\beta(d - d_0)}, \qquad
\confirmed{\beta = 1.0~\text{\AA}^{-1}\ \text{(Smalley 2003)};
\quad E^\circ_\text{MB} = -0.250\ \text{V vs Ag/AgCl (Bernabi 2019)}}
\end{equation}

\begin{needsbox}
$k_\text{ET}^0 = 10^4$~s$^{-1}$ -- this is an estimate. Measure directly on the actual probe
architecture by AC voltammetry (frequency sweep of the SWV peak height vs scan rate).
\end{needsbox}

\section{Layer 11: SWV Measurement Model}


Square-wave voltammetry (SWV) applies a rapid sequence of voltage pulses and measures the
difference in current between forward and backward pulses. The result is a sharp peak at
the MB formal potential ($-0.250$~V), whose height is proportional to the number of intact
probes.

\begin{equation}
i_\text{peak}(T) = i_0 \times (1 - F_k(T))
\end{equation}

At $T = 60$~min: $i_\text{peak}$ drops from 100\% to 78--98\% across the clinical range
(17--200~ng/mL), giving $\Delta i_\text{peak} \approx 20\%$ discriminability between bounds.

\begin{needsbox}
SWV parameters ($f = 15$~Hz, $\Delta E_\text{SW} = 25$~mV) -- optimise by running a parameter
sweep on the actual device. Also note the noise bandwidth assumption (B~=~1~Hz) needs
verification against the actual signal-processing chain (see audit finding~16).
\end{needsbox}

\section{Layers 12--13: Peak Extraction and Ratiometric Readout}


The SWV output is a current vs voltage curve. We fit a Gaussian peak to find its height
(or area), then take the ratio of the active to control electrode currents. The ratio cancels
out all non-specific effects (temperature, ionic strength, fouling, Ca$^{2+}$) that affect both
electrodes equally.

\begin{equation}
R(T) = i_\text{active}(T) / i_\text{control}(T), \qquad F_k = 1 - R(T)/R(0)
\end{equation}

Error propagation through the ratio gives:
$\sigma_{F_k} \approx 2\,\sigma_i / i_0$
(four independent current measurements -- not $\sqrt{2}$ as computed in the noise budget;
see audit finding~17).

\section{Layer 14: Electronics and Noise}


Every electrical measurement has noise. This layer breaks down the noise by source
(thermal noise in the resistor, ADC quantisation, flicker noise) and computes the
minimum detectable $F_k$ change.

\textbf{Key finding.} Electronics are not the bottleneck. The noise floor from the
electronics gives an instrument LOD of 0.075~ng/mL at $T = 30$~min. The actual
sensor LOD is 8.8~ng/mL -- 120$\times$ worse -- because the surface kinetics are slow
at low concentrations, not because the electronics lack sensitivity.

\begin{align}
\sigma_\text{Johnson} &= \sqrt{4k_BT/R_f \cdot B} \approx 0.004~\text{pA}
  \quad (R_f = 1~\text{G}\Omega,\; B = 1~\text{Hz})\\
\sigma_\text{ADC} &= \text{LSB}/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.088~\text{pA}
  \quad (16\text{-bit},\; \pm10~\text{nA range})\\
\sigma_{F_k}^\text{elec} &= 2\,\sigma_\text{total}/i_0 \approx 0.003\%
  \quad (\text{four-measurement propagation; electronics floor only})
\end{align}

\textbf{Note on the two noise figures used in this report.} The electronics-only floor
$\sigma_{F_k}^\text{elec} = 0.003\%$ is the minimum achievable with ideal surface chemistry and
a perfect electrode. The calibration in Layer~15 uses $\sigma_{F_k}^\text{cal} = 0.5\%$, which
is 167$\times$ larger. These are \textit{not} the same quantity. The 0.5\% figure is a
conservative working estimate of total measurement noise drawn from replicate electrochemical
aptasensor studies in the literature (Plaxco group; Kelley group), where run-to-run variability
in probe density, SAM uniformity, and pipetting dominates over electronics. Reporting LOD using
0.5\% is standard practice for this class of sensor because the electronics floor is never the
limiting factor in practice.

Both figures are estimates. The experiment that resolves this is straightforward: run 10 replicate
blank measurements (buffer, no MMP-9) on identically prepared electrodes and compute
$\sigma_{F_k}$ directly. Until this is done, LOD and LOQ values should be treated as
indicative rather than definitive.

\begin{needsbox}
$R_f = 1$~G$\Omega$, bandwidth $B = 1$~Hz, ADC range $\pm10$~nA -- confirm all three with
the electronics team. The B~=~1~Hz assumption requires explicit low-pass filtering at the
15~Hz SWV sample rate (otherwise B could be up to 7.5~Hz and noise worsens by 2.7$\times$).
\end{needsbox}

\section{Sensitivity and Limit of Detection}

\subsection{What limits the sensitivity?}

Noise sources from most to least limiting:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Surface kinetics (most limiting):} Slow cleavage rate at low $[E]$ sets
  the kinetic LOD = 8.8~ng/mL at $T = 30$~min.
\item \textbf{Inhibitor capture:} TIMP-1 reduces effective $[E]$ by $>$70\%; this
  effectively multiplies the apparent LOD by 3--4$\times$ relative to buffer calibration.
\item \textbf{ADC quantisation:} $\sigma_q = 0.088$~pA (dominant electronic noise,
  but negligible vs kinetics).
\item \textbf{Johnson thermal noise:} $\sigma_J = 0.004$~pA (irrelevant).
\item \textbf{Fouling:} $<$0.01\% signal suppression (negligible).
\end{enumerate}

SNR $= F_k / \sigma_{F_k}$ where $\sigma_{F_k} = 0.003\%$ (electronics only). All values far
above the SNR $= 3$ threshold. The device is not electronics-limited in the clinical range.

\section{Conclusions and Design Recommendations}

\subsection{Is the sensor viable?}

The MMP-9 detection channel is viable in principle. The computed LOD of 8.8~ng/mL lies below
the 17~ng/mL clinical lower bound, the electronics add negligible noise, and the C15 substrate
shows acceptable selectivity over plasmin and cathepsin~B. However, three issues must be
resolved before the design can be considered ready for fabrication:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The HNE channel will not work in real wound fluid.} At the expected A1AT
  concentration in chronic wound exudate (100~$\mu$g/mL, $\approx$1.92~$\mu$M), HNE is
  completely neutralised within $\sim$0.02~seconds. The device subtitle ``MMP-9/HNE Ratiometric
  Sensor'' is a misnomer for real-wound deployment. The ratiometric principle is still sound,
  but the control electrode must use a protease-resistant scrambled peptide rather than an
  HNE-cleavable substrate. \textbf{This is the single highest-priority design change.}

\item \textbf{No valid linear calibration window exists at $\alpha = 0.3$.} The sensor only
  becomes sensitive to 17~ng/mL at $T > 82$~min, but the linear calibration regime ends at
  $T < 51$~min. These constraints are mutually exclusive. The practical consequence is that
  either (a) a 4PL (sigmoidal) calibration must be adopted, accepting a more complex readout,
  or (b) the surface chemistry must be improved to raise $\alpha$ above $\sim$0.5, which closes
  the gap. Measuring $\alpha$ on the actual C15/MCH/gold electrode system is the single most
  important experiment the team can run.

\item \textbf{TIMP-1 suppression degrades the in-wound LOD by approximately 4-fold.} Buffer
  calibration gives LOD~$= 8.8$~ng/mL. In real wound fluid with TIMP-1 in 13-fold molar
  excess over MMP-9, more than 70\% of MMP-9 is captured before it can reach the probe.
  The effective LOD in wound fluid is estimated at $\sim$35--50~ng/mL, which is above the
  17~ng/mL clinical lower bound. The sensor would miss the lower half of the clinical range
  unless wound-fluid calibration is used, or TIMP-1 is removed from the sample (e.g.\ by
  immunodepletion on the swab).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The single most important experiment}

Measure $\alpha$ -- the on-electrode cleavage rate relative to solution -- on the actual C15
substrate in the actual buffer, at the actual probe density. Every prediction in this report
(LOD, $F_k$, calibration linearity, operating window) scales directly with $\alpha$. If
$\alpha = 0.1$, the sensor fails at any clinically relevant concentration and incubation time.
If $\alpha = 0.5$, all problems described above become tractable.

The protocol is: (1) measure $k_\text{cat}/K_m$ for C15 cleavage by MMP-9 in solution via
fluorescence; (2) prepare an electrode with the target SAM; (3) incubate at known $[E]$ for
a set time; (4) measure $\Gamma_0$ before and after by integrating the MB CV peak; (5)
$\alpha = (\Delta\Gamma/\Gamma_0\,T\,[E]) / (k_\text{cat}/K_m)_\text{solution}$.

\subsection{Recommended design changes before fabrication}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item Replace HNE-cleavable control probe with a scrambled protease-resistant sequence.
\item Lower probe density from 3~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ to 1.5--2~pmol\,cm$^{-2}$ to improve
  steric accessibility ($f_\text{steric}$ rises from 9\% to 50--70\%).
\item Adopt 4PL calibration from the outset; do not assume linear response.
\item Calibrate in wound-fluid matrix (or spiked wound-mimic), not in clean buffer.
\item Confirm $[\text{A1AT}]$ in chronic wound exudate before any further HNE-related design work.
\end{enumerate}
